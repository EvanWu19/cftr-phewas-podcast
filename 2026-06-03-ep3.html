Blurb

This study uses DNA methylation — chemical marks layered on top of identical DNA — to ask why monozygotic (identical) twins with the same CFTR mutations can have very different CF severity. In 22 CF twin pairs (44 individuals) from the European CF Twin and Sibling Study, genome-wide methylation (Illumina 450K array, >400,000 CpG sites) showed that even clinically discordant identical twins have nearly identical methylomes (mean intra-pair difference ≈ −0.1%). A strict severity-tracking filter yielded 802 candidate "inflammation-related differentially methylated positions" (iDMPs), but ~87% reflected blood-cell-composition shifts, leaving 106 plausibly acquired. Tested in 109 lung-cancer cases vs 109 matched controls, most showed no significant case-control difference — but one region in the gene body of PKP3 (Plakophilin-3) was hypermethylated in severe-CF twins and highly variably methylated across the cancer cohort. Luciferase reporter assays in lung epithelial cells (A549, H1299) showed this region acts as a promoter whose activity is silenced by methylation (p = 0.00085), next to a methylation-sensitive CTCF site. For Runyu, the paper is a reminder that CF severity is not written in CFTR sequence alone — epigenetics is an independent, often-ignored layer.

Plain-English glossary

  • CpG site — a spot where a C nucleotide sits next to a G; the usual target of DNA methylation.
  • CTCF — a DNA-organizing protein whose binding shapes chromatin loops and is itself methylation-sensitive.
  • DfO (distance from origin) — the study's combined CF severity score (from FEV1% and weight-for-height%).
  • Delta — the within-pair gap in severity between two twins (their discordance).
  • DNA methylation — a methyl chemical tag added to DNA (usually at CpG sites) that can switch genes on/off without changing the sequence; promoter methylation typically silences a gene.
  • Epigenetics — heritable/stable control marks layered "on top of" the DNA sequence.
  • FEV1% — forced expiratory volume in 1 second, a standard lung-function measure.
  • iDMP (inflammation-related differentially methylated position) — a CpG site whose methylation tracks with CF severity/discordance in this study.
  • Illumina 450K array — a chip measuring methylation at >450,000 CpG sites genome-wide.
  • Luciferase reporter assay — fuses a candidate DNA element to a light-producing gene to test whether it drives transcription; brightness = activity.
  • Monozygotic twins — identical twins from one fertilized egg; share essentially the same DNA sequence.
  • PKP3 (Plakophilin-3) — a desmosomal (cell-junction scaffold) protein gene implicated in carcinogenesis; the locus with the methylation-sensitive promoter found here.
  • Promoter (gene body) — a promoter switches a gene on; "gene body" methylation can behave differently from promoter methylation.
  • TCGA — The Cancer Genome Atlas, a public tumour/normal data resource used here to pre-screen marks.

Methods to lift

  • Discordant-monozygotic-twin design — strip out genetic variation to isolate epigenetic/environmental drivers of a phenotype with very few subjects.
  • Illumina 450K + RnBeads — genome-wide methylation measurement and QC.
  • Cell-composition deconvolution (Houseman/Reinius reference) — estimate blood cell-type fractions from methylation and flag marks that merely reflect cell-mixture shifts (the essential negative control here — 87% of hits were cell-type artifacts).
  • TCGA pre-screen — intersect candidate marks with tumour-vs-normal methylation to prioritize cancer-relevant loci.
  • Targeted bisulphite NGS — deep, region-specific methylation validation in a larger case-control cohort (mean ~1,694× coverage).
  • Luciferase promoter/enhancer assay ± in-vitro methylation — turn a methylation association into a functional, mechanistic test in the relevant cell type.

What to verify first

  • Whether the CF cohort has DNA-methylation data at all alongside sequence (many cohorts have only one layer — do not assume).
  • Whether blood methylation is an acceptable surrogate for the lung/outcome of interest (this study is blood-based, not lung).
  • Power: 22 twin pairs (only 6 discordant) plainly underpowered the severity signal; assess whether a few-thousand-patient cohort is adequate for an epigenetic prediction model.
  • Whether candidate-locus methylation is confounded by blood cell composition (the dominant artifact here) — deconvolution is mandatory.
  • That the PKP3 case-control result was "highly variable," not a significant difference — the closed loop is the functional promoter assay, not a population association.

Gap-map (CFTR-WGS paper)

Closes: Demonstrates a clean association-to-mechanism loop for one locus — the PKP3 gene-body region is a genuine, methylation-silenced promoter active in lung epithelial cells (luciferase, two cell lines), adjacent to a methylation-sensitive CTCF site; and establishes that global blood methylation is nearly invariant even across severity-discordant identical CF twins (severity is local/subtle, not a methylome-wide rewrite).

ALEX Welcome back. Today's third paper takes us into cystic fibrosis itself, from a different angle than usual. It's a 2022 paper in the journal Epigenetics by Esther Schamschula, Manuela Hofmann, Silke Kahlert, and colleagues at the Medical University of Vienna and Hannover, titled "Disease-related blood-based differential methylation in cystic fibrosis and its representation in lung cancer revealed a regulatory locus in PKP3 in lung epithelial cells." We're reading it because almost every CF paper we cover asks how DNA sequence differences change the disease — this one asks something subtler: how do chemical marks layered on top of identical DNA change it?

JORDAN That last part is the bit I need unpacked. "Chemical marks on top of DNA." What does that mean, and what's "methylation"?

ALEX Let's build it carefully. Your DNA sequence is fixed, but cells can decorate it with small chemical tags that change whether genes are switched on or off without changing a single letter. That layer of control is called epigenetics — "epi" meaning "on top of." The most studied tag is DNA methylation: a methyl group, basically a tiny carbon-and-hydrogen cap, gets attached to the DNA at specific spots. Those spots are almost always a C sitting next to a G in the sequence — written CpG, the "p" just marking the bond between them. As a rough rule, methylation in the control region at the front of a gene tends to silence it. Methylation patterns differ between cell types and can shift with environment and inflammation, which is the hook for this paper.

JORDAN And why study identical twins?

ALEX Because identical twins are the perfect natural experiment for epigenetics. Monozygotic twins — that's the technical term, meaning they came from one fertilized egg — share essentially the same DNA sequence. So if two such twins both have cystic fibrosis but one has much worse lung disease than the other, the difference can't be their CFTR mutations, because those are identical. CFTR, our anchor gene, is the gene broken in CF; here both twins carry the same CFTR defect. So something other than sequence is driving the divergence — environment, or epigenetics, or both. Twins let you spot epigenetic differences with very few people, because you've stripped out the genetic noise. This study used 22 monozygotic twin pairs — 44 individuals — from the European CF Twin and Sibling Study, sorted by how discordant each pair was in disease severity.

JORDAN How do they even measure "severity" and "discordance" here?

ALEX Two clinical numbers. One is FEV1 percent — forced expiratory volume in one second — basically how much air you can forcefully blow out, a standard lung-function score. The other is weight-for-height percent, a nutrition measure. They combine these into a severity score they call distance-from-origin, and the gap between the two twins in a pair is the discordance, which they call Delta. Pairs were grouped as concordant — similar to each other — intermediate, or discordant. The logic: hunt for methylation marks that track with the discordance, because those are candidates for what's actually driving the severity difference.

JORDAN And the blood — why look at blood methylation if cystic fibrosis is a lung disease? Wouldn't you want lung tissue?

ALEX You would, ideally, and that's a real tension in the design, so let me name it plainly. You can't routinely biopsy a living person's lung for a research study — it's invasive and risky. Blood, by contrast, is easy to draw. So the field leans on blood as a surrogate tissue, on the bet that some disease-relevant methylation changes are reflected in blood cells too, especially for a disease like CF where the whole body is bathed in chronic inflammation and immune cells circulate through the lung. It's a defensible bet, but it's a bet — a mark you find in blood might not exist the same way in lung, and a lung mark might never show up in blood. The authors are doing the convenient-but-imperfect thing, and we should keep that caveat lit the whole way through.

JORDAN So what was the open question — what did they want to find?

ALEX They had a two-part ambition. First, within CF: are there methylation marks in the blood that correlate with disease severity, presumably reflecting the massive chronic inflammation that defines CF lungs? Second, a bigger swing: do any of those inflammation-related marks also show up in lung cancer? The reasoning is that chronic inflammation is a known cancer risk factor, and several genes that modify CF lung disease — they list mucins like MUC4 and MUC5AC, and others like EHF and GSTM1 — are also implicated in lung cancer. So CF becomes a model for studying how chronic lung inflammation might leave epigenetic scars relevant to cancer. They're explicit that CF itself isn't known to raise lung cancer risk — they're using it as an inflammation model.

JORDAN Ambitious. How did they actually run it?

ALEX In stages. First, genome-wide methylation in the 44 twins using an Illumina array — a chip that measures methylation at over 400,000 CpG sites at once. They cleaned the data and applied a careful selection: a mark had to be strongly differentially methylated in discordant pairs but not in concordant pairs, and had to move consistently with severity. That filtering left 802 disease-related sites, which they call inflammation-related differentially methylated positions, or iDMPs. Then a crucial reality check — they asked how many of those marks simply reflect changes in blood cell composition rather than true acquired changes, because inflammation shifts which immune cells are in your blood, and different cells have different methylation. A striking 87 percent of the marks were blood-cell-type-specific, leaving only 106 as plausibly "acquired." I'll push on the design here: with only six discordant pairs anchoring the severity signal, and most candidate marks explainable by cell-mixture, the effective evidence base is thin — they're honest about this, but it's the central limitation. Then they tested the most promising marks in a larger sample — 109 lung cancer patients and 109 matched healthy controls — by targeted sequencing, and finally did lab experiments on the single most interesting site.

JORDAN And the answer? Did they find their inflammation-and-cancer link?

ALEX Partly, and the honest version is more interesting than a clean win. The first finding is almost a non-finding that matters: globally, identical twins — even ones clinically discordant for severe CF — had nearly indistinguishable methylomes, with an average intra-pair difference of about negative 0.1 percent. So severe inflammation does not broadly rewrite the blood methylome; whatever's happening is local and subtle. When they tested their candidate marks in the lung cancer cohort, most showed no significant difference between cancer cases and controls after correction. But one region stood out — a stretch in the gene body of a gene called PKP3, short for Plakophilin-3, which makes a structural protein in the scaffolding that holds epithelial cells together and is already known to play a role in cancer. That PKP3 region was hypermethylated in the blood of the more severely affected CF twins, and in the cancer cohort it was wildly variable — strongly methylated in some people, barely in others — independent of any clinical feature like smoking.

JORDAN Before the lab work — I want to make sure I understand the cell-composition problem you flagged, because it sounds like it nearly sank the whole thing.

ALEX It's the most important methodological lesson in the paper, so yes, let's be concrete. Methylation is read here from whole blood, but whole blood is a soup of different cell types — granulocytes, T cells, B cells, monocytes, natural killer cells — and each type has its own methylation fingerprint. Now, CF inflammation shifts the recipe of that soup; severely affected patients tend to have more neutrophils, a kind of granulocyte. So if you compare a severe twin to a mild twin and find a methylation difference, you face a fork: did the actual DNA marks change, or did the mixture of cells just change, so you're really measuring "more neutrophils" dressed up as "different methylation"? They checked, and 87 percent of their candidate marks matched known cell-type differences — meaning the large majority were probably cell-mixture effects, not acquired changes. That's why only 106 of 802 survived. Any methylation study on blood that skips this check is essentially uninterpretable, and that's a rule you'd carry into any future epigenetic work.

JORDAN "Highly variable" sounds like noise, not a result. Why did they chase it?

ALEX Fair challenge, and they answered it with function rather than more correlation. They took that PKP3 region into the lab and put it into a luciferase reporter assay — a standard trick where you fuse a DNA piece to a gene that makes light, so brightness tells you whether that DNA piece can switch on transcription. In two lung epithelial cell lines, the region acted as a promoter — it drove strong gene activity. And when they chemically methylated that same region, the activity collapsed. So they showed, mechanistically, that methylation of this PKP3 site flips a functional switch in lung cells. They also found a nearby binding site for CTCF, a protein that organizes how DNA folds and whose binding is itself methylation-sensitive. That's a real, if narrow, result: a methylation-controlled regulatory element in PKP3 that's active in lung tissue.

This paper is a reminder that two people with identical CFTR mutations still diverge, and epigenetics is part of that gap. In plain terms, layer an epigenetic measurement on top of the genetic one and see if it earns its keep. Formally: in a CF cohort with both sequence and DNA-methylation data, test whether blood-based methylation at candidate inflammation-related loci improves prediction of a severity phenotype over a model using CFTR genotype and modifier variants alone.
