Blurb (~150w)

Barr and colleagues run a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) with a twist most PheWAS lack: time. Using All of Us — a diverse U.S. biobank linking whole-genome data to electronic health records — they take 21,357 people who already had a substance use disorder (SUD) and ask not whether but how soon they go on to acquire other diagnoses. The engine is Cox proportional-hazards survival analysis: the clock starts at each person's first SUD record, and six polygenic scores (alcohol, cannabis, depression, externalizing, PTSD, schizophrenia) are tested against hundreds of later "phecode" diagnoses. They find 86 phenome-wide significant associations, including each psychiatric score predicting time to its own diagnosis and an "externalizing" score tagging a smoke/inject behavioral pathway. The proposed direction for the CFTR project: stop asking whether a CFTR genotype associates with a CF complication and start asking whether it predicts the age of onset — by porting this survival-PheWAS machinery to the registry's dated events.

Plain-English glossary

  • AFR-like / EUR-like: Genetic-ancestry groupings — people whose genome most resembles African or European reference panels. Analyses were run separately in each, then pooled.
  • Cox proportional hazards: A survival-analysis model asking whether a factor raises the rate at which an as-yet-undiagnosed person gets an event next.
  • EHR (electronic health record): The clinical chart — diagnoses with dates attached.
  • False discovery rate (FDR): The expected fraction of false alarms among declared hits; here capped at 5% to handle hundreds of tests.
  • GWAS (genome-wide association study): The large discovery study that scans the whole genome and estimates each variant's risk weight; the source of the PGS weights.
  • Hazard ratio (HR): Output of a Cox model. 1.0 = no effect on timing; 1.20 = ~20% higher instantaneous rate of the event per unit of the predictor.
  • Index date: The clock-start for survival analysis — here, each person's first recorded SUD diagnosis.
  • PGS / PRS (polygenic score): A single number per person summing thousands–millions of small weighted genetic effects into one inherited-liability "dial."
  • Phecode: A validated grouping of raw ICD-9/10 billing codes into clinically meaningful conditions, used as the PheWAS outcomes.
  • PheWAS (phenome-wide association study): Fix one genetic feature, scan it against the whole catalog of diagnoses.
  • Survival analysis: Statistics of time-until-an-event (not just death); the framework that adds a clock to a PheWAS.
  • SUD (substance use disorder): The index condition here.
  • WGS (whole-genome sequencing): Reading essentially all of a person's DNA; how All of Us obtained the genetic data.

Methods to lift

  • Survival-PheWAS framing — a Cox proportional-hazards model (R survival v3.7.0) per outcome, with an index date, instead of a cross-sectional logistic PheWAS; recovers timing/order, not just co-occurrence.
  • Phecodes — validated ICD-9/10-CM groupings (Denny/Wei/Wu) as the outcome vocabulary; threshold outcomes at ≥1% prevalence in the analytic sample.
  • Polygenic scores as the exposure dial — six pre-trained scores applied to WGS-genotyped participants.
  • Ancestry-split-then-meta-analyze — run EUR-like and AFR-like separately, pool by meta-analysis; the honest handling of PGS portability.
  • FDR (Benjamini–Hochberg) at 5% for multiple-testing control across hundreds of tests.

Key numbers

  • Full EHR sample N = 393,596; SUD + WGS analytic subset N = 21,357 (wider SUD definition N = 67,729, ~900 phecodes).
  • 86 phenome-wide significant PGS→diagnosis associations.
  • Concordant signals: SCZ-PGS → schizophrenia HR 1.21 (1.15–1.27); PTSD-PGS → PTSD HR 1.13 (1.08–1.19); AUD-PGS → alcohol disorders HR 1.08 (1.05–1.11).
  • Off-target: SCZ-PGS → psychosis HR 1.21, paranoid disorders HR 1.30; EXT-PGS → emphysema HR 1.16, oxygen dependence HR 1.13, hepatitis C HR 1.15; CUD-PGS → hepatitis C HR 1.10.

Proposed direction (conditional)

Conditional on inspection of CFF WGS variant calls and registry phenotype linkage: within the CF cohort, use Cox proportional-hazards survival models — with date of CF diagnosis (or birth) as the index time — to test whether CFTR genotype categories, or a polygenic modifier score, are associated with the age of onset of specific complications (e.g., CF-related diabetes, CF liver disease), rather than with their lifetime presence alone.

What to verify first

  • Whether the CFF registry records reliable dated onsets (not just ever-had-it flags) for the target complications.
  • Whether ~5,000 patients yield enough events (not just enough people) per complication for a stable Cox model.
  • Whether right-censoring and follow-up are well-defined per patient (entry/exit dates).
  • Whether any polygenic modifier score is ancestry-portable in the CF cohort's composition — the same portability caveat Barr flags.
  • Whether sample overlap with other CF modifier programs would make any "replication" non-independent.

ALEX Right, and this is the key term: a polygenic score, abbreviated PGS, sometimes called a polygenic risk score or PRS. Picture this — instead of one variant, you take results from a giant prior study that measured thousands or millions of tiny genetic differences across the genome, each nudging risk up or down by a hair. You add all those nudges up, weighted, into a single number for each person. That number is their polygenic score for, say, schizophrenia. High score, more inherited liability; low score, less. It's a dial, not a switch.

JORDAN Where do the weights come from?

ALEX From a GWAS — a genome-wide association study, the discovery study that scanned the whole genome in a huge sample and estimated how much each variant shifts risk. The PGS is just that GWAS, repackaged so you can apply it to a new person. Two more terms and we're set. EHR is the electronic health record — your actual clinical chart, diagnoses with dates attached. And WGS, whole-genome sequencing, is reading essentially all of a person's DNA letter by letter, which is how All of Us got the genetic data here. The workhorse model is called Cox proportional hazards. In plain terms, a Cox model asks: at any given moment, for people who haven't yet gotten the diagnosis, does carrying a higher polygenic score raise the rate at which they get it next?

They start with the full All of Us sample that has health records — 393,596 people — and use that to look at social risk factors for ever having a substance use disorder at all. ALEX Because polygenic scores are notoriously brittle across ancestry — a score trained mostly in European-ancestry samples loses accuracy in everyone else, since the underlying variant patterns differ.
