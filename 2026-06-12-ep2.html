Blurb

Pimplaskar et al. (Am J Hum Genet, 2026, open-access) measure inclusion (participation/recruitment) bias directly in the UCLA ATLAS biobank by comparing 104,516 enrollees against the full 1,568,927-person UCLA Health background population over 2016–2023. A machine-learning classifier predicts enrollment from EHR features alone with AUROC 0.85 — enrollment is emphatically non-random. The dominant driver is receiving primary care at UCLA (OR 8.44, p<1e−300); enrollees also have more visits/year (12.8 vs 6.7) and skew White/non-Hispanic, privately-insured, non-smoking. Crucially, the bias distorts genetics: applying inverse probability weighting (IPW) recovers ~54% more known GWAS associations (e.g., PPARG–type 2 diabetes, rescued from unweighted p=0.076 to weighted p=2.57e−5), and shifts polygenic-score PheWAS patterns across 1,117 phecodes and five PGS. Proposed direction: model sequencing-inclusion probability in the CF cohort and test whether IPW changes modifier-variant↔severity associations. Open question: does the CF project have a background "all CF, enrolled or not" comparator to weight toward — and is a small cohort robust to the extra variance IPW adds?

Plain-English glossary

  • AUROC (area under the ROC curve) — 0.5-to-1 accuracy score; 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect. Here 0.85 = EHR features predict enrollment very well.
  • Biobank — research collection linking DNA to medical records.
  • Collider / selection bias — distortion induced by conditioning on who is selected into a sample; can create fake correlations or mask real ones.
  • EHR (electronic health record) — the hospital's digital patient chart.
  • GWAS (genome-wide association study) — scan of millions of common variants for trait associations.
  • Health-system-linked biobank — recruits from one hospital system, ties DNA to that system's EHR.
  • Inclusion / participation / recruitment bias — the sample differs systematically from the population because enrollment is opt-in and non-random.
  • Inverse probability weighting (IPW) — estimate each person's probability of enrolling, then up-weight under-represented and down-weight over-represented individuals so the weighted sample mimics the background population.
  • Odds ratio (OR) — ratio of odds of an outcome between groups; >1 = over-represented/more likely.
  • Phecode — standardized disease category derived from billing/ICD codes.
  • Polygenic score (PGS) — single number summing many small variant effects to estimate inherited liability for a trait.
  • PGS-PheWAS — scanning one polygenic score against many phenotypes (phecodes) at once.

Methods to lift

  • Background-comparator design — compare enrollees against the entire source health-system population, not just within enrollees, to make the bias visible.
  • Enrollment classifier — ML model predicting enroll/not-enroll from structured EHR features (demographics, insurance, smoking, social-vulnerability index, healthcare utilization); AUROC quantifies how non-random enrollment is; Shapley values rank which features drive it.
  • Inverse probability weighting (IPW) — derive weights from estimated enrollment probability, re-run GWAS/PGS analyses weighted vs unweighted, compare replication.
  • Replication benchmark — test recovery of an external set of known variant–trait associations (here from a curated GWAS reference) as the objective metric of whether correction helps.
  • PGS-PheWAS sensitivity — run polygenic-score scans across many phecodes both weighted and unweighted; associations stable across both are more trustworthy.

"EHR" stands for electronic health record — the digital chart a hospital keeps on every patient: diagnoses, visits, labs. When a biobank is "health-system linked," it means it recruits patients from a particular hospital system and ties their DNA to that hospital's EHR.

JORDAN And the word in the title — "inclusion bias"?

ALEX That's the heart of it, so let me unpack it carefully. Most biobanks use opt-in consent — you have to actively agree to join, usually while you're already at the hospital for something. That means the people in the biobank are not a random slice of the population. They're the people who interact with that health system and choose to enroll. "Inclusion bias" — the authors also call it participation and recruitment bias — is the distortion that creates. The sample is systematically different from the broader population in ways that might be tangled up with both genetics and health.

JORDAN Why would that distortion touch the genetics, though? Enrolling in a biobank doesn't change your DNA.

ALEX Perfect question, and it's the subtle thing the paper exists to demonstrate. Enrolling doesn't change your DNA — but it changes who you're comparing. If the people who enroll are, say, sicker, or older, or more likely to be of one ancestry, or use the hospital more, then any genetic association you measure is measured inside that skewed group. The variant-disease relationship you estimate can be pulled off its true value because the sample itself is a filtered, non-random population. Statisticians call this collider bias or selection bias — the act of selecting on who participates can induce fake correlations or hide real ones. A couple more terms and then we go in: "GWAS," genome-wide association study, is the workhorse scan that tests millions of common DNA variants across the genome to see which ones associate with a trait. And "polygenic score," or PGS, is a single number that sums up many small genetic effects to estimate someone's inherited liability for a trait.

ALEX The background is that biobanks have become the engine of modern human genetics — they're how we discover variant-trait associations at scale. Everyone has known, hand-wavily, that biobank samples aren't random. But the effect of that non-randomness on the actual genetic results — does it matter, how much, and can you fix it — was understudied. People mostly assumed that with a big enough sample, who enrolled would wash out. This paper sets out to measure the bias directly in one real biobank, show whether it bends the genetic findings, and test a correction.

They trained a classifier — a machine-learning model — to predict, from those EHR features alone, whether a given person enrolled in the biobank or not. If enrollment were random, that model would fail. Then, for the genetic part, they used a technique called inverse probability weighting, which I'll define when we hit results, to try to undo the bias and see if the genetic associations changed.

JORDAN So the biobank over-samples the engaged, well-insured, frequently-seen patient. What does that do to the actual genetics?

ALEX This is the payoff. They used inverse probability weighting — IPW. The idea: estimate each person's probability of enrolling from those EHR features, then weight under-represented people more heavily and over-represented people less, so the weighted sample behaves more like the background population. Then they re-ran the genetic analyses both ways, unweighted and weighted, and compared. The result: weighting let them replicate substantially more known GWAS associations — on the order of 54 percent more established variant-trait links recovered than without the correction.
