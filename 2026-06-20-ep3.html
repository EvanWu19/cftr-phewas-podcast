Plain-English glossary

  • CFTR modulator — small-molecule drug targeting the CFTR protein: potentiators (e.g., ivacaftor) hold the channel open; correctors (e.g., elexacaftor/tezacaftor/lumacaftor) aid folding/trafficking. Triple therapy = elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor (Trikafta).
  • CFTR2 — international project curating clinical + functional data on CFTR variants; the panel's evidence base.
  • In cis / in trans — two variants on the same chromosome copy (cis) vs opposite copies (trans); flips interpretation when one partner is a null.
  • Mis-splicing — disruption of exon/intron stitching into mRNA, yielding wrong/broken transcript; if it's the primary defect, little correct protein exists for a modulator to act on.
  • Nonsense / frameshift variant — truncating change expected to yield little/no protein from that copy.
  • Phase — knowing which variants are in cis vs trans; recoverable only via trio data, long-read sequencing, or family segregation.
  • Potentiator / corrector — the two modulator mechanisms (open the channel / fix folding).
  • Variant of uncertain significance (VUS) — pathogenicity unestablished.

JORDAN Let's ground the biology first. What's a CFTR modulator, and what does it mean that the FDA "approved variants" for it?

ALEX Good — this is the CF-clinical layer worth being careful about. CFTR is the gene; its protein is a chloride channel at the cell surface, and in cystic fibrosis it's broken. A CFTR modulator is a small-molecule drug that targets the protein itself rather than the symptoms. There are two broad mechanisms: "potentiators" like ivacaftor hold the channel open once it's at the surface, and "correctors" like lumacaftor, tezacaftor, and elexacaftor help the misfolded protein fold and traffic to the surface in the first place. The triple combination elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor — branded Trikafta — transformed CF care for a large fraction of patients. Now, the regulatory part: the FDA doesn't approve these drugs for "everyone with CF." It approves them for people carrying specific CFTR variants. And in December 2020 the FDA expanded that approved list dramatically — from 39 variants to 183.

JORDAN How did they expand it that much? Did they run trials on 144 new variants?

ALEX No, and that's precisely the tension the paper addresses. The expansion was based largely on in-vitro evidence — lab assays showing a variant's protein responds to the drug in cell models — not on a clinical trial for each variant. That's a reasonable way to extend access, because you'll never run a trial for every rare variant. But it means the approved list now includes variants where the in-vitro signal may not translate to a real clinical benefit in a patient. The CFTR2 expert panel — CFTR2 being the international project, which Karen is central to, that curates clinical and functional data on CFTR variants — reviewed the expanded list and flagged a subset that they caution may not actually respond.

JORDAN So what are the categories they flagged? This is the heart of it.

ALEX From the abstract, the panel identified four categories of approved variants that may not produce a clinical response. First, 15 variants they classify as non-CF-causing — meaning the panel's read is these don't cause cystic fibrosis in the first place, so modulating CFTR is treating a disease the variant doesn't produce. Second, 45 variants of uncertain significance — VUS — where there isn't enough evidence to say the variant is even pathogenic, let alone drug-responsive. Third, six variants known or suspected to cause mis-splicing as their primary molecular defect rather than an amino-acid substitution. And fourth, eight variants known to occur in cis with another deleterious variant — a nonsense or frameshift change — that isn't expected to produce any CFTR protein at all.

JORDAN Walk me through "mis-splicing as the primary defect" and "in cis." Those are the genomics terms I'd trip on.

ALEX Both worth defining carefully. Splicing is how the cell stitches together the coding pieces of a gene — exons — and removes the in-between pieces — introns — to make the final messenger RNA. A mis-splicing variant disrupts that stitching, so you get a wrong or broken transcript. Why it matters for modulators: these drugs act on the CFTR protein. If the variant's main damage is at the RNA-splicing stage — you're making little or no correct protein to begin with — then a corrector or potentiator that works on the folded protein has little to act on. The drug's mechanism and the variant's defect are mismatched. Now "in cis": every person has two copies of CFTR, one per chromosome. Two variants are "in cis" if they sit on the same physical copy, and "in trans" if they're on opposite copies. If an approved variant is sitting in cis with a nonsense or frameshift variant — one that truncates the protein and likely yields no product from that copy — then approving treatment based on the first variant is misleading, because that whole chromosome's CFTR is probably dead regardless of how drug-responsive the first variant looks in isolation.

JORDAN That cis point is subtle. The approval is keyed to one variant, but the biology depends on what else is on the same strand.

ALEX Exactly, and that's the deep lesson for genotype-phenotype work. A variant's annotation in isolation can be actively misleading if you don't know its haplotype context — what else travels with it on the same chromosome. A haplotype is the specific combination of variants inherited together on one physical copy. Phase — knowing which variants are in cis versus in trans — is not a detail; here it flips the clinical interpretation. This is the kind of thing a naive variant-level lookup gets wrong and a haplotype-aware analysis gets right.

JORDAN And the VUS bucket — 45 of the 74 — that's the biggest one. Why would a variant of uncertain significance end up on an approval list at all?

The approval pathway leaned on in-vitro response — a variant's protein reacting to the drug in a cell assay. A VUS is, by definition, a variant where we haven't established that it's pathogenic. So you can have a situation where a lab assay shows drug response, the variant gets approved, and yet the panel can't even confirm the variant causes disease. That mismatch — assay-positive but classification-uncertain — is the conceptual core of why 45 variants land in the caution set, and it's a clean illustration of why functional evidence and clinical classification have to be tracked as separate axes, not collapsed into one yes/no.

ALEX Right, and the mapping is the whole point of the caution list, so it's worth being concrete. CFTR variants are often grouped by what they break. Some variants make a protein that reaches the cell surface but doesn't open properly — gating defects; the classic example is G551D. A potentiator like ivacaftor is built for those: it holds the channel open. Other variants make a protein that misfolds and never traffics to the surface — processing defects; F508del is the canonical one. Correctors like tezacaftor and elexacaftor help that protein fold and get to the surface, and then a potentiator helps it work once there. Now look back at the panel's four caution categories through that lens. A variant whose primary defect is mis-splicing isn't a gating or processing problem at the protein level at all — it's upstream, at the RNA stage, so neither a potentiator nor a corrector has a well-formed protein target to rescue. A variant in cis with a nonsense or frameshift change is making essentially no protein from that copy — again, nothing for the drug to grab. So the categories aren't arbitrary; they're the places where the drug's mechanism and the variant's actual molecular lesion don't line up.
