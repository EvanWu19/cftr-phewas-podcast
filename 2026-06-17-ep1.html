How did this episode land for you?

EVE — Disease variant prediction with deep generative models of evolutionary data (Frazer et al., 2021)

Blurb. EVE (evolutionary model of variant effect) is an unsupervised deep generative model — a variational autoencoder trained on a protein family's multiple sequence alignment — that scores missense variants by how unlike a naturally-evolved sequence the mutant looks (the "evolutionary index"), then converts that continuous score into a calibrated pathogenic/benign probability with an explicit "uncertain" band via a two-component mixture model. Crucially it uses no clinical labels, so it sidesteps the sparsity, bias, and circularity of training on ClinVar. The abstract reports predictions for >36 million variants across 3,219 disease genes, evidence to reclassify >256,000 VUS, and accuracy that beats label-trained predictors and matches deep mutational scanning experiments. Lands on the Karen / variant-interpretation priority: CFTR is exactly the single-protein, long-VUS-list gene EVE is built for. Buildable artifact — a CFTR VUS triage notebook that joins precomputed EVE scores against CFTR2 + ClinVar and ranks the discordances. (Non-OA paper; episode built from the published abstract + public methods/resource, flagged abstract-only on-air.)

Plain-English glossary.

  • Deep mutational scanning (DMS): lab assay that engineers thousands of variants of a protein and measures, en masse, whether each still folds/functions; increasingly used as direct evidence in variant classification.
  • Evolutionary couplings / co-variation: when two alignment positions change together across evolution because they physically contact in 3D; a signal pure per-column conservation misses.
  • Evolutionary index: EVE's variant score — the log-likelihood difference between mutant and wild-type sequence under the learned distribution.
  • Homologs: evolutionarily related versions of a protein across other species, the rows of the MSA.
  • Multiple sequence alignment (MSA): thousands of homologous sequences stacked and aligned column-by-column so each column is "the same position" across evolution.
  • Variant of uncertain significance (VUS): a variant with no confident benign/pathogenic call — a clinical dead end.
  • Zero-shot prediction (here): scoring variants with a model that never saw any disease labels — pathogenicity inferred purely from evolutionary plausibility.

Full citation. Frazer J, Notin P, Dias M, Gomez A, Min JK, Brock K, Gal Y, Marks DS. Disease variant prediction with deep generative models of evolutionary data. Nature. 2021. PMID 34707284 · DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-04043-8. (No PMCID; not open access.)

Methods / tools to lift.

  • Unsupervised, family-specific generative model over an MSA → label-free variant scoring (dodges ClinVar circularity).
  • Evolutionary index (wild-type vs mutant log-likelihood) as a continuous effect score.
  • Two-component mixture model to calibrate a continuous score into pathogenic/benign with an explicit uncertain zone (abstain instead of forcing a call).
  • Precomputed EVE scores are publicly downloadable per protein — usable as a drop-in evidence column.

ALEX Today on Track A we're reading the paper that made "you don't need labels to call a variant pathogenic" a respectable sentence: Frazer, Notin, Dias and colleagues from Debora Marks' lab, "Disease variant prediction with deep generative models of evolutionary data," Nature, 2021. The model is called EVE — evolutionary model of variant effect. One flag up front: this one isn't open access, so I'm working from the published abstract plus what's in the public methods and the EVE resource, and I'll tell you when I'm leaning on the abstract versus the broader record rather than a line I can read off the full text.

JORDAN Good, I'll hold you to that. Let me start with the thing the whole paper hinges on — they say they predict pathogenicity "without relying on labels." What's the label they're proudly not using?

ALEX The label is a clinical verdict — a curated call from a database like ClinVar that says "this variant causes disease" or "this variant is benign." Most variant-effect predictors are supervised: you train a classifier on thousands of those known-pathogenic and known-benign examples, and it learns to mimic them. The problem the abstract names directly is that those labels are sparse, biased, and of variable quality. They cluster on a few well-studied genes, they carry the prejudices of whoever curated them, and a model trained on them can essentially memorize and then look artificially good. EVE's move is to throw the labels away entirely and learn from evolution instead.

JORDAN So define the evolution part for me, because that's the biology I want nailed down. What does "evolutionary data" actually mean as model input?

ALEX It means a multiple sequence alignment — an MSA. You take your protein, say CFTR, and you go find every evolutionarily related version of it across thousands of other species — the homologs. You stack those sequences up and line them up column by column, so that each column is "the same position" across all that evolutionary history. Now you read down a column. If a position is bright-red conserved — the same amino acid in a sea lamprey and a human and everything between — evolution has been telling you for half a billion years that you cannot touch that residue without breaking the protein. If a column is a free-for-all, that position tolerates change. EVE learns the full statistical distribution of those natural sequences — not just per-column conservation, but which positions co-vary, which combinations evolution actually allows.

JORDAN Say more about "co-vary" — why does it matter that two positions change together?

ALEX Because that's the signal that pure conservation misses. Imagine two residues that physically touch when the protein folds, and one is a bulky positive charge balanced against a negative charge across the contact. Evolution can swap that pair — flip both charges — and the protein still works, so neither column looks strictly conserved on its own. But they're locked together: you almost never see one flip without the other. That coupling is a fingerprint of a 3D contact, and a model that captures the joint distribution, not just the per-column frequencies, learns those constraints. It's the same evolutionary-couplings idea that earlier let people predict protein contacts and folds before AlphaFold; EVE repurposes it to score variants.

JORDAN And the model that learns that distribution is a variational autoencoder, which I know, so don't explain it to me. What I want to know is: how do you get from "I've learned what natural sequences look like" to "this specific human mutation is dangerous"?

ALEX This is the elegant part. Once EVE has learned the distribution over the family, you ask it a likelihood question. You score the wild-type sequence and you score the mutant sequence, and you take the difference in log-likelihood — they call it the evolutionary index. If the variant makes the sequence look much less like something evolution would ever produce, that's a big negative number — strongly suggestive of pathogenic. If the mutant looks just as plausible as the wild-type, the position tolerates it — benign. The key conceptual point is that this is zero-shot: the model never saw a single disease label. It's inferring "would nature build this?" and using unnaturalness as a proxy for "breaks function."

JORDAN But "unnatural" is a continuous score and a clinician needs a verdict. How do they cross that gap?

ALEX They fit a two-component mixture model on the evolutionary-index distribution — essentially saying the scores fall into two clouds, a benign cloud and a pathogenic cloud — and they assign each variant a probability of belonging to each, with an explicit uncertain zone in the middle for variants the model won't commit on. That uncertain band matters: rather than forcing a call on every variant, EVE quarantines the ones it can't separate, which is exactly the honest behavior you want from an evidence source.

JORDAN Let's back up to motivation. Why was this such a big deal in 2021? Hadn't we had SIFT and PolyPhen for years?

ALEX We had, and that's the background. The abstract makes the scale of the problem brutal: over 98% of variants in disease-related genes still have unknown consequences. Think about what that means clinically — you sequence a patient, you find a missense change in a relevant gene, and the honest answer is almost always "we don't know if this matters." That's a variant of uncertain significance, a VUS, and a VUS is a clinical dead end — you can't act on it, you can't counsel a family on it. The older tools — SIFT, PolyPhen, and the supervised heavyweights — existed, but the field had grown skeptical of them precisely because of the label problem.

JORDAN What was the actual skepticism — that they were wrong, or that they were cheating?

ALEX Closer to cheating, in a subtle way. If your predictor was trained on ClinVar labels, and then you evaluate it on ClinVar variants, you've got circularity — the model and the test share the same biased source, so the accuracy number is inflated and doesn't tell you how it'll do on a genuinely novel variant. A 2019 ClinGen assessment essentially landed on "most in-silico predictors aren't reliable enough to count as strong evidence" on their own. EVE's pitch is that an unsupervised, evolution-only model dodges the circularity completely, because evolution doesn't know what's in ClinVar — so when it agrees with a clinical call, that agreement is genuinely independent information.

JORDAN What did they actually build it on — how many genes, how many variants?

ALEX From the abstract: they predicted the pathogenicity of more than 36 million variants across 3,219 disease genes, and they provided evidence to reclassify more than 256,000 variants of uncertain significance. So this isn't a toy on one protein — it's a proteome-scale resource. Per gene, the recipe is: build the deep MSA, train a family-specific generative model, compute evolutionary indices for every possible missense change, fit the mixture model, and emit a calibrated EVE score per variant.

ALEX And here's where I'd push on a design choice. Everything rests on the MSA being deep and clean. A family-specific model needs hundreds to thousands of genuinely homologous sequences to learn co-variation; if your protein has shallow evolutionary coverage, or if a particular domain is poorly aligned, the model in that region is running on fumes. For a big multi-domain membrane protein, the cytoplasmic, well-conserved domains may be beautifully covered while a transmembrane stretch is thin. So a single per-protein quality number can hide real per-position variation in how much you should trust the score.

JORDAN Did it deliver? What's the headline result?

ALEX Two claims, both from the abstract. First, EVE outperforms computational approaches that rely on labeled data — so the label-free model beats the supervised ones on the same benchmarks. Second, and this is the one that turns heads: it performs on par with, if not better than, predictions from high-throughput experiments. That means deep mutational scanning — lab assays where you engineer thousands of variants of a protein and physically measure whether each one still folds or functions. Those assays are increasingly used as direct evidence in variant classification. EVE, a model that saw zero clinical labels and zero wet-lab assays, lands in the same accuracy neighborhood as the experiments. That's the result that made people take evolutionary generative models seriously as clinical-grade evidence.
