Blurb

Liran Bar and colleagues (EBioMedicine, 2026) use the gnomAD reference database (>800,000 people, 25 countries) to re-estimate the global burden of cystic fibrosis across six ancestry groups. Counting CF-causing CFTR alleles per group, applying Hardy-Weinberg (incidence ≈ q²), and multiplying by real birth numbers from representative high-population countries, they conclude that while per-capita CF incidence is lower in non-European groups, the absolute number of CF births in Asia, Africa, and South America may equal or exceed Europe and North America — i.e., a majority of CF births globally now occur outside Europe. F508del falls from ~65% of CF alleles in Europeans to as low as 9% in East Asians, so Eurocentric screening panels and modulator-eligibility lists miss many non-European patients. The proposed direction for Runyu: profile the CFF cohort's own ancestry composition and CFTR allelic spectrum against gnomAD's ancestry-stratified catalog before any PheWAS, to define whose biology the cohort can speak for. Open question: whether the cohort even contains enough non-European patients (or usable ancestry fields) to make that stratification feasible.

Plain-English glossary

  • Ancestry group (genetic) — a grouping by inferred genetic background (European, Latino/Admixed, African/African-American, South Asian, East Asian, Middle Eastern); may not match self-reported race/ethnicity.
  • Carrier frequency — fraction of a population carrying one CF-causing variant.
  • ClinVar — a public archive where labs deposit variant-pathogenicity interpretations; gnomAD uses its designations.
  • F508del — the most common CF-causing variant; ~65% of CF alleles in Europeans, far less elsewhere.
  • gnomAD (Genome Aggregation Database) — a large public catalog of human genetic variation pooled across >800,000 people; the standard population-frequency denominator.
  • Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium — population-genetics formula; for a recessive disease, birth incidence ≈ q² where q is the combined disease-allele frequency.
  • HEMT (highly effective modulator therapy) — drugs (e.g. elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor) that repair/boost faulty CFTR protein; approved only for specific variant lists.
  • Incidence — rate of affected births (here, per 100,000).
  • Mutalyzer — a tool that checks a variant's notation is biologically valid.
  • Newborn screening panel — a fixed list of specific variants a screening test checks for; variants off the list can be missed.
  • q (disease-allele frequency) — combined frequency of all CF-causing alleles in a group; q² gives birth incidence.

It pools DNA sequences from over 800,000 people across many countries and ancestries, and for every spot in the genome it tells you how often each variant shows up. Researchers use it as the denominator for "how rare is this variant in the general population." When they say "burden of CF," they mean two linked things: the carrier frequency — what fraction of people carry one CF-causing variant — and the incidence, the rate at which babies are actually born with CF. Remember CF is recessive: you need two broken copies to have the disease, one from each parent.

JORDAN And how do you get from "carrier frequency" to "how many babies are born with it"?

ALEX Through a piece of classic population genetics called Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. It's a simple formula. If the combined frequency of all disease-causing alleles is some value — call it q — then the fraction of people born with two copies is q squared. So if CF-causing alleles together make up about 2% of CFTR copies in a population, the birth incidence is 0.02 times 0.02, which is 0.0004, or about 1 in 2,500. That q-squared step is the engine of the whole paper. They count CF-causing alleles in gnomAD for each ancestry group, square it, and convert to births.

JORDAN That sounds clean, but how do they decide which variants count as "CF-causing"? That tripped up yesterday's paper too.

ALEX Same crux, and they handle it carefully. They take gnomAD's pathogenicity labels, which come from a database called ClinVar — a public archive where labs deposit their interpretations of whether a variant causes disease. Then they cross-check each variant against CFTR2, the expert-curated CF database we met yesterday, and against a tool called Mutalyzer that confirms the variant notation is biologically valid. Variants flagged pathogenic, or pathogenic-slash-likely-pathogenic, go into q. Everything benign or uncertain goes into the non-disease pool. They also benchmark their numbers against an independent dataset from Johansen Taber and colleagues to make sure they're not way off.

They used Brazil for Latino, Nigeria for African, India for South Asian, China for East Asian, and Egypt for Middle Eastern, pulling birth totals from a United Nations database. They also tested how many CF-causing alleles four common commercial screening panels — Hologic, EUv2, Illumina, and Wisconsin's expanded panel — would actually catch in each group, and how many variants are eligible for the modern modulator drugs.

Second, ClinVar labels are uneven in quality, and for non-European variants the curation is thinner, which means you can undercount CF-causing alleles precisely where you most want accuracy. Third, using one country to stand in for an entire continental ancestry group is a real simplification. They acknowledge these, but it means the headline numbers are best read as order-of-magnitude, not precise counts.

JORDAN So what did they find?

In plain terms: CF is not rare in absolute terms outside Europe; it's just under-counted. They also nailed down the variant-mix problem. F508del, which accounts for about 65% of CF-causing alleles in Europeans, drops to roughly 33% in Latino, 29% in African-American, 32% in South Asian, 27% in Middle Eastern, and just 9% in East Asian groups.
