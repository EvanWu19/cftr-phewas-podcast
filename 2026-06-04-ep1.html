Blurb

This UK Biobank methods paper (502,369 participants, ~440,000 analyzed after QC) asks whether rare genetic variants — those seen in under 1% of people — can improve polygenic risk scores (PRS), which normally use only common variants. Kang and colleagues build rare-variant scores (rvPRS) for 13 binary and 5 quantitative traits, two ways: by bundling rare variants per gene (gene-burden) and by testing each variant individually (single-SNP), using both directly-sequenced exome data (WES) and cheaper imputed chip data (IMP). Three findings stand out: single-SNP rvPRS beat gene-burden rvPRS; imputed-data rvPRS generally beat exome-derived ones; and for 6 of 12 validated traits, a combined score (cvPRS + rvPRS = tPRS) significantly out-predicted the common-variant score alone. Rare variants explained ~10–11% of the variance the combined score captured. For Runyu, the proposed direction is to test whether a rare-variant score built from CF-cohort whole-genome data sharpens prediction of a defined CF complication beyond a common-variant score — with the open caveat that this paper's gains rode on 440,000 people, and CF cohorts are ~5,000.

Plain-English glossary

  • cvPRS (common-variant PRS) — a risk score built from common variants only; the standard kind.
  • Gene-burden testing — bundling all rare variants in a gene and testing the total "burden" against a trait, since any one rare variant is too rare to signal alone.
  • GCTA — Genome-wide Complex Trait Analysis; software used here to estimate SNP-based heritability.
  • GRM (genetic relationship matrix) — a table of how genetically related participants are; used to correct for relatives. A "sparse" GRM saves computing power.
  • Heritability (h²) — the share of a trait's variation explained by genetic differences.
  • IDI (integrated discrimination improvement) — measures whether adding a predictor improves how well a model separates cases from non-cases.
  • IMP (imputed genotype data) — cheap chip genotypes plus statistical "filling-in" of unmeasured variants from a reference panel.
  • MAF (minor allele frequency) — how common the rarer version of a variant is; "rare" here means MAF < 1%.
  • Mask — a recipe defining which variants to include in a burden test (by predicted damage and frequency).
  • Mixed model — a statistical model that accounts for relatedness and structure among participants (SAIGE-GENE+ is one).
  • NRI (net reclassification improvement) — measures whether a new predictor correctly shifts people into better risk categories.
  • PRS (polygenic risk score) — one number summing many variants' small effects to estimate genetic risk for a trait.
  • r2redux — an R package that formally tests whether one score's R² beats another's.
  • rvPRS (rare-variant PRS) — a risk score built from rare variants (MAF < 1%).
  • SAIGE-GENE+ — a scalable mixed-model program for genome-wide and gene-based association testing in large biobanks.
  • Single-SNP testing — testing each variant individually (SNP = single-letter DNA difference).
  • tPRS (total PRS) — the combined cvPRS + rvPRS score.
  • WES (whole exome sequencing) — directly sequencing the ~2% of the genome that codes for proteins.

ALEX Welcome back. Today's first paper is a 2025 study in the journal Communications Biology by Jung-Oak Kang and colleagues, titled "Studying rare variant polygenic risk scores using whole exome sequencing and imputed genotype data." It's a methods paper built on the UK Biobank — half a million British volunteers with deep genetic and health data. We're reading it because it tackles a question that sits right at the heart of where your project might go: can the rare DNA changes that ordinary genetic risk scores ignore actually add real predictive power, and if so, how do you build a score that uses them?

JORDAN Before we go anywhere, I need the vocabulary. Half the title is acronyms. What's a "polygenic risk score," and what makes a variant "rare"?

ALEX Let's build it from the ground up. A variant is just a spot where people's DNA differs — one person has an A, another has a G. A common variant is one where the rarer version shows up in a lot of people; a rare variant is one where the rarer version is unusual, here meaning it appears in less than one percent of people. That one-percent line is called the minor allele frequency, or MAF — literally how frequent the less-common version is. Now, a polygenic risk score, abbreviated PRS, is a single number that adds up the small effects of many variants across your genome to estimate your genetic risk for some trait, like diabetes or height. "Poly" means many, "genic" means genes — you're summing many tiny genetic nudges into one score.

JORDAN And the paper splits that into two flavors — common and rare?

ALEX Exactly. A common-variant PRS, which they shorten to cvPRS, is the standard kind — it's built almost entirely from common variants, because those are easy to measure cheaply on a genotyping chip. The novelty here is the rare-variant PRS, rvPRS, built from those sub-one-percent variants. And they also build a combined score, the total PRS or tPRS, which is just cvPRS and rvPRS added together. The whole paper is a bake-off: does adding the rare-variant score on top of the common one actually help?

JORDAN Got it. But there's another pair of acronyms in the title — WES and "imputed genotype." What are those?

ALEX Two different ways of reading someone's DNA. WES is whole exome sequencing — you actually sequence, letter by letter, just the exome, which is the roughly two percent of the genome that codes for proteins. It's where most known disease-causing rare variants live, so it's a targeted, high-quality read of the important part. The other method, imputed genotype data — they call it IMP — starts from a cheap genotyping chip that only directly measures about a million pre-chosen spots, and then statistically fills in, or imputes, the millions of variants in between by comparing your measured spots to a big reference panel of fully sequenced genomes. Imputation is like knowing a few words of a sentence and a giant library of similar sentences, then guessing the missing words. The surprising claim of this paper is that imputed data can actually build better rare-variant scores than direct exome sequencing.

JORDAN That does sound backwards. So what was the actual problem they set out to solve?

ALEX Here's the motivation. For years, polygenic risk scores have been built almost entirely from common variants, because the data was cheap and plentiful. But common variants don't explain all the inherited risk we know exists from twin studies — there's a stubborn gap people call the missing heritability. A natural suspect for that gap is rare variants: individually rare, but collectively numerous, and often more damaging because natural selection hasn't had as much time to weed them out. The trouble is there's been no agreed recipe for folding rare variants into a risk score. Should you test each rare variant one at a time, even though any single one is seen in only a handful of people? Or should you bundle all the rare variants in a gene together and test the bundle? And which data source — the exome you sequenced, or the imputed chip — gives you a better rare-variant score? Nobody had laid out a clean, general protocol. That's the gap Kang and colleagues wanted to fill.

JORDAN When you say "bundle all rare variants in a gene," is that a named technique?

ALEX It is — it's called gene-burden testing, or burden testing. The idea: any one rare variant is too rare to show a statistical signal on its own, so you count how many rare variants a person carries across an entire gene and test whether that total burden tracks with the trait. The alternative they compare it to is single-SNP testing — SNP meaning single nucleotide polymorphism, a one-letter DNA difference — where you test each variant individually after all. So the first big design question is burden versus single-SNP. Hold that thought, because the answer surprised them.

JORDAN Okay, so how did they actually run this?

ALEX They started with about 502,000 UK Biobank participants and, after quality filtering, kept around 440,000 people of European ancestry who had both kinds of data. Then — and this is the disciplined part — they split those people into three non-overlapping groups. A discovery set of about 242,000 people, used to run the association tests that find which variants matter. A training set of about 122,000, used to build and tune the scores. And a validation set of about 74,000, held back and untouched until the very end, to get an honest read on performance. Keeping those groups separate is what stops you from fooling yourself — if you tune and test on the same people, everything looks great and nothing replicates.

JORDAN What tools did they reach for?

ALEX The workhorse for the association tests was a program called SAIGE-GENE+, which is a mixed-model method — meaning it mathematically accounts for the fact that some participants are relatives, which would otherwise create false signals. It uses something called a genetic relationship matrix, or GRM, basically a big table of how related everyone is; they used a "sparse" version to save computing power. For the rare variants from exome data they tried nine different "masks" — recipes for which variants to bundle, based on how damaging each one is predicted to be and how rare it is. They estimated heritability with a tool called GCTA, and compared the predictive power of competing scores with an R package called r2redux, which gives you a proper statistical test for whether one score beats another rather than just eyeballing it. I'll push back on one choice here: they used self-reported white-British ancestry only. That keeps the statistics clean, but it means none of these scores are validated outside that one ancestry group — a real limitation for any score you'd want to use broadly.

JORDAN Fair. So did the rare-variant scores actually deliver?

ALEX Partly, and the pattern is the interesting part. First headline: single-SNP rare-variant scores beat the gene-burden ones. Bundling variants into a gene burden, the supposedly sophisticated approach, lost to just testing variants individually. Second headline: the imputed-data scores generally beat the exome-sequenced scores — the cheap chip plus imputation outperformed direct sequencing for building these scores. Third, the rare-variant score on its own explained only about ten to eleven percent of the variance that the combined score captured — so rare variants are a real but modest slice. And critically, for six of twelve validated traits, the combined tPRS beat the common-variant score alone by a statistically significant margin. For eight of the thirteen binary disease traits, two formal improvement measures — net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement, which ask whether adding the rare score correctly moves people into better risk categories — were significantly positive.

JORDAN Put a number on the heritability piece — that's the part I keep hearing matters.

ALEX For body mass index, their rare-variant heritability from exome data was about 7.9 percent, but from imputed data it was about 14.2 percent — nearly double. Same direction for blood pressure. That's their evidence that imputed data is capturing rare-variant signal that exome sequencing alone misses, probably because imputation reaches variants outside the protein-coding exome. So they did answer their central question: yes, rare variants add predictive value, single-SNP scoring and imputed data are the better recipe, and the gains are real but bounded — roughly a ten percent slice, meaningful for some traits and negligible for others.

JORDAN So where does this point for the project?

ALEX Here's one concrete direction it suggests. Your CFTR cohort has whole-genome sequencing, which sees rare variants directly — including the rare regulatory variants outside the exome that this paper hints are doing real work. One thing you could explore down the line is building a rare-variant score for a CF complication — say, CF-related diabetes — from the modifier variants in the genome, and asking whether it adds predictive power on top of a conventional common-variant score for the same complication. In plain terms: do the rare modifier variants in CF patients sharpen our ability to predict who develops a given complication? Formally: does a single-SNP rare-variant polygenic score derived from CF-cohort whole-genome data improve risk prediction for a defined CF comorbidity beyond a common-variant score alone, measured by reclassification metrics in a held-out subset?

JORDAN And what does this paper close versus leave open for that?

ALEX What it closes: it gives you a vetted protocol — split discovery, training, and validation; prefer single-SNP over burden; consider imputation alongside sequencing; judge improvement with reclassification statistics and a proper R-squared comparison test, not just by eye. That's a ready-made template. What it leaves open is mostly about scale and fit. This study rode on 440,000 people; the things we'd need to check before committing are whether anything like this is powered at roughly 5,000 CF patients, since rare-variant scores are data-hungry and CF cohorts are small. We'd also need to check whether a polygenic-score framing even fits CF, where every person already carries two CFTR mutations — the trait isn't "do you have the disease," it's "which complication and how severe," which changes the modeling. And we'd want to confirm the cohort actually has enough cases of any single complication to train and validate a score at all. None of that is settled here — it's what we'd verify before treating this as a path rather than an idea.
