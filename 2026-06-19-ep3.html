Plain-English glossary

  • AGTR2: type-2 angiotensin II receptor gene (X chromosome); part of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS).
  • FEF25-75: forced expiratory flow over the middle half of the FVC — a small-airways readout.
  • FEV1: forced expiratory volume in 1 second — standard spirometry lung-function measure.
  • FVC: forced vital capacity — total air exhaled in a forced breath.
  • Linkage disequilibrium (LD): nearby variants inherited together as a correlated block; a GWAS hit usually flags a haplotype, not one proven causal variant.
  • Minor allele frequency (MAF): frequency of the less common allele (rs5952223 ≈ 0.26).
  • Modifier gene: a gene whose variation shifts disease severity among people who all carry the primary disease mutation; 50–80% of CF lung-disease variation is non-CFTR genetic.
  • Renin-angiotensin system (RAS): angiotensinogen → (renin) → angiotensin I → (ACE) → angiotensin II, signaling via AGTR1 and AGTR2.
  • Regulatory variant: a non-coding variant that changes how much of a gene is made, rather than altering its protein.

ALEX Track C today is "AGTR2 absence or antagonism prevents cystic fibrosis pulmonary manifestations," Darrah and colleagues, including Garry Cutting, Mike Knowles and Lisa Strug on the author list, Journal of Cystic Fibrosis, 2019. This one's a gift for the modifier catalog: it takes a GWAS hit — the AGTR2 locus on the X chromosome — and chases it all the way from a statistical association in CF patients to a druggable mechanism validated in two independent mouse models. We're reading it because that full arc, from association to direction-of-effect to a drug, is exactly what a modifier gene needs to graduate from "interesting SNP" to "candidate target."

JORDAN Set the biology up for me. We always say CFTR causes CF — so what's a "modifier" even doing here?

ALEX CFTR mutations cause the disease, but they don't fully set how severe your lungs get. The paper opens with the key number: heritability studies say 50 to 80% of the variation in CF lung disease is due to genetic differences *other* than CFTR. That leftover heritability is the modifier hunting ground — other genes whose variation nudges outcomes up or down for people who all already have CF. AGTR2 is one such candidate. It's the type-2 angiotensin II receptor gene, and it sits in the renin-angiotensin system — the RAS pathway most people know from blood-pressure control. Quick chain: angiotensinogen gets cleaved to angiotensin I, ACE cleaves that to active angiotensin II, and angiotensin II signals through two receptors, AGTR1 and AGTR2. Most of what we know about RAS is cardiovascular; its role in the lung is far less understood, which is part of why this is interesting.

JORDAN Why would an angiotensin receptor have anything to do with the lung at all? I think of it as a blood-pressure thing.

ALEX Fair instinct, and the paper leans on the lung-specific evidence to justify the whole project. In the airway, angiotensinogen is secreted by airway smooth-muscle cells and apoptotic epithelial cells, then cleaved down to active angiotensin II locally — so the pathway runs right there in lung tissue, not just in the vasculature. There are independent hints the RAS pathway is off in CF: patients show heightened RAS activation in response to salt deprivation, plausibly a compensation for salt loss, and higher serum ACE levels track with faster lung-function decline. AGTR2 itself is expressed in bronchi, airway epithelium, mucous glands, endothelium, fibroblasts and macrophages, and in other lung diseases — pulmonary fibrosis, COPD — AGTR2 antagonism attenuates fibrosis and elevated receptor levels show up in diseased tissue. So there's a real, if incomplete, biological prior that dialing down AGTR2 signaling helps an injured lung. That prior is what made a cardiovascular receptor a credible CF lung target.

JORDAN And a couple of genetics terms in their setup — the locus is on the X chromosome and there's "linkage disequilibrium." Translate.

ALEX Linkage disequilibrium — LD — means nearby variants are inherited together as a block, so they're statistically correlated; when a GWAS lights up a region, it's usually flagging a haplotype block, not one proven culprit. Here the original GWAS by the International CF Gene Modifier Consortium found multiple SNPs in LD in an interval on the X chromosome containing AGTR2, all associating with a longitudinal lung-function measure based on FEV1 — forced expiratory volume in one second, the standard spirometry readout of how much air you can blow out in a second. The lead SNP was rs5952223, a C/T polymorphism with a minor allele frequency of 0.26. And one subtlety they're careful about: the associating variants are in a non-coding region 3-prime of AGTR2, so this is almost certainly about gene regulation — how much AGTR2 you make — not a change to the protein itself.

JORDAN So what was unknown before this paper? They already had the GWAS.

ALEX Two big gaps, and they map onto the two weaknesses of any GWAS hit. First, GWAS gives you association, not causation, and crucially not *direction* — it tells you the locus matters but not whether more AGTR2 or less AGTR2 is the problem. You can't design a drug without knowing which way to push. Second, the original signal needed replication and extension. So the paper has a clear three-part plan: replicate the genetic association in a separate cohort and extend it to additional lung-function measures; then use genetically modified mice to nail down direction of effect; then test whether a drug reproduces the genetic result. That last step is what turns a modifier into a potential therapy.

JORDAN Walk me through what they actually did.

ALEX Three layers. Layer one, human genetics: a local CF cohort of 126 participants from the Cleveland CF center, mean age about 9 — notably younger than the original GWAS cohort — with spirometry. They genotyped rs5952223 and tested it against FEV1, FVC and FEF25-75 percent predicted. FVC is forced vital capacity, the total air exhaled; FEF25-75 is flow over the middle half of that exhalation, a small-airways readout. Because the spirometry distributions were skewed, they used median (quantile) regression with bootstrapped standard errors and clustering for repeated measures — a sensible robust choice. Layer two, mouse genetics: they crossed an Agtr2-knockout mouse into two independent CF mouse models — one carrying F508del, the most common CF mutation, and one carrying R117H — and measured lung mechanics on a computer-controlled ventilator. Layer three, pharmacology: they gave wild-type and CF mice subcutaneous PD123,319, a selective AGTR2 antagonist, two milligrams per kilogram, five days a week for 12 weeks starting at weaning, and measured the same lung mechanics.

JORDAN Where would you push on the design?

ALEX The mouse lung-mechanics endpoints are my pushback. CF mice don't replicate human CF lung disease — they don't get the bronchiectasis or the mucus plugging, and the authors say so. What they measured is respiratory-system compliance, tissue elastance, tissue damping — peripheral-lung mechanical properties — which they frame as a model of *early* CF lung disease. That's a defensible framing, but it means the mouse result is about early mechanical changes, not the destructive disease that actually kills people. The human-genetics layer is what tethers it to real lung function, and the small-airways FEF25-75 association is the nice bridge, because small-airways disease is plausibly that early-compartment phenotype.

JORDAN Give me the results. Did each layer deliver?

ALEX Layer one replicated and extended. The GWAS risk genotype — C in males, CC in females — was associated with 7.1% lower FEV1 percent predicted, 95% confidence interval roughly minus 5.1 to minus 9.0. Same risk genotype: 4.4% lower FVC and 5.3% lower FEF25-75, both significant. So the association replicated in a younger cohort and extended to two new, functionally related lung measures — suggesting the modifier acts early. Layer two: deleting Agtr2 restored respiratory-system compliance, tissue elastance, tissue damping and static compliance to near wild-type in F508del CF mice — though it did *not* fix distal airspace enlargement, so it's some but not all of the phenotype. And it worked in the R117H model too, which tells you the benefit is independent of CFTR genotype. Layer three, the payoff: the AGTR2 antagonist PD123,319 brought CF-mouse lung function to near wild-type levels — matching the genetic knockout — while having no effect in wild-type mice. So less AGTR2 signaling is beneficial, and you can get there with a drug.

JORDAN That CFTR-genotype-independence seems like the headline for us.

ALEX It is. Because the benefit showed up in two different CFTR mutations, the mechanism plausibly applies across CF regardless of mutation class — which is the dream for a modifier-derived therapy: one that helps everyone, not just one genotype. And it points the opposite way from the modulator drugs, which are CFTR-mutation-specific.

JORDAN Do they actually know how reducing AGTR2 helps?

ALEX No — and they're honest that the mechanism remains unknown, which is the catalog-relevant caveat. The RAS pathway touches tissue growth, inflammation, apoptosis, tissue repair and vascular responsiveness, so AGTR2 could be acting through any of those. They can say the direction — less signaling is better — and that it's CFTR-genotype-independent, but not the molecular route. For our purposes that's actually useful information: a modifier with a resolved direction but an open mechanism is exactly the kind of entry where a phenome-wide scan can earn its keep, because the phenotypes it does or doesn't touch become evidence about the mechanism. If the AGTR2 locus also moved, say, a vascular or inflammatory CF endpoint, that would point somewhere; if it's lung-isolated, that points elsewhere.

ALEX This is the **CF modifier-gene catalog** — Track C's core job, feeding the genes-and-variants catalog. AGTR2 goes in as a replicated, direction-resolved, mouse-validated lung-function modifier acting through regulation, on the X chromosome, CFTR-genotype-independent. The idea — take the AGTR2 locus, in particular rs5952223 and its LD block, and lay out a phenome-wide scan specification across CF-relevant endpoints beyond lung function: CF-related diabetes, liver disease, nutritional status, Pseudomonas colonization. The notebook would assemble the candidate variant set with their LD partners, define each endpoint and its covariates, and pre-register the X-chromosome handling — because the analysis has to encode sex correctly. Input: the AGTR2 locus variant list plus an endpoint dictionary. Difficulty small-to-medium. It's a design-and-scaffolding artifact — the value is a clean, pre-specified plan for asking whether this lung modifier touches anything else in the CF phenome, which is precisely the PheWAS white-space the catalog is built to expose.

JORDAN What would we need to check before believing or building on this?

ALEX A few things, all phrased as what we'd verify. First, the human replication is small — 126 patients, mostly self-identified white, single center — so we'd want to see the locus tested in a larger, more diverse CF cohort before treating the effect size as portable, and the paper notes this region was checked in CF-related diabetes and was *not* associated, which is a useful negative we'd want to confirm. Second, direction of effect is established in mice, not humans; the leap from "knockout helps mouse lung mechanics" to "antagonism helps human CF lung disease" is a hypothesis a trial would test, not a settled fact. Third, the X-chromosome encoding: any reuse of this locus has to handle hemizygous males versus females correctly, and the authors combined sexes on the argument of no differential expression — we'd re-examine that assumption in our own analysis. Fourth, there's a drug-class caution worth carrying: ACE inhibitors would reduce AGTR2 signaling and might help, but ARBs block AGTR1 and could *raise* angiotensin II at AGTR2 — potentially worse — so the mechanism has a directionality that matters for any real-world-data look at antihypertensive use in CF. None of this is decided; it's the checklist.

ALEX AGTR2 is the rare modifier with the full receipts — replicated association, resolved direction, two mouse models, and a drug — which makes it both a catalog anchor and a clean target for a pleiotropy scan across the CF phenome.
