Blurb

For years we've measured genetic effects one variant at a time, as if each DNA position votes independently. Fu and colleagues built FAME (FAst Marginal Epistasis test) to ask a different question at biobank scale: does a single variant's effect get modulated by the rest of a person's genome — its "polygenic background"? Earlier methods (MAPIT) couldn't run on hundreds of thousands of people; FAME uses a randomized variance-components estimator to test 500,000 people across 500,000 variants in ~6 hours on 32 GB of RAM. Applied to 53 quantitative traits in ~291,000 UK Biobank individuals, it found 16 significant marginal-epistasis signals across 12 traits, all replicating in either a held-out UKBB split or the All of Us cohort. Strikingly, the interaction component was on average ~12× larger than the variant's ordinary additive (GWAS) effect. Proposed direction (conditional): test whether CFTR-region and known-modifier variants show marginal epistasis against quantitative CF severity traits — with the honest caveat that ~5,000 CF patients may be far too few to power it.

Plain-English glossary

  • Additive effect — the standard GWAS assumption that each variant's contribution simply adds up, independent of other variants.
  • All of Us (AoU) — a large US research cohort (used here for external replication).
  • Calibration — a test is "calibrated" if, when there is truly no effect, it returns a false positive at exactly the expected rate.
  • Epistasis — when the effect of one genetic variant depends on the state of other variants (genes interacting, not just adding).
  • FAME (FAst Marginal Epistasis test) — the paper's tool for testing marginal epistasis on biobank-scale data.
  • GWAS (genome-wide association study) — scans many variants for association with a trait, one variant at a time, additively.
  • Heritability — fraction of a trait's variation across people explained by genetics.
  • Linkage disequilibrium (LD) — the tendency of nearby variants to be inherited together (correlated).
  • Mailman algorithm — a fast matrix-multiplication trick that speeds up the genotype computations.
  • MAF (minor allele frequency) — how common the rarer version of a variant is.
  • MAPIT — an earlier marginal-epistasis method that doesn't scale to biobank size.
  • Marginal epistasis — whether a single target variant's effect is modulated by the genetic background as a whole, rather than by one named partner variant.
  • Method-of-Moments (MoM) estimator — the (randomized) statistical machinery FAME uses to estimate its variance components quickly.
  • Missing heritability — the gap between twin-study heritability and what GWAS variants explain; epistasis is one candidate explanation.
  • Polygenic background — the combined effect of all of a person's other variants.
  • Quantitative trait — a trait measured on a continuous scale (e.g., a lab value), as opposed to a yes/no disease.
  • SNP (single-nucleotide polymorphism) — a single-letter DNA difference between people ("snip").
  • UK Biobank (UKBB) — ~500,000-person British cohort with genetic + health data.
  • Variance-components model — splits trait variation into buckets (additive genetic, interaction, noise) and estimates each.

Methods to lift

  • FAME — variance-components LMM that partitions trait variance into additive, marginal-epistasis-at-target-SNP, and residual buckets; tests whether the interaction bucket > 0. Public code; ~6 h for 500K people × 500K SNPs on 32 GB RAM.
  • Randomized Method-of-Moments estimator — approximates the heavy matrix computations by multiplying by ~100 random vectors instead of exact computation; the source of FAME's scalability.
  • LD-block regression fix — to avoid additive effects leaking in as fake interactions, regress out and exclude the LD-block neighbors of each target SNP before testing (required for calibration; costs sensitivity to local epistasis).
  • Internal + external replication design — split UKBB into discovery/replication halves, then replicate in All of Us — a template for any modifier-interaction claim.
  • Marginal-vs-GWAS variance comparison — report interaction heritability alongside the variant's GWAS heritability to show the interaction's relative magnitude.

ALEX Welcome back. Today's first paper is a 2025 study in the journal Nature Genetics by Boyang Fu and colleagues, titled "A biobank-scale test of marginal epistasis reveals genome-wide signals of polygenic interaction effects." It's a statistics-and-methods paper built on the UK Biobank, the half-million-person British research cohort with deep DNA and health data. We're reading it because it goes straight at a question that quietly haunts your whole project: does a single DNA variant's effect depend on the rest of a person's genome — and if so, can we even measure that at scale?

JORDAN Before we go one inch further, I need the vocabulary, because the title is mostly jargon to me. What is "epistasis," and what does "marginal" add to it?

ALEX Let's build it from nothing. A variant is a spot where people's DNA differs — one person has an A, another has a G at the same position. The single-letter kind has a name: a single-nucleotide polymorphism, abbreviated SNP, pronounced "snip." Now, a GWAS — genome-wide association study — is the workhorse method that scans hundreds of thousands of SNPs across many people and asks which ones line up with a trait, like height or cholesterol. A GWAS treats each variant as if it acts alone and just adds up. Epistasis is the word for when that's false: when the effect of one variant depends on what's happening at other variants. Genes talking to each other, not just voting independently. And "marginal" epistasis is a clever narrowing of the question. Instead of asking "does variant X interact with this specific other variant Y," which means checking billions of pairs, marginal epistasis asks a smaller question: "does variant X's effect get modulated by a person's overall genetic background, all the other variants taken together as a lump?"

JORDAN So it's the difference between asking "who exactly is variant X arguing with" versus just "is variant X being pushed around by the crowd at all"?

ALEX That's a perfect way to put it. And the crowd version is statistically much cheaper, because you ask it once per variant instead of once per pair. One more term you'll need: heritability. That's the fraction of a trait's variation across people that's explained by genetics. There's a famous puzzle called missing heritability — twin studies say a trait is, say, fifty percent genetic, but the SNPs a GWAS finds only add up to maybe fifteen percent. One long-standing suspect for the missing piece is epistasis: interactions that the additive, one-variant-at-a-time GWAS simply can't see.

JORDAN Okay, so the motivation is partly to chase that missing heritability. But people have wanted to test epistasis for years. What was actually blocking them?

ALEX Two walls, one statistical and one computational. The statistical wall: if you test every pair of variants for an interaction, you're running billions of tests, and to avoid being fooled by chance you have to set an absurdly strict bar for significance, which kills your power to detect anything real. The marginal-epistasis trick — asking only "does this variant interact with the genetic background as a whole" — dodges that, because you've collapsed billions of pair-tests into one test per variant. It also pools lots of tiny, individually-invisible interaction effects into one aggregate signal, which can actually be easier to detect. So conceptually, marginal epistasis was already attractive. The problem was the second wall: computation. The earlier method that did this, called MAPIT, simply could not run on biobank-sized data. The authors mention it would need more than three days just to handle twenty thousand people and ten thousand variants — and the UK Biobank has three hundred thousand people and half a million variants. At that scale MAPIT needs terabytes of memory. It's a non-starter.

JORDAN So the contribution isn't a brand-new idea, it's making an existing idea actually runnable on real biobank data?

ALEX Right, and that's worth respecting — a method you can't run is a method that doesn't exist in practice. Their tool is called FAME, which stands for FAst Marginal Epistasis test. The whole paper is: here's FAME, here's proof it gives trustworthy answers, and here's what it found when we finally could run it on the UK Biobank.

JORDAN So how does FAME actually pull off the speed-up? Walk me through the method.

ALEX FAME uses what's called a variance-components model. The idea: take all the variation in a trait across people and split it into buckets. One bucket is ordinary additive genetics — the normal GWAS-style summed-up effects. A second bucket is the marginal epistasis at the one target variant you're examining — how much extra variation comes from that variant interacting with the genetic background. A third bucket is leftover noise. FAME's job is to estimate the size of that middle bucket and test whether it's meaningfully bigger than zero. Now, the speed. Computing these buckets exactly means multiplying enormous matrices — tables with hundreds of thousands of rows. The standard exact approach chokes. FAME instead uses a randomized estimator: rather than crunch the whole giant matrix, it multiplies it by a modest number of random vectors — basically random probes — and gets an approximate answer that's almost as good. They found about a hundred random probes is plenty. Combined with a fast trick called the Mailman algorithm for the matrix math, FAME can test marginal epistasis on five hundred thousand people across half a million variants in roughly six hours using thirty-two gigabytes of memory — a desktop-server amount, not a supercomputer.

JORDAN I want to push on one thing. If you're testing whether a variant interacts with "everything else," how do you keep ordinary additive effects from leaking in and faking an interaction?

ALEX That's exactly the design choice I'd flag too, and to their credit they hit the same problem. When they picked target variants from GWAS hits, FAME's results came out inflated — too many false positives. Their fix: around each target variant there's a neighborhood of correlated variants — that correlation is called linkage disequilibrium, or LD, just meaning nearby variants travel together. They statistically removed the additive effects of that LD neighborhood and excluded those neighbors from the interaction test. After that scrub, the test was calibrated — meaning when there's truly no interaction, it correctly says so at the right rate. It's an honest fix, but notice the cost: by carving out the local neighborhood, they admit they probably miss any interaction that happens locally, right next to the target.

JORDAN So what did they actually find when they ran it? Did the genome turn out to be full of these interactions, or nearly empty?

ALEX Somewhere in between, and the honest answer is "modest but real." They applied FAME to fifty-three quantitative traits — things you measure on a scale, like blood lipids or liver enzymes — across about two hundred ninety thousand people. They tested their GWAS-significant variants and found sixteen solid marginal-epistasis signals across twelve traits, at a very strict significance bar. Sixteen is not a flood. But here's the part that made me sit up: for those sixteen, they estimated how much trait variation the interaction explained, and compared it to how much the variant explains through its ordinary additive GWAS effect. The interaction piece was on average about twelve times larger than the additive piece — ranging up to forty-fold. So where epistasis exists at all, it can dwarf the plain additive signal we usually report.

JORDAN Twelve times bigger than the effect we normally measure — that's striking. Did they check it wasn't a fluke?

ALEX They did, two ways. They split the UK Biobank in half and showed signals from one half replicated in the other. Then they went to a completely separate cohort — the American All of Us program — found five traits with enough people, and all five signals replicated. That external replication is the strongest evidence in the paper. They also dug into specifics. One example: a variant near the liver gene PNPLA3 — a gene tied to fatty-liver disease — showed strong marginal epistasis for a liver enzyme called ALT, and they traced part of it to a known interacting variant in another liver gene, HSD17B13. Another: a testosterone-associated variant that interacted only in females, sitting at a spot that alters estrogen-receptor binding. So the interactions aren't just statistical ghosts — some map onto sensible biology.
