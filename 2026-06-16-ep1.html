How did this episode land for you?

Blurb. Jumper et al. (Nature, 2021) introduced AlphaFold2, the CASP14 system that predicts protein 3D structure from sequence at near-experimental accuracy — median backbone error of 0.96 Å versus 2.8 Å for the next best method, roughly within a single carbon atom's width. The architecture pairs an "Evoformer" trunk (which jointly processes a multiple sequence alignment and a residue-pair graph via triangle attention) with a 3D structure module using equivariant invariant-point attention, trained with the FAPE loss, recycling, and noisy-student self-distillation. For variant interpretation the decisive output isn't the coordinates but pLDDT, a calibrated per-residue confidence (0–100) that reliably tracks local accuracy. Where it lands: the Karen Raraigh variant-interpretation collaboration. Buildable artifact: a CFTR variant structural-context annotator that maps a missense variant onto the AlphaFold CFTR model and reports pLDDT, burial, domain, and distance to the ATP pocket / nearest pathogenic residue.

Plain-English glossary

  • Ångström (Å) — 0.1 nanometer; a single carbon atom is ~1.4 Å wide. The unit structural accuracy is reported in.
  • Backbone — the repeating N–Cα–C atomic spine running down a protein chain.
  • CASP — Critical Assessment of protein Structure Prediction; biennial blind contest using newly solved, unreleased structures.
  • Domain (structural) — a self-contained folding unit within a protein (e.g., CFTR's NBD1).
  • Equivariant / invariant point attention — attention that reasons in 3D so predictions rotate consistently when the whole protein is rotated.
  • GDT / lDDT-Cα / r.m.s.d. — structure-accuracy metrics (global distance test; local distance difference test on Cα atoms; root-mean-square deviation).
  • MSA (multiple sequence alignment) — a stack of evolutionarily related sequences aligned position-by-position; co-evolving positions tend to be in 3D contact.
  • NBD1 / NBD2 — CFTR's two nucleotide-binding domains; bind/hydrolyze ATP to gate the channel. F508del deletes a residue in NBD1.
  • pLDDT — AlphaFold's per-residue confidence (0–100); high = ordered/reliable, low = disordered/unmodellable.
  • Protein Data Bank (PDB) — public repository of experimentally solved structures.
  • Residue — one amino acid position in the chain.
  • Side chain — the chemically variable group on each residue that distinguishes amino acids.

ALEX Today we're going all the way back to the paper that reset an entire field: Jumper and colleagues, "Highly accurate protein structure prediction with AlphaFold," Nature, 2021. This is the DeepMind team's CASP14 system, and we're reading it on Track A — variant interpretation — because before you can ask whether a CFTR missense change is benign or pathogenic, you increasingly want to know where that residue sits in the folded protein, and AlphaFold is what made residue-level structure cheap enough to ask that question for every variant.

JORDAN Okay, I want to start with vocabulary, because "structure prediction" gets thrown around like everyone knows what's being predicted. What is the actual output here?

ALEX Fair. A protein is a chain of amino acids — the residues — strung together like beads. That linear sequence is what your DNA encodes. But the chain folds up into a specific three-dimensional shape, and that shape is what does the work: it forms the channel, the binding pocket, the hinge. The "structure" AlphaFold predicts is the 3D coordinates of every atom in that folded chain, given only the amino acid sequence. Two terms you'll hear: the backbone is the repeating nitrogen–carbon–carbon spine that runs down the chain, and the side chains are the chemically distinct bits hanging off each residue that give each amino acid its personality.

JORDAN And accuracy is measured in Ångströms?

ALEX Right — an Ångström is a tenth of a nanometer. For scale, a single carbon atom is about 1.4 Ångströms across. So when they report accuracy of around one Ångström, they're saying the predicted atoms land within roughly an atom's width of where they actually are. One more term: a multiple sequence alignment, or MSA. You take your protein and find evolutionarily related versions of it across thousands of other organisms, stack them up, and line up the corresponding positions. Where two positions mutate together across evolution, they're probably touching in 3D. That co-evolution signal is the raw fuel structure predictors run on.

JORDAN So what was the state of the world before this paper that made it a big deal?

ALEX Predicting structure from sequence had been an open problem for fifty years. There were two camps. One simulated the physics — model the forces, let the chain settle into its lowest-energy shape. Beautiful in theory, computationally brutal, and it broke down for anything but small proteins. The other camp leaned on evolution and homology: if your protein looks like one whose structure was already solved experimentally, borrow that template. That worked when a close relative existed in the Protein Data Bank, the public repository of experimentally solved structures. But for proteins with no solved relative, predictions were far from experimental accuracy.

JORDAN And experimental accuracy means actually solving the structure in a lab.

ALEX X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, NMR — months to years per protein, and some proteins resist all three. About a hundred thousand unique structures had been solved that way, against billions of known sequences. Enormous gap. And that gap is why this matters for us: CFTR's experimental structures came relatively late and only for certain states, so for a long time, reasoning about where a CFTR residue sits meant either having a crystal structure of a close relative or guessing. The field ran a blind contest every two years called CASP — Critical Assessment of protein Structure Prediction — where organizers use structures that have just been solved but not yet released, so nobody can cheat by looking them up. CASP is the gold standard. In CASP14, in 2020, AlphaFold didn't just win, it posted numbers that looked like a different category of method.

JORDAN Why does no-template performance matter so much? Couldn't you always find some relative?

ALEX For something like CFTR, often yes — but the whole point of a general tool is the cases where you can't. The previous best methods degraded badly exactly when no close homologue had been solved, which is the regime where you most need help. AlphaFold's headline claim is that it stays near experimental accuracy even with no similar structure known — they show a 2,180-residue protein with no structural homologues predicted with accurate domains and correct domain packing. That generality is what turned structure from a per-protein research project into something you can run for an entire proteome overnight.

JORDAN Give me those numbers.

ALEX Median backbone accuracy of 0.96 Ångströms — under one atom width — versus 2.8 for the next best group. All-atom accuracy, which includes the side chains, was 1.5 Ångströms against 3.5 for the best alternative. And critically, they showed it held up on a large set of freshly released Protein Data Bank structures deposited after their training cutoff, not just the contest targets. So it generalized.

JORDAN Walk me through what they actually built, because I know there's an architecture with a name.

ALEX Two main stages. The first is the trunk, built from a repeated block they call the Evoformer — forty-eight of them stacked. The Evoformer takes two things: the MSA, represented as a sequences-by-residues array, and a pairwise representation, a residues-by-residues array encoding the relationship between every pair of positions. The clever bit is treating structure as a graph problem — residues are nodes, and the pair representation holds the edges. They use what they call triangle updates and triangle attention: if you know something about the relationship between residue A and B, and between B and C, that constrains A to C. Triangles enforce geometric consistency. And there's a feedback loop — information flows from the pair representation back into the MSA representation and around again.

JORDAN That's the part that's genuinely new versus just "a big transformer."

ALEX Exactly. The attention is geometry-aware, not generic. The second stage is the structure module. It represents each residue as a free-floating rigid body — they call it a "residue gas" — with a rotation and a translation in space, all initialized stacked at the origin, and then it refines them into real coordinates. The key innovation there is an equivariant attention mechanism — invariant point attention — that reasons in 3D so that if you rotate the whole protein, the predicted relationships rotate with it instead of breaking. The training loss, FAPE, compares predicted atom positions to true ones under many different local alignments, which forces both global and local correctness.

JORDAN You said you'd push back on a design choice. Where?

ALEX The reliance on deep MSAs. They showed accuracy is roughly flat until the alignment gets shallow — below about thirty sequences — and then it falls off a cliff. They even quantified it: stripping their big sequence databases dropped mean accuracy by 6.1 GDT points, concentrated in a few targets. For a well-studied protein like CFTR that's fine, the alignment is deep. But it means the method's confidence is partly a function of how much evolutionary data exists, not just the physics — and that has consequences when you start trusting it for variant effects, which we'll get to.

JORDAN They also did something with self-training?

ALEX Yes — noisy-student self-distillation. They took a trained network, predicted structures for around 350,000 diverse sequences, kept the high-confidence ones, and retrained from scratch on a mix of real Protein Data Bank data plus those predictions. Bootstrapping from unlabeled sequence. And there's recycling — they run the whole network up to four times, feeding the output back as input each pass, so the structure refines iteratively. They could even watch the trajectory across the forty-eight blocks and saw it converge smoothly toward the final fold.

JORDAN Now the piece that matters most for our track — the confidence score.

ALEX This is the single most important thing for variant work. AlphaFold emits a per-residue confidence called pLDDT, on a 0-to-100 scale, and it reliably predicts how accurate that residue actually is. There's also pTM, a whole-structure confidence. So you don't just get coordinates, you get a calibrated, position-by-position "how much should you trust me here." Ordered, well-packed regions score high; floppy, disordered regions score low.

JORDAN And that maps onto CFTR how?

ALEX CFTR is a big membrane protein with a clear domain layout: two membrane-spanning domains that form the chloride channel pore, two nucleotide-binding domains — NBD1 and NBD2 — that bind and hydrolyze ATP to gate the channel, and a regulatory "R" domain that's intrinsically disordered. F508del, the common mutation, deletes a single residue in NBD1 and wrecks folding and trafficking. Now, here's where Beat 6 lands, and it lands squarely on the Karen Raraigh variant-interpretation collaboration. When you're triaging a CFTR missense variant of uncertain significance, structural context is a real feature: is the residue buried in the NBD1 core, lining the ATP pocket, sitting at the NBD1–NBD2 interface, or is it out in the disordered R domain where almost anything is tolerated? AlphaFold gives you that context for free, and pLDDT tells you whether to trust the local geometry before you reason from it.

JORDAN One more before the build — does it matter that AlphaFold predicts the wild-type protein, when what we care about is the variant?

ALEX It matters a lot, and it's a subtlety people skip. AlphaFold gives you the structure of the reference CFTR sequence. It does not, in this 2021 form, tell you what a specific amino acid swap does to stability or function — that's a different prediction task. So the honest use is contextual: you're using the wild-type fold to ask "what neighborhood does this residue live in," not "is the structure destroyed." You could in principle fold the mutant sequence too and compare, but small single-residue changes often barely move the predicted backbone, so naive before-and-after folding is a weak signal for missense effect. The downstream missense predictors we'll cover tomorrow exist precisely because structure alone doesn't close that gap.

JORDAN So treat AlphaFold as the map, not the verdict.

ALEX Exactly — the map of where every residue sits, with a per-residue confidence on the map itself. That's genuinely valuable and it's free; just don't ask the map to also be the judge.

JORDAN So what's the buildable thing?

ALEX A CFTR variant structural-context annotator. MVP: a notebook that takes a CFTR missense variant in HGVS protein notation — say p.Arg347His — maps it onto the AlphaFold CFTR model, and returns four things: the residue's pLDDT, its solvent accessibility (buried versus surface), its distance to the nearest known pathogenic residue and to the ATP-binding pocket, and which domain it falls in. Input is a list of variants; output is a table you can paste next to REVEL or AlphaMissense scores. Difficulty: small-to-medium — the AlphaFold human structures are already downloadable, so it's mostly geometry and bookkeeping, no model training.

JORDAN Before anyone trusts that, what do we need to check?

ALEX Several things, all framed as "verify, don't assume." First, AlphaFold predicts one static structure; CFTR is a channel that moves between open and closed states, and a variant's damage might be about dynamics, not the resting fold — so a single structure can mislead. Second, that pLDDT caveat: low confidence in the R domain isn't evidence a variant there is benign, it's evidence the model can't see it. Third, and this is the big one the field learned after 2021 — AlphaFold's pLDDT is a confidence in the structure, not a measure of variant pathogenicity. People tried using it directly as a variant-effect score and it underperforms purpose-built predictors. So it's a context feature feeding interpretation, never a verdict. And we'd want to confirm the model covers the membrane-spanning regions well, since membrane proteins were historically harder. None of this is a settled pipeline — it's a feature to validate against CFTR2's classified variants before leaning on it.
