Blurb

Min Yu and colleagues (Cell Genomics, 2026) deliver the strongest evidence to date that the mutations causing cystic fibrosis also lower the risk of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Using a discovery set of 38,558 IBD cases vs 66,945 European-ancestry controls plus a diverse-ancestry replication of 42,475 cases vs 192,050 controls, they show the most common CF mutation, F508del, is protective against IBD (OR 0.82, p≈9×10⁻¹¹), strongest for Crohn's disease (OR 0.79). A burden test aggregating 141 other curated CF-causing variants confirms collective protection (OR 0.85, p≈3.9×10⁻⁷), while non-CF-causing variants — the negative control — show nothing. The proposed direction for Runyu is the mirror image: inside a CF cohort (two broken copies), does residual CFTR function grade with IBD and gut-inflammation phenotypes? The open question is whether a protective signal seen in heterozygous carriers has any analogue when both alleles are already disabled, and whether 5,000 patients can power such a test.

Plain-English glossary

  • Burden test — a rare-variant method that collapses many rare variants into one combined per-person count, then tests whether carrying any of them shifts disease risk; needed because each rare variant alone is too rare to reach significance.
  • Carrier (heterozygous) — a person with one broken gene copy and one working copy; healthy for CF, but the unit of interest in this paper.
  • CD (Crohn's disease) — an IBD subtype that can affect any part of the digestive tract.
  • CFTR — the chloride/bicarbonate channel gene; broken in CF; the gene this whole project studies.
  • CFTR2 — an expert-curated clinical database that classifies each CFTR variant as CF-causing, varying clinical consequence, non-CF-causing, or unknown.
  • Exome sequencing — sequencing only the ~2% protein-coding genome; cheaper than whole-genome and catches most CF-causing variants.
  • F508del (deltaF508) — the single most common CF-causing variant; an in-frame deletion; ~1.5% MAF in non-Finnish Europeans.
  • IBD (inflammatory bowel disease) — chronic gut inflammation; two main subtypes, Crohn's (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC).
  • LoF (loss-of-function) — a mutation that breaks the protein so it works less or not at all.
  • MAF (minor-allele frequency) — how common a variant is in the population.
  • Negative control — a variant set expected to show no effect (here, non-CF-causing variants); confirms the real signal isn't an artifact.
  • Odds ratio (OR) — the risk multiplier; below 1 = protective. OR 0.82 ≈ 18% lower odds.
  • PheWAS (phenome-wide association study) — fixes one gene and asks which of many traits/diagnoses it associates with.
  • Single-variant test — tests one specific variant's association with disease.
  • UC (ulcerative colitis) — an IBD subtype confined to the colon.

Methods to lift

  • CFTR2-anchored variant labeling — use an expert clinical database to define the "causal" variant set rather than computational prediction; outperformed naive molecular-consequence filters here.
  • Unweighted burden test — collapse a curated rare-variant set into a single mask genotype (sum of allele dosages); tested with METAL inverse-variance fixed-effect meta-analysis across studies.
  • Logistic mixed model with Firth correction — handles case-control imbalance for rare-variant association; 5 PCs + exome-capture-kit covariate to control structure/batch.
  • Negative-control variant set — non-CF-causing CFTR variants run in parallel to confirm specificity.
  • Hidden-CF sensitivity analysis — quantify how many undiagnosed CF patients in controls would be needed to erase the signal (≈200, implying implausible 36% diagnostic sensitivity).
  • AlphaMissense benchmarking — a structure-based ML pathogenicity predictor; useful but low-specificity vs curated labels for this gene.
  • Local ancestry inference (SHAPEIT5 phasing → FLARE) for admixed-cohort burden testing at the CFTR locus.

It's called "Cystic fibrosis risk variants confer protection against inflammatory bowel disease," first author Min Yu and colleagues, published in 2026 in Cell Genomics, which is a respected open-access genetics journal from the Cell Press family. We're reading it because it's the cleanest recent evidence that the very mutations that cause cystic fibrosis also lower the risk of a completely different disease — and that idea sits right at the heart of what a phenome-wide scan of CFTR would be looking for.

JORDAN Okay, before we go one inch further, I need the vocabulary. You said "phenome-wide." What does that even mean, and what's IBD?

ALEX Fair. Let's build the toolkit. IBD is inflammatory bowel disease — a group of long-term conditions where the gut lining is chronically inflamed. It has two main flavors: Crohn's disease, which can hit anywhere in the digestive tract, and ulcerative colitis, which is confined to the colon. You'll hear me say CD for Crohn's and UC for ulcerative colitis. Now, "phenome" is just the whole catalog of a person's traits and diagnoses — every phenotype. A PheWAS, phenome-wide association study, fixes one gene and asks, "which of these many traits does it associate with?" That's the lens this whole podcast is building toward for CFTR. Two more terms you'll need today. A loss-of-function variant — I'll say LoF — is a mutation that breaks the protein so it works less or not at all; that's exactly what CF-causing CFTR mutations do. And minor-allele frequency, MAF, is just how common a variant is in the population — a MAF of 1.5% means roughly one and a half of every hundred copies of that spot in the genome carry the variant.

JORDAN And a "carrier"?

ALEX A carrier has one broken copy and one working copy — heterozygous. People with CF have two broken copies. Carriers are healthy as far as CF goes, but the question this paper asks is whether that one broken copy quietly does something elsewhere in the body.

JORDAN So why would anyone expect a CF mutation to protect against a gut disease? That sounds backwards.

ALEX It does, but there's a beautiful logic. CFTR is a channel that pumps chloride and bicarbonate out of epithelial cells — the cells that line your lungs, gut, and other surfaces. That ion flow controls how watery and how acidic the mucus is. In the gut, that mucus is a barrier that traps microbes and keeps the immune system from overreacting. Now think about cholera: cholera toxin hijacks CFTR, cranks it wide open, and you get catastrophic watery diarrhea. There's a long-standing hypothesis that CF carriers were historically protected from cholera because their half-strength CFTR couldn't be hijacked as hard. The authors take that same intuition into IBD. IBD is fundamentally a disease of a leaky, inflamed gut barrier. If having slightly less CFTR activity changes mucus and barrier behavior, maybe it nudges IBD risk down. And there's precedent for "protective" genetics being medically gold — people with broken PCSK9 have low cholesterol and less heart disease, and that observation literally created a blockbuster drug class. Protective variants tell you which direction to push a system safely.

JORDAN Got it. So what exactly was unknown before this paper?

ALEX Whether the protection is real. Earlier studies hinted that CFTR LoF variants might lower intestinal inflammation, but the results were a mess — some said protection, some said increased risk, some said nothing. Small samples, inconsistent definitions of which variants count as "CF-causing." The field needed a large, carefully curated test. That's the gap Yu and colleagues set out to fill, and the way they defined "CF-causing" is the smartest part of the design.

JORDAN Walk me through how they actually did it.

ALEX They assembled a very large exome dataset. Exome means they sequenced just the protein-coding ~2% of the genome, which is cheaper than whole-genome and catches most CF-causing mutations because those are mostly in coding regions. The discovery stage was 38,558 IBD cases versus 66,945 controls, all of European ancestry, drawn from the International IBD Genetics Consortium plus the UK Biobank — the UK Biobank being a half-million-person research cohort with genetics and health records. Then a replication stage added 42,475 cases and 192,050 controls across diverse ancestries. The clever move is how they labeled variants. Instead of guessing which CFTR mutations are damaging, they leaned on CFTR2 — a clinical database curated by experts who reviewed real CF patients to decide whether each variant actually causes disease. They called the curated pathogenic ones "CF-risk" variants and everything else "non-CF-risk," which becomes their negative control. Then they ran two kinds of test. A single-variant test asks whether one specific variant associates with IBD. And a burden test — this is key for rare variants — collapses many rare variants into one combined count per person and asks whether carrying any of them, in aggregate, shifts risk. You need that because each rare variant alone is too rare to reach significance.

JORDAN Where would you push back on the design?

ALEX My pushback is the one they actually anticipated, which I respect. If you ascertain controls from hospitals, you might accidentally enrich them with undiagnosed CF patients, and that could fake a protective signal. They handled it by removing everyone whose genetics predicted CF, and then doing a sensitivity calculation — roughly 200 hidden CF carriers would have had to be missed in the controls to erase the result, which would require an implausibly bad 36% diagnostic sensitivity. That's a thoughtful defense. My lingering worry is subtler: the whole thing rests on CFTR2's curation being right and near-complete, so the result is only as good as that database.

JORDAN And the verdict — did the protection show up?

ALEX Strongly. The single most common CF mutation, F508del — an in-frame deletion with a MAF around 1.5% in Europeans — was protective against IBD overall, with an odds ratio of 0.82 and a p-value of about 9 times 10 to the minus 11. Quick translation: an odds ratio below 1 means lower risk, so 0.82 is roughly an 18% reduction, and that tiny p-value means it's extremely unlikely to be chance. The effect was strongest for Crohn's, odds ratio 0.79, and weaker and only nominally significant for ulcerative colitis. Then the burden test, aggregating 141 other CF-risk variants and excluding F508del, also showed protection — combined odds ratio 0.85, p around 3.9 times 10 to the minus 7. The negative control behaved: the non-CF-risk variants showed no such enrichment, which is exactly what you want, because it says the signal tracks real CF biology and not just "any rare CFTR variant." And it replicated at nominal significance in East Asian and African-American groups too. So yes — they answered their question. CF-risk variants protect against IBD, mostly Crohn's, across multiple ancestries.

JORDAN Before we get to me — did they say anything about how the protection actually works in the gut?

ALEX They offered mechanism hypotheses, and they're worth knowing because they shape what phenotypes you'd look at. One: less CFTR activity means less watery secretion, so the mucus barrier stays thicker and better at trapping microbes — the same logic as the cholera-resistance story. Two, and this one's specific: in the gut, CFTR is most highly expressed by a cell type called BEST4-positive enterocytes, which help keep the gut calm and are known to shut down during active ulcerative colitis. The idea is that carriers' BEST4 cells run in a lower-reactivity mode that's more stress-tolerant. Three: CFTR also shapes the gut microbiome — in mice, the deltaF508 version let far fewer Salmonella Typhi bacteria cross into the gut wall. So the protection might be barrier, cell-state, microbiome, or all three. They're explicit these are hypotheses for follow-up, not proven.

JORDAN And there was something about variant annotation being a big deal?

ALEX Yes, and it's a methods lesson you should file away. They tested whether a popular machine-learning tool called AlphaMissense — which predicts whether a missense mutation is damaging from protein structure — could substitute for the hand-curated CFTR2 labels. It couldn't, fully: AlphaMissense flagged many variants as "likely pathogenic" that the curated database says aren't CF-causing, so it had low specificity. The burden test was strongest with the expert clinical labels and noticeably weaker with naive computational filters. The takeaway: how you decide which variants "count" can make or break a rare-variant test, and for CFTR you're lucky to have a gold-standard clinical database that most genes don't.
