Blurb (~150w)

Wen and colleagues (Nature Genetics, 2025) compared clonal hematopoiesis (CH) — the age-related expansion of a mutated blood-stem-cell clone — across 136,401 admixed participants of the Mexico City Prospective Study (MCPS) and 416,118 European-ancestry participants of the UK Biobank (UKB). CH was significantly less common in MCPS (3.12% vs 4.92%; OR 0.59, P=7.3×10⁻¹⁸⁵), and within MCPS its frequency rose with each individual's European-ancestry fraction (P≈10⁻¹⁹), pointing to a genetic rather than environmental driver. Variant-level analysis found ancestry-specific effects (notably at TCL1B) and, via cross-ancestry meta-analysis, five novel CH loci. Proposed CF direction: treat the ancestry-dependence of a known CFTR-modifier effect (e.g., SLC26A9→CFRD) as a testable hypothesis rather than assuming a borrowed effect size is universal. Open question: whether the CFF registry has the non-European sample size and WGS-supported local-ancestry inference to power such a test at all.

Plain-English glossary

  • Admixed / admixture: a genome that is a mosaic of more than one ancestral population (MCPS ≈ 66% Indigenous American, 31% European, 3% African).
  • Biobank: a research collection linking many people's DNA to their health records.
  • Clonal hematopoiesis (CH): an acquired state where one mutated blood-stem-cell clone makes an outsized share of blood cells; rises with age, nudges blood-cancer/heart risk.
  • Confidence interval (CI): the plausible range around an estimate.
  • Cross-ancestry meta-analysis: statistically combining results from cohorts of different ancestry to gain power and find shared signals.
  • DNMT3A / TET2: the two most common CH driver genes (epigenetic regulators); CH is sub-typed by which driver mutated.
  • Driver mutation: the acquired mutation that gives a clone its growth advantage.
  • GWAS / ExWAS: genome-wide / exome-wide association study — scanning many genetic spots against a trait.
  • Local ancestry inference: labelling each chunk of a genome by its ancestral origin (here via RFMix).
  • MAF (minor allele frequency): how common the rarer version of a genetic spot is.
  • Odds ratio (OR): ratio of odds between two groups; <1 = less likely, >1 = more likely.
  • peddy: a software classifier assigning continental ancestry from sequencing data.
  • REGENIE: a scalable program for testing variants against a trait while accounting for relatedness.
  • Somatic (vs germline): acquired-during-life (somatic) rather than inherited (germline).
  • WES / WGS: whole-exome (protein-coding ~2%) / whole-genome sequencing.

Methods to lift

  • Within-cohort ancestry-fraction regression — regress the phenotype on each individual's continental-ancestry fraction within one admixed cohort, holding country/recruitment constant; the cleanest way to separate genetic effect from environment.
  • RFMix local-ancestry inference — walks the genome labelling each window's ancestral origin (39,861 windows here); enables haplotype- and gene-level ancestry analysis.
  • peddy — ML classifier for continental ancestry from sequencing data (≥95% probability threshold used to define strata).
  • REGENIE — scalable single-variant and gene-based association testing accounting for relatedness; used for GWAS + ExWAS.
  • Cross-ancestry (stratified) meta-analysis — IVW and P-value-based (METAL) combination of summary statistics across MCPS + UKB; recovered 5 loci neither cohort found alone.
  • TOPMed as an external multi-ancestry replication panel for overall CH.

Proposed direction (conditional)

Conditional on inspection of CFF WGS variant calls and registry phenotype linkage: within the CF cohort, test whether the association between a candidate CFTR-modifier variant and a CF phenotype is itself modified by individual continental-ancestry fraction (estimated by local-ancestry inference), rather than assuming a modifier effect size estimated in European-ancestry cohorts transfers unchanged to the registry's ancestry mix.

What to verify first

  • Actual ancestry composition of the CFF registry — how many non-European patients, given the cohort is ~5,000 total vs Wen's 136,401 admixed.
  • Whether the WGS data supports local-ancestry inference (variant density + reference panels for the relevant populations).
  • Whether any modifier effect is estimable per ancestry stratum at CF sample sizes (slicing 5,000 by ancestry may leave strata underpowered).
  • Whether a single self-reported race field is being used where genetic-ancestry fraction is what the analysis actually needs.

JORDAN Good morning, Runyu. Today's first paper is "Comparative analysis of the Mexico City Prospective Study and the UK Biobank identifies ancestry-specific effects on clonal hematopoiesis," by Wen and colleagues, published in 2025 in Nature Genetics. We're reading it because it asks a question that sits right underneath your whole project: when you measure a genetic effect in one population, does that same number hold up in a different one? For a registry as diverse as the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation's, that is not a side issue — it is the issue.

ALEX Exactly. And the honest reason I picked it is that it's a clean, well-powered worked example of cross-ancestry comparison done carefully. Before we open it up, though, almost every word in the title needs unpacking, so let me go slowly.

JORDAN Please. Start with the thing being studied. What is "clonal hematopoiesis"?

ALEX Hematopoiesis is just the process by which your bone marrow makes blood cells. Over a lifetime, the stem cells that do this pick up random DNA typos — mutations. Usually harmless. But occasionally one stem cell gets a mutation that gives it a slight growth edge, so its descendants — its "clone" — start to make up an outsized share of your blood. That state is clonal hematopoiesis, CH for short. It's common in older people and it nudges up the risk of blood cancers and heart disease. For today, though, CH is mostly just the example trait. The paper's real subject is ancestry.

JORDAN So what's a "biobank," and what are these two specifically?

ALEX A biobank is a giant research collection that links people's DNA to their health records. The UK Biobank, UKB, is the famous one — about half a million mostly European-ancestry adults in Britain. The Mexico City Prospective Study, MCPS, is a comparable cohort of over 150,000 adults recruited in Mexico City. The crucial difference is ancestry. The MCPS participants are admixed — meaning their genomes are a mosaic stitched together from more than one ancestral population. On average these participants are about 66 percent Indigenous American, 31 percent European, and 3 percent African in their DNA. That admixture is what makes the study possible.

JORDAN And a couple of the shorthand terms you'll lean on?

ALEX Three quick ones. An odds ratio, OR, compares the odds of something between two groups — an OR below one means less likely, above one means more likely. A confidence interval, CI, is the plausible range around that estimate. And MAF, minor allele frequency, is just how common the rarer version of a genetic spot is — a variant with MAF of one percent shows up on one percent of chromosomes. Hold those three; they recur all morning.

JORDAN Okay. So what was actually unknown here that made the study worth doing?

ALEX The background problem is one you've heard me grumble about before: the overwhelming majority of genetic studies have been done in people of European descent, recruited mostly in the US and UK. That creates a blind spot. We don't actually know whether the genetic effects we've catalogued in Europeans hold in everyone else, because we mostly haven't looked. For clonal hematopoiesis specifically, one earlier study had claimed CH frequency was about the same across ancestries. But that hadn't been tested at scale in a large non-European cohort. So Wen and colleagues had two questions. First, descriptively: is CH more or less common in a Latin American cohort than in a European one? Second, mechanistically: are there genetic variants whose effect on CH depends on ancestry — variants you'd simply miss if you only studied Europeans?

JORDAN And why does an admixed cohort help answer that, as opposed to just comparing two separate populations?

ALEX This is the elegant part. Because each MCPS participant carries a personal blend of Indigenous American, European, and African DNA, you can ask a within-person question instead of a between-country one. You can line up MCPS individuals and ask: among people in the same cohort, same city, same recruitment, does CH get more common as the European fraction of their genome goes up? If yes, that points to genetics rather than to lifestyle or healthcare differences between two countries — because you've held the country constant.

JORDAN How did they actually do it? Walk me through the machinery.

ALEX They started from sequencing data. Most of this work uses WES — whole-exome sequencing, which reads only the roughly two percent of the genome that codes for proteins — to call the CH driver mutations, using a tool called Mutect2 that flags somatic, meaning acquired-not-inherited, variants in a list of 74 known blood-cancer genes. To assign each person's continental ancestry they used a classifier called peddy, trained on reference populations. Then the clever step: a tool called RFMix walks along the genome and labels each chunk as Indigenous American, European, or African — that's called local ancestry inference, and it's what turns the genome into the mosaic map I described. For the association tests — scanning variants against CH — they used REGENIE, which is a standard, scalable program for testing millions of genetic variants against a trait while accounting for relatedness between people. One design choice I'd push on: they restricted the admixed analysis to people with at least 95 percent confidence in the "admixed American" label, which keeps things clean but does throw away ambiguous individuals — and the ambiguous middle is exactly where some of the most admixed, most informative people sit.

JORDAN One thing before results — you keep saying "subtypes" of CH. What does that mean?

ALEX Good catch. Clonal hematopoiesis isn't one thing; it's named after whichever driver gene picked up the mutation. The two most common drivers are genes called DNMT3A and TET2 — both are "epigenetic regulators," meaning they normally help control which genes a cell switches on without changing the DNA letters themselves. A clone driven by a DNMT3A mutation can behave differently from one driven by TET2, so researchers analyze them separately — DNMT3A-CH, TET2-CH, and so on. That granularity matters here, because the ancestry-specific effects Wen found weren't uniform across subtypes — a variant could push one subtype up and leave another flat, or even push it the other way.

JORDAN Got it. So what did they find — did they answer their two questions?

ALEX Cleanly, yes. First question: CH was significantly less common in Mexico City than in the UK — 3.12 percent versus 4.92 percent, an odds ratio of 0.59 with a confidence interval of 0.57 to 0.61, and an astronomically small p-value, 7.3 times ten to the minus 185. That difference held up even after matching people by age and sex. So the earlier "CH is the same everywhere" claim doesn't survive at this scale. Second question, the within-cohort test: among MCPS participants, CH frequency rose with the European fraction of the genome — a positive association with a p-value around ten to the minus 19. So ancestry tracks CH risk even inside one cohort. And at the variant level they found genuinely ancestry-specific signals — most strikingly at a locus called TCL1B, where variants had opposing effects on different subtypes of CH. A rare variant in the TCL1B promoter, present on about a third of a percent of chromosomes, raised the risk of one CH subtype with an odds ratio above four, and another subtype with an odds ratio near seventeen. By then pooling MCPS and UKB in a cross-ancestry meta-analysis — statistically combining the two cohorts — they pulled out five brand-new CH loci, near genes called PARP11/CCND2, MEIS1, and MYCN, that neither cohort would have found alone.

JORDAN So the diversity wasn't just fairness — it bought new discoveries.

ALEX Right, and that's the headline I want you to keep. Cross-ancestry comparison did two things at once: it corrected a wrong belief (CH frequencies differ), and it found biology that a Europeans-only study was structurally blind to. Both matter for you.

JORDAN So bring it home. What concrete direction does this suggest for the CFTR work?

ALEX Here's one possible direction this episode suggests — not the plan, just a thread. You already track CFTR modifier genes — SLC26A9 and CFRD risk, for instance. The unspoken assumption whenever we quote a modifier's effect size is that the number is universal — that an SLC26A9 risk allele bumps diabetes risk by the same amount in a patient of European ancestry as in one of African or admixed ancestry. Wen's paper is a standing argument that you shouldn't assume that for free. So the direction is: treat ancestry-dependence of a CF modifier effect as a hypothesis to test, not a footnote. In plain English — does a known CF modifier hit harder, softer, or differently depending on a patient's genetic ancestry? Formally: within the CF cohort, estimate whether the association between a candidate modifier variant and a CF phenotype is modified by individual continental-ancestry fraction, using local-ancestry inference rather than a single self-reported race label.

JORDAN And conditional on that direction — what does Wen actually close for you, and what would you still have to check?

ALEX What it closes: it hands you a validated template. The machinery — local-ancestry inference with something like RFMix, then regressing the phenotype on ancestry fraction within one cohort — is exactly the tool you'd reuse, and Wen shows it works and what a real signal looks like. What it leaves wide open is whether you can run it at all, and that's all about your data, which neither of us has inspected. The things we'd need to check, framed honestly as checks and not conclusions: first, what is the actual ancestry composition of the CFF registry — how many patients are non-European, because Wen had 136,000 admixed people and you have on the order of 5,000 patients total. Second, whether the whole-genome sequencing data even supports local-ancestry inference — that needs decent variant density and reference panels for the relevant populations. Third, and soberingly, whether any modifier effect is estimable per ancestry stratum at CF sample sizes, because slicing 5,000 people by ancestry could leave each slice too small to say anything. None of those are settled. They are the homework that would have to come before any ancestry-interaction claim.
