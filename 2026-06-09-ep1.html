Blurb (~150w)

Zhu and colleagues (Cell Genomics, 2026) tackle a long-standing computational wall: an ancestral recombination graph (ARG) — a reconstructed genome-wide family tree of DNA — can in principle capture the effect of unmeasured genetic variants, but building ARG-based mixed-model analyses for a biobank has been prohibitively slow. They introduce ARG-RHE, a randomized Haseman-Elston method that multiplies through the genotype matrix in sublinear time, enabling scalable heritability estimation and gene-region association testing. Applied to 337,464 UK Biobank participants, 21,159 gene regions, and 52 blood traits, ARG-RHE found 8% more gene-trait associations than imputation alone — though at higher compute cost than the plain imputed region test. The proposed direction for the CF project: test whether region-based (variant-pooling) approaches recover more modifier signal than single-variant scanning in the small ~5,000-person CF WGS cohort. Open question: can a 5,000-person cohort support reliable ARG inference at all, and do binary CF phenotypes fit a quantitative-only tool?

Plain-English glossary

  • ARG (ancestral recombination graph): a reconstructed family tree of every chunk of DNA in a sample, capturing who is related to whom at each position along the chromosome.
  • Array / genotyping array: a chip that reads a pre-chosen subset of common DNA spots (here ~800,000) rather than the whole genome.
  • Association testing (region-based): asking whether a whole gene region, with its variants pooled, is associated with a trait — rather than testing one variant at a time.
  • Complex trait: a measurable feature (height, blood pressure, a blood count) influenced by many genes plus environment.
  • CPU-hours: a measure of compute cost — one processor running for one hour.
  • GRM (genetic relatedness matrix): a grid giving the genetic similarity of every pair of people in a study; the engine inside a mixed model.
  • GWAS (genome-wide association study): scanning millions of genome positions to find variants associated with a trait.
  • Haseman-Elston / RHE (randomized Haseman-Elston): a "moment-based" (rather than likelihood-based) method for estimating heritability, made scalable with randomization.
  • Heritability: the fraction of a trait's variation explained by genetics.
  • Imputation: statistically guessing the DNA letters you didn't directly measure, using a reference panel of fully-sequenced people. HRC = a common reference panel.
  • LMM (linear mixed model): a model that tests variant-trait association while correcting for hidden relatedness among participants.
  • Randomized algorithm: an algorithm that uses random sampling to approximate a too-costly exact answer, trading tiny imprecision for a large speedup.
  • Regenie: a widely used existing tool for biobank-scale association testing (the comparison baseline here).
  • Sublinear time: computation that grows slower than the size of the data.
  • UKBB (UK Biobank): a research resource with genetic + health data on ~500,000 UK participants.
  • WGS (whole-genome sequencing): reading a person's entire DNA letter by letter.

Methods to lift

  • ARG-RHE — randomized Haseman-Elston estimator running on an ARG; does both heritability estimation and gene-region association testing for quantitative traits.
  • Region-based variance-component testing — pools variants within a gene region into a single test (the power-recovery idea most relevant to a small cohort).
  • Randomized trace estimation — the speedup engine; approximates expensive matrix quantities by random projection.
  • ARG inference from array variants — reconstruct the genealogy from ~800k genotyped variants, then use it to "see" unmeasured variation.
  • Comparators: HRC-RHE (imputed region test), Regenie (single-variant LMM), ACAT-V (p-value combination within regions).

An ARG is the giant branching diagram that captures, for each position along the chromosome, who is related to whom and how recently. The claim of this line of research is: if you have that genealogy, you implicitly know about variants nobody measured, because shared ancestry leaves a trace even for the letters you never read.

The engine inside an LMM is the genetic relatedness matrix, the GRM: a big grid that says, for every pair of people, how genetically similar they are.
