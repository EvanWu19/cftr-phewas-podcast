How did this episode land for you?

Blurb

A companion to FAME (read in today's first episode). SME — Sparse Marginal Epistasis test — makes biobank-scale interaction scans faster by not testing a target variant against the whole genome. Instead it "masks" the search down to a biologically-chosen subset of variants — for example, only those in DNase I-hypersensitive (open, regulatory) DNA active in the relevant tissue. On UK Biobank data (~349,411 people, ~543,813 SNPs) SME ran ~10× faster than FAME and ~90× faster than MAPIT (3.7 days vs 38 vs 324), stayed calibrated under the null where FAME's statistics inflated at large N, and recovered sensible, literature-supported interaction signals for red-blood-cell traits (top hit SCGN, p≈1.4×10⁻¹¹; multiple CARMIL1 signals). A built-in honesty check — a trait (hematocrit) where the mask was expected to be unhelpful — correctly failed to light up. Proposed direction (conditional): a sparse CF scan masked to the known short list of CF modifier loci — appealing precisely because it minimizes the multiple-testing burden a ~5,000-patient cohort can't otherwise afford.

Methods to lift

  • SME — marginal-epistasis variance-components test that masks the interaction search to an externally-chosen variant subset; ~10× faster than FAME, ~90× faster than MAPIT.
  • Masking from external data — build the mask from DNase-hypersensitive (regulatory) maps for the relevant tissue, or from GWAS summary statistics, to concentrate power where interactions are plausible.
  • Hutchinson's stochastic trace estimator + shared random vectors — reuse heavy computations across many target SNPs to amortize cost.
  • Built-in negative control — deliberately run a trait where the mask should be unhelpful (hematocrit) to confirm the method doesn't rubber-stamp the prior.
  • Calibration-at-scale comparison — they show FAME's statistics inflate as N grows while SME stays calibrated; worth replicating before trusting either at a given sample size.

Proposed direction (conditional)

Conditional on inspection of CFF WGS variant calls and registry phenotype linkage: run a sparse marginal-epistasis scan with the mask restricted to established CF lung-function modifier loci (chr5p13 SLC9A3/CEP72, chr11p13 EHF/APIP, and other catalogued modifiers), testing whether CFTR-region variants show interaction signal concentrated at those modifier regions against a quantitative severity phenotype — explicitly to minimize multiple-testing burden in a small cohort.

What to verify first

  • Whether even a focused/masked scan retains power at ~5,000 patients (SME's hits came from ~349,000 people). Same make-or-break check as FAME.
  • Whether CF-relevant regulatory maps (DHS or equivalent) exist for the airway/epithelial tissues that matter, or whether the practical "mask" reduces to the coarse modifier-locus list.
  • Whether a quantitative CF severity score behaves like SME's validated blood-cell traits; binary CF complications need separate treatment.
  • Whether the catalogued modifier-region variants are cleanly called in the CF WGS data (not yet personally inspected).

ALEX Our second paper today is a 2025 study in the American Journal of Human Genetics by Julian Stamp and colleagues, titled "Sparse modeling of interactions enables fast detection of genome-wide epistasis in biobank-scale studies." It's a close cousin of the first paper we read this morning — same broad goal, different engine. We're reading it back-to-back with the FAME paper on purpose, because together they show two different bets on how to make gene-interaction scans practical, and the contrast teaches you more than either alone.

JORDAN Since we just did the vocabulary an hour ago, give me the fast refresher and then tell me what's genuinely new here.

ALEX Quick refresher. A SNP — single-nucleotide polymorphism — is a one-letter spot where DNA differs between people. A GWAS, genome-wide association study, scans many SNPs and asks which line up with a trait, treating each as if it acts alone. Epistasis is when that assumption fails — when one variant's effect depends on others. And marginal epistasis is the narrowed, cheaper question: not "which exact pair of variants interact," but "is this one variant's effect modulated by the genetic background as a whole?" The UK Biobank, abbreviated UKBB, is the half-million-person British cohort everyone tests these methods on. That's all carryover from the FAME episode. Here's what's new. This tool is called SME — Sparse Marginal Epistasis test. The key new word is sparse. FAME, when it tests a target variant, considers that variant's interactions with essentially every other variant in the genome. SME's bet is that most of those interactions are noise, and that you should deliberately *narrow* the search to a small, biologically-chosen subset of variants before you scan.

JORDAN So FAME interacts the target with everything; SME interacts it with a hand-picked few. What does "biologically chosen" mean in practice?

ALEX It means SME brings outside information to decide which variants are worth interacting with. Their main example uses something called DNase I-hypersensitive sites, abbreviated DHS. Let me unpack that, because it's a real concept worth owning. Your DNA is wound up tightly most places, but in regions where genes are actively being switched on, the DNA is loosened and exposed — and an enzyme called DNase I can cut there. So a "DNase-hypersensitive site" is a map-marker for *open, regulatory, actively-used DNA*. SME uses that map to say: only bother testing interactions involving variants that sit in these active regulatory regions. They call this narrowing a "mask" — you mask out the variants you've decided to ignore. The promise is that by not wasting effort on the boring, closed regions, you go much faster and you concentrate your statistical power where biology says interactions are likely.

JORDAN That's an interesting trade. You might gain power by focusing — but couldn't you also miss a real interaction that happens to live in a region your mask threw away?

ALEX That's precisely the central risk, and you put your finger on it before they even raised it. SME is a bet that the mask is pointing at the right places. If your outside information is good, you win on both speed and power. If your mask is wrong for a given trait, you've blindfolded yourself. They actually test this honestly — I'll come back to it — by deliberately running it on a trait where they *expect* the mask to be unhelpful, to see what happens.

JORDAN Good. So what was the actual problem motivating this? FAME already exists — why build another one?

ALEX Because even FAME, fast as it is, is still expensive at true genome-wide scale, and the authors wanted something faster and more targeted. Let me give the context. The whole reason these marginal-epistasis methods matter is the unresolved debate about whether non-additive genetics — interactions — meaningfully shape complex traits. Some studies say interactions contribute little; others suspect they hide real signal, including some of the missing heritability we discussed with FAME. You can't settle that debate without methods that actually run on biobank data. The earliest good method, MAPIT, needed terabytes of memory and was simply impossible at this scale. FAME made marginal epistasis runnable. SME's pitch is: we can make it dramatically faster still, by being sparse — and faster matters not just for convenience but because it lets you run many more analyses, test more traits, and use richer external data to guide the search.

JORDAN Put a number on "dramatically faster."

ALEX They benchmarked all three on the same UK Biobank data — about three hundred forty-nine thousand people of European ancestry, roughly five hundred forty thousand variants. On a single processor core, SME ran genome-wide testing ten times faster than FAME and ninety times faster than MAPIT. In wall-clock terms: SME needed about three-point-seven days, FAME about thirty-eight days, MAPIT about three hundred twenty-four days. And it did it inside a manageable memory budget. So roughly an order of magnitude over its closest competitor.

JORDAN How does it get that speed? Is it the same kind of trick FAME used?

ALEX Partly the same family, partly the sparsity. Like FAME, SME uses a variance-components model — splitting trait variation into additive-genetics, interaction, and noise buckets — and it estimates those buckets with randomized approximations rather than exact, back-breaking matrix math. The shared engine there is something called Hutchinson's stochastic trace estimator, which is a fancy name for "estimate a hard matrix quantity by probing it with random vectors instead of computing it exactly," plus the same fast Mailman algorithm FAME used for the genotype math. But SME adds two extra speed sources. One is the masking we discussed — by ignoring most variants, the effective dataset each test touches is much smaller. The other is that SME reuses heavy shared computations across many target variants at once, instead of recomputing from scratch each time. ALEX's pushback: the elegance comes at a price the authors are upfront about — SME's answers are only as trustworthy as the external mask you feed it, so you've moved part of the problem from "compute everything" to "choose the right prior information," which is its own judgment call.

JORDAN So did it work? When they ran it for real, did the focusing pay off?

ALEX Two-part answer, and both parts matter. First, calibration — does it give honest false-positive rates when there's truly no interaction? They simulated trait data with only additive effects, no interactions, and SME correctly stayed quiet, with well-behaved p-values. Notably, they report that FAME's test statistics became *inflated* as sample size grew in their setup, while SME stayed calibrated — so SME isn't just faster, they argue it's better-behaved at large samples. Second, the real application. They scanned four blood-cell traits — mean corpuscular hemoglobin and its cousins, basically measures of red-blood-cell size and hemoglobin content — using a mask built from DNase-hypersensitive sites measured during red-blood-cell development. The logic: interactions relevant to red-cell traits should live in DNA that's active while red cells form.

JORDAN And the genes it found — did they make sense, or were they random?

ALEX They made sense, which is the encouraging part. For mean corpuscular hemoglobin, the strongest interaction signal sat at a variant near a gene called SCGN, secretagogin, with a p-value around one-times-ten-to-the-minus-eleven. Several more significant signals clustered at a gene called CARMIL1, which prior red-cell studies had already implicated. Other hits landed near hemoglobin-related and immune genes. Crucially, many of these were supported by independent published work on red-cell biology — so SME wasn't manufacturing novelty, it was finding interaction signal at genes the field already had reason to believe in. And remember that honesty test I promised: they also ran a trait, hematocrit, where they *expected* a red-cell-development mask to be unhelpful, because hematocrit is governed more by blood oxygen than by that developmental program — and there the focused approach didn't light up. That negative result is reassuring, because it shows the method isn't just rubber-stamping whatever you point it at.

JORDAN Okay. Bring it home to cystic fibrosis. What's one concrete direction this gives me?

ALEX Here's the bridge, and it's actually tighter than FAME's. Cystic fibrosis comes from mutations in one gene, CFTR — the chloride-and-bicarbonate channel — but disease severity varies enormously between people with identical CFTR mutations, and the field explains that with modifier genes. We already have a short list of strongly suspected modifier loci: the chr5p13 region around SLC9A3 and CEP72, the chr11p13 region around EHF and APIP, and a handful of others. SME's whole philosophy — don't scan everything, scan a biologically-motivated subset — fits that situation like a glove. One plain-English version of the direction: instead of asking whether a CFTR variant interacts with the entire genome, deliberately ask whether it interacts with that short, pre-known list of CF modifier regions. The formal one-sentence version: apply a sparse marginal-epistasis scan in which the mask is restricted to established CF lung-function modifier loci, testing whether CFTR-region variants show interaction signal concentrated at those modifiers against a quantitative severity phenotype. Frame this as one possible direction this episode suggests — its appeal is exactly that it slashes the multiple-testing burden a small cohort can't otherwise afford.

JORDAN Given that proposal, what does the paper close for me, and what would I still have to nail down?

ALEX What it closes: it gives you a fast, calibrated tool whose core design assumption — restrict the search using outside knowledge — is the single best fit for a small, prior-rich problem like CF modifiers. Where FAME tests against everything, SME lets you spend your limited statistical budget only where CF biology already points, which is exactly what a small cohort needs. What it leaves open, and these are checks not conclusions. First, the sample-size question from the FAME episode hasn't gone away — SME's hits also came from roughly three hundred fifty thousand people, and the CF cohort is around five thousand; we'd need to honestly assess whether even a focused scan has power at that size, and it might not. Second, the mask's quality is everything — for red cells they had clean developmental DNase maps; we'd need to check whether comparable CF-relevant regulatory maps exist for the airway tissues that matter, or whether the "mask" would just be the modifier-locus list, which is a coarser object. Third, SME was validated on quantitative blood traits, so a CF severity score would need the same scrutiny FAME's traits got, and binary CF complications would need separate treatment. And the standing reminder: none of this presumes you've examined the CF cohort's variant calls or settled a phenotype — whether those modifier-region variants are even cleanly measured there is itself a first-order thing to verify.
