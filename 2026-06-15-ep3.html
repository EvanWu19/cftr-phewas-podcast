Blurb (~150 words)

A clean, classroom-grade demonstration of a CF modifier gene at work. Miller et al. used newborn-screening immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT) as an indirect biomarker of prenatal exocrine pancreatic damage — within CF infants, lower IRT signals worse pancreas destruction. Testing 8 meconium-ileus risk variants across SLC26A9, SLC6A14, and SLC9A3 in 111 Colorado (discovery), 37 Wisconsin, and 80 French CF patients, they found SLC26A9 rs7512462 strongly associated with IRT in Colorado (P=1.16×10⁻³; −49 ng/mL per risk allele; ~9% of variance), replicated in Wisconsin (P=0.03; ~13%), but not France (P=0.76) — most likely an IRT-assay/measurement artifact across 22 French regional labs. Combined North American P=6.77×10⁻⁵. Neither SLC6A14 nor SLC9A3 was associated with the pancreas trait. Proposed direction: a variant-anchored phenome-wide scan of SLC26A9/rs7512462 across the CFF registry to map its full pleiotropic reach. Open warning: phenotype-measurement heterogeneity across centers can erase a real signal.

ALEX Welcome back. Our cystic fibrosis paper today is from 2015, in the Journal of Pediatrics, first author M. R. Miller and colleagues, titled "Variants in Solute Carrier SLC26A9 Modify Prenatal Exocrine Pancreatic Damage in Cystic Fibrosis." It comes out of the International CF Gene Modifier Consortium, the big multi-site collaboration of labs at Hopkins, North Carolina, Case Western, Toronto, and Paris. I picked it because it's a clean, almost classroom-perfect example of a CF "modifier gene" doing its job — and because the gene in question, SLC26A9, is one we've met before, now showing up against a brand-new phenotype.

JORDAN Let's not assume I remember. What's a "modifier gene" in cystic fibrosis?

ALEX Good — let's rebuild it. Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in a single gene, CFTR — the Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane conductance Regulator. CFTR's job is to be a channel that moves chloride, a component of salt, across the surface of cells, which keeps the fluid layer on those surfaces thin and watery. Break CFTR and that fluid turns thick and sticky, clogging the lungs, the pancreas, the gut. But here's the puzzle that launched this whole field: two patients can carry the exact same CFTR mutation and still have very different disease. One's pancreas fails completely; another's holds on longer. The difference comes partly from other genes — "modifier genes" — that nudge the severity up or down without causing the disease themselves. Finding them is the heart of CF genetics.

JORDAN And SLC26A9 is one of these modifiers?

ALEX It is, and it's a natural suspect. SLC26A9 is itself an anion transporter — it moves chloride and bicarbonate across the same cell surface where CFTR sits. It can physically touch CFTR and is influenced by CFTR's activity. So the intuition is: when CFTR is broken, a more-active SLC26A9 might partly pick up the slack, moving some ions and fluid and softening the damage. It's been previously linked to two CF complications — CF-related diabetes and "meconium ileus," which is a newborn intestinal blockage. This paper asks whether it also touches the pancreas.

JORDAN Why the pancreas, and what's "exocrine" mean there?

ALEX The pancreas has two jobs. The "endocrine" part makes insulin. The "exocrine" part makes digestive enzymes and pumps them into the gut. In CF, that exocrine part gets choked by thick secretions — and the damage starts before birth, in utero. The clever move in this paper is how they measured prenatal pancreas damage without ever touching a pancreas.

JORDAN How do you measure damage to an organ you can't see?

ALEX Through a number every newborn-screened baby already has: IRT — immunoreactive trypsinogen. Trypsinogen is a precursor of a digestive enzyme the pancreas makes. In CF newborns, it leaks into the blood and runs high, which is exactly why elevated IRT is the trigger used in newborn screening to flag possible CF. But here's the subtle logic the authors lean on: among CF babies, a lower IRT actually signals worse pancreatic damage — because a more destroyed pancreas has fewer working cells left to produce trypsinogen in the first place. So within this already-high-IRT group, lower means more damage. IRT becomes a proxy biomarker for how badly the pancreas was hit before birth.

JORDAN That's a counterintuitive direction. Lower number, worse disease. Okay — how did they run the study?

ALEX They gathered CF patients from three sites: 111 from Colorado, 37 from Wisconsin, and 80 from France. Everyone was of European descent, carried two severe CFTR mutations known to cause pancreatic insufficiency, and had a newborn-screening IRT value on record. They tested eight specific genetic variants — "SNPs," single-nucleotide polymorphisms, meaning single-letter spots that vary between people — spread across three candidate modifier genes: SLC26A9, plus two others called SLC6A14 and SLC9A3. These eight were chosen because they'd already been tied to that newborn intestinal blockage, meconium ileus. The statistical tool was multiple linear regression — a method that asks whether each variant's dose, zero, one, or two copies, tracks with the IRT number while accounting for things like sex and CFTR genotype. They treated Colorado as the discovery sample and Wisconsin and France as independent replication tests.

JORDAN Let me question one thing — three different countries, are the IRT numbers even comparable?

ALEX Sharp, and it turns out to be the crux of the whole paper. The IRT assays differ by site — different commercial kits, different antibodies, and the authors note there's no certified universal reference standard for IRT. So they deliberately did not pool the raw values; they analyzed each site separately. And as you'll see, that caution was vindicated, because the French data behaved differently.

JORDAN So what did they find?

ALEX In the Colorado discovery sample, three variants in SLC26A9 were associated with IRT, and the standout was one called rs7512462, with a p-value of 1.16 times ten-to-the-minus-three — about one in a thousand by chance. Let me unpack the effect. Each additional copy of the "risk" version of rs7512462 was associated with an IRT lower by 49 nanograms per milliliter — meaning worse pancreatic damage — and that single variant explained about nine percent of the variation in IRT. Nine percent from one spot is a lot in this game. Critically, neither of the other two genes, SLC6A14 and SLC9A3, showed any association with the pancreas measure — their p-values were nowhere near significant.

JORDAN And did rs7512462 hold up in the replication sites?

ALEX This is where it gets instructive. In Wisconsin, yes — rs7512462 replicated, p-value 0.03, same direction, the risk allele tied to lower IRT, explaining about thirteen percent of the variation there. But in France, nothing — p-value 0.76, effectively a flat line, the effect simply wasn't there. When they combined the two North American samples, the signal was strong and clean: p-value 6.77 times ten-to-the-minus-five, explaining about ten percent of IRT variation. They also confirmed the three SLC26A9 variants were tagging one underlying signal, not three separate ones.

JORDAN A skeptic would ask — could that Colorado-and-Wisconsin signal just be some confounder riding along, like sex or which CFTR mutation people carried?

ALEX They checked exactly that, and it's worth seeing because it's the kind of robustness test your own hits will need. They re-ran the rs7512462 association adding one covariate at a time — sex, CFTR genotype, the age the blood was drawn, which IRT assay was used, meconium-ileus status, and the genetic-ancestry principal components — and the variant's effect barely budged. The effect size stayed around minus 51 nanograms per milliliter and the p-value stayed in the same range throughout. They also restricted to just the patients homozygous for F508del — the single most common severe CFTR mutation, meaning two identical broken copies — and the signal held there too. So it's not being carried by mutation type or sex or assay; it's tracking the SLC26A9 variant itself.

JORDAN And mechanistically, why would this transporter touch the pancreas at all?

ALEX The authors point to expression and biology. SLC26A9 is expressed in the human pancreas, it moves chloride and bicarbonate, and it can physically partner with CFTR. Their reading: when CFTR fails, a more-active SLC26A9 provides some compensating ion and fluid movement that protects acinar tissue — and the other two genes, SLC6A14 and SLC9A3, simply aren't expressed the right way in the pancreas, which fits why only SLC26A9 lit up here.

JORDAN So did they answer their own question, or dodge it?

ALEX They answered it, but with a wrinkle they handle well. Their headline question — does SLC26A9 modify prenatal pancreatic damage — gets a confident yes, replicated across two cohorts, with a biologically sensible mechanism. But their secondary question — whether many apical-membrane genes jointly shape this trait, the way they do for other CF complications — got a no; SLC26A9 essentially acted alone here. And the French non-replication they treat honestly: rather than explain it away, they argue it most likely reflects the IRT measurement problem — France used 22 regional labs with varying assays and a different IRT distribution overall — not a failure of the biology. That's a model of how to report a mixed result.

JORDAN How does a 2015 pancreas-and-newborn-screening paper feed your CFTR PheWAS?

ALEX Two ways, and the first is structural. This is a textbook "positive control" for you. SLC26A9 at rs7512462 is one of the most reproduced modifier signals in all of CF genetics — it's turned up for diabetes, for meconium ileus, and here for the pancreas. So when you eventually scan the registry's whole-genome data phenome-wide, this locus is a known landmark. If your pipeline recovers an SLC26A9 signal for a pancreas-related or nutritional phenotype, that's reassurance your machinery works. If it doesn't, that's a flag to debug before trusting any of your novel hits.

JORDAN And the second way?

ALEX The second is the more interesting science. Notice what SLC26A9 is doing across these papers — diabetes, gut obstruction, pancreas. That's pleiotropy: one gene affecting many traits. A PheWAS is built precisely to map pleiotropy, because it tests one variant against the entire menu of outcomes at once. So one possible direction this episode suggests — plainly: take rs7512462 and the SLC26A9 region and, instead of asking about one complication at a time the way each prior paper did, ask the registry the whole question at once — every pancreatic, nutritional, diabetic, gastrointestinal, and respiratory endpoint simultaneously, to draw the full map of what this one modifier touches. Formally: perform a variant-anchored phenome-wide scan of the SLC26A9 locus across the CFF registry phenotype catalog to characterize the complete pleiotropic profile of rs7512462, using the established CFRD, meconium ileus, and pancreatic associations as internal positive controls.
