Blurb

Today's episode pairs a CorEvitas psoriatic arthritis/spondyloarthritis registry poster testing a prevalent new-user design (PNUD) against the conventional active-comparator new-user design (ACNU) with Lund, Richardson, and Stürmer's 2015 review of the historical foundations of ACNU — retrieved in full text. The episode builds the design ladder from the ground up: prevalent-user bias (which combines a depletion-of-susceptibles survivorship problem with a covariate-timing problem), the new-user design that fixes it by anchoring follow-up at treatment initiation, the active comparator that additionally addresses confounding by indication, and finally the prevalent new-user design — Suissa's extension for cases, like this registry, where a clean new-user cohort of switchers with varied prior-treatment histories isn't obtainable. The CorEvitas team (Kerti, Moore-Clingenpeel, McLean, and colleagues) found that PNUD, using a time-conditional propensity score, yielded roughly 25% more matched pairs than standard ACNU (193 vs 154). What this arms Runyu with: fluency in why ACNU is now the default PASS design, a precise mechanical account of what PNUD actually does differently, and a clear-eyed read on what a 25% power gain costs — an estimand that is an average over this registry's particular mixture of treatment-history strata, tested here for feasibility, not for bias.

Plain-English glossary

Active-comparator new-user (ACNU) design — A study design that combines the new-user design with an active comparator: cohorts of new initiators of the index drug and new initiators of a comparator drug prescribed for the same indication are assembled and followed from their respective initiation dates. Regarded as the current standard in pharmacoepidemiology and the default expected by FDA Sentinel and most PASS protocols.（中文：活性对照新使用者设计，将新使用者设计与活性对照相结合：分别以研究药物与同适应症对照药物的新使用起始日为起点建立队列并随访，是当前药物流行病学研究的标准设计。）

Confounding by indication — Bias arising because the reason a drug was prescribed (the clinical indication) is itself related to the outcome's risk, so the drug appears associated with the outcome even absent any causal effect.（中文：适应症混杂，指用药的临床适应症本身与结局风险相关，导致药物与结局出现虚假关联。）

Depletion of susceptibles — Selection bias in which individuals most likely to react badly to a drug leave the observed population early (through discontinuation, adverse events, etc.), so those remaining under observation are a survivorship-filtered, more tolerant subgroup.（中文：易感人群耗竭，指对药物反应较差的个体较早退出被观察人群，剩余观察对象为经过生存筛选、耐受性更好的亚群。）

New-user design — A design that anchors the start of follow-up ("time zero") to the date of treatment initiation, so covariates are measured before exposure begins and the hazard's time-varying shape after initiation can be properly evaluated, rather than pooling prevalent and incident users together.（中文：新使用者设计，将随访起点锚定在治疗开始日期（"时间零点"），确保协变量在暴露之前测量，并能正确评估起始后风险随时间变化的形态。）

Prevalent new-user design (PNUD) — Suissa's extension of the new-user framework for settings where a clean new-user cohort is unobtainable: patients switching to the drug of interest from varying prior-treatment histories are matched, using a time-conditional propensity score, to comparators at the same point in their own treatment course, so prior exposure history is balanced rather than excluded.（中文：流行新使用者设计，是Suissa针对无法获得"干净"新使用者队列情形提出的扩展方法:将具有不同既往治疗史、正转换至目标药物的患者，按时间条件倾向评分与处于同一治疗阶段的对照者匹配，从而平衡而非排除既往暴露史。）

Prevalent-user bias — Bias from enrolling patients who are already on a drug at an unknown point in their treatment course, which both conditions on having survived and tolerated the drug (a form of depletion of susceptibles) and risks measuring covariates that the treatment itself has already altered.（中文：流行使用者偏倚，指纳入已在用药、治疗阶段不明的患者所产生的偏倚，既存在生存/耐受筛选问题，也可能在协变量测量时已受到治疗本身的影响。）

Time-conditional propensity score — A propensity score computed conditional on how far along in a treatment sequence a person is at the point of switching or matching, rather than only on static baseline covariates.（中文：时间条件倾向评分，指在计算倾向评分时，纳入患者在治疗序列中所处阶段这一条件，而非仅基于基线静态协变量。）

JORDAN Let's build it properly, then, from the bottom. Where does the ladder start.

ALEX It starts with the problem you're trying to escape, which is prevalent-user bias. A prevalent user is someone you enrol into your study already on the drug, at some unknown point in their treatment course — could be week two, could be year three. Lund's paper lays out why that's dangerous in two distinct ways. First, sampling prevalent users conditions on survival and on tolerating the drug. If a drug causes an adverse event that's concentrated in the first weeks of use, anyone who had that event is already gone from your prevalent sample by the time you enrol them — you're only looking at the people who made it through. That's depletion of susceptibles again, showing up here as a design flaw rather than a follow-up-time artifact: the susceptible people have already been removed from the population you're sampling, not just from the tail of your follow-up.

JORDAN You said two ways.

ALEX Second, prevalent-user sampling messes with your covariates. If you measure someone's characteristics at the point you happen to enrol them, some of those characteristics may already have been changed by the drug itself — weight, blood pressure, some lab value, whatever the drug affects. So when you adjust for that covariate, you're not adjusting for a pre-treatment confounder, you're adjusting for something the treatment partly caused, which is a different and much worse problem. Lund frames this as a temporality failure — the covariate assessment and the exposure assessment aren't in the right order relative to each other.

JORDAN So the fix is the new-user design.

ALEX Right, and Lund traces this back further than I expected — Feinstein in nineteen seventy-one, writing about what he called chronology bias, arguing that studies need an inception cohort anchored at a real starting point, not a survivor cohort assembled at some arbitrary later date. The new-user design operationalizes that: you only enrol people at the moment they start the drug, which the paper calls time zero, and covariates get measured before that point, so the temporality problem from prevalent-user sampling is gone by construction. Guess, later in the eighties, adds the formal argument for why this matters statistically — hazards for drug side effects are very often not constant over time since initiation, so pooling total exposed time as your denominator, mixing someone's second week on the drug with someone's second year, treats a non-constant hazard as if it were constant, and that's simply the wrong model.

JORDAN What does the new-user design cost you.

ALEX In a mature market, new initiators of a second- or third-line drug are rare, because most patients who'll ever need it have already started it. So your new-user cohort for exactly the drug you care about most — the one people escalate to after other options fail — can be small, and the people who are new users at this late stage of their disease may look systematically different from a first-line new user population. You've fixed the temporality problem and introduced a sample-size and representativeness problem.

JORDAN That's where the active comparator comes in.

ALEX The active comparator answers a different bias — confounding by indication. Lund walks through Kramer's nineteen eighty-seven argument for this almost verbatim: if you compare a drug's users against non-users, some of what you're measuring is just the reason the drug was prescribed in the first place, because the indication itself predicts the outcome. Kramer's line, and I'm reading this straight from the paper because it's worth getting right, is that you should compare the drug's risk against "some other real therapeutic option for patients with the same clinical indication," the same way a trial compares two active treatments rather than treatment against nothing. So the active comparator is chosen to be prescribed for the same indication, at roughly the same point in the treatment pathway, so that whatever channeling remains between the two drugs is narrower than the channeling between drug and no drug at all.

JORDAN And putting those two pieces together is ACNU.

ALEX Active-comparator new-user. New users of the index drug and new users of a comparator drug for the same indication, both anchored at their own initiation date, both measured on covariates before that date. Lund is explicit that this is now the default FDA Sentinel and PASS protocols expect, precisely because it handles confounding by indication at the design stage, before any statistical adjustment, rather than trying to model your way out of it afterward. An inactive or non-user comparator costs you exactly what we just described — indication confounding baked into the reference group from the start.

JORDAN So where does the prevalent new-user design fit, if ACNU is already the standard.

ALEX PNUD is Suissa's answer to the case where a clean new-user cohort genuinely isn't obtainable — exactly the psoriatic arthritis and spondyloarthritis situation her team is in. These are patients who switch onto a given biologic from a range of different prior-treatment histories — some coming off a first biologic, some off a second, some off a non-biologic systemic — so there is no single clean initiation date shared across the cohort the way there is for a true first-ever drug start. PNUD's move is to match each of those switchers to a comparator at the same point in their own treatment course, using a time-conditional propensity score — a propensity score computed conditional on how far along in the treatment sequence someone is when they switch, not just on their static baseline covariates. Instead of excluding people with a complicated prior-treatment history to preserve a clean new-user cohort, you balance that history across the two arms directly.

JORDAN Now the poster. What did her team actually find.

ALEX Applying the prevalent new-user design in that registry gave roughly twenty-five percent more matched pairs than the conventional active-comparator new-user design — a hundred ninety-three versus a hundred fifty-four. That's the whole finding as reported: feasibility, and the size of the matched cohort under each design.

JORDAN Twenty-five percent more pairs doesn't sound dramatic on its own.

ALEX It's not dramatic as a number, but the mechanism it points to is real. In a registry where the eligible population for a clean ACNU comparison is already small, going from a hundred fifty-four to a hundred ninety-three matched pairs can be the difference between a comparison that has enough power to report a hazard ratio with a usable confidence interval and one that doesn't. That's not a cosmetic gain, that's the difference between a reportable finding and a null result you can't interpret, in a PASS where a null result you can't interpret is itself a regulatory problem.

JORDAN What does that gain cost.

ALEX This is where I want to push, and push specifically. The matched population under PNUD is a mixture — people who switched in at different points, with different prior-treatment histories, matched to comparators at those same points. The estimand you get out of that isn't "the effect of this drug versus that drug for a new initiator," it's an average effect over whatever mixture of treatment-history strata happened to populate this registry at this particular time. That's a much harder thing to generalize from. If another registry, or the same registry five years from now, has a different mix of first-line-to-second-line versus second-to-third switchers, the PNUD estimate from this analysis doesn't necessarily transport there, because the estimand itself is partly a property of this sample's composition rather than a fixed biological quantity.
