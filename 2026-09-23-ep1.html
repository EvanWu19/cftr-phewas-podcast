Blurb

This episode pairs a CorEvitas simulation study with the paper that explains why the question it asks matters. The CorEvitas item, an ISPE 2025 oral presented by Austin with Moore-Clingenpeel among the co-authors, is a simulation built from real switching patterns in the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry, comparing three ways of defining an exposure episode — intention-to-treat, overlapping, and once-exposed-always-at-risk restricted to the drug of interest (OEAR-DOI) — for a long-latency outcome. Underneath it sits Samy Suissa's 2008 paper on immortal time bias, retrieved abstract-only here, which formalizes the failure mode these episode definitions are trying to avoid: person-time that is, by construction, outcome-free because the exposure-defining event hadn't happened yet. This episode arms Runyu with the mechanics of immortal time bias — how it enters a cohort, which direction it biases results, and why it connects directly to time-zero alignment — and a grounded read on why OEAR-DOI is the more defensible default for a long-latency outcome, along with the honest limits of a simulation calibrated to one registry's switching intensity.

Plain-English glossary

Exposure episode — The span of time counted as a patient being "exposed" to a drug for analytic purposes; ambiguous exactly when patients switch, stop, or restart treatment.（中文：将患者计为"暴露于某药物"的时间段，在换药、停药或重新用药时定义会变得模糊。）

Immortal time bias — Bias arising from person-time during which, by construction of the exposure definition, the outcome could not have occurred; enters via misclassifying that time as exposed or via excluding it from analysis.（中文：由于暴露定义的构造方式,某段随访时间在设计上不可能发生结局事件,由此产生的偏倚;可能来自将该时间误分类为暴露,或直接将其从分析中剔除。）

Latency — The biological delay between a causal exposure and the outcome becoming clinically detectable.（中文：致病暴露与结局临床可检测之间的生物学延迟。）

Lag (analytic) — The design choice made to accommodate biological latency, typically by shifting the risk window forward or excluding early follow-up time.（中文：为适应生物学潜伏期而做出的分析设计选择,通常表现为将风险窗口后移或剔除早期随访时间。）

Once-exposed-always-at-risk (OEAR) — A design convention in which a patient, once exposed to a drug, remains in that drug's risk set for the rest of follow-up regardless of discontinuation or switching; OEAR-DOI restricts this to the drug of interest while allowing accrual of risk-time for a subsequent drug.（中文：一种设计惯例,患者一旦暴露于某药物,无论后续是否停药或换药,在整个随访期内都留在该药物的风险集合中;OEAR-DOI 将其限定于目标药物,同时允许患者为后续药物累积风险时间。）

Risk window / risk-continuation window — The period following exposure during which an outcome is attributed to that exposure; short for acute events, potentially years for malignancy.（中文：暴露之后可将结局归因于该暴露的时间段;急性事件的窗口很短,恶性肿瘤可能长达数年。）

Time-zero alignment — The requirement that cohort eligibility, exposure assignment, and the start of the risk-time clock all be pinned to the same instant; misalignment is a primary route into immortal time bias.（中文：要求队列纳入资格、暴露分配与风险时间起点必须对齐到同一时刻;三者错位是产生免疫时间偏倚的主要途径。）

ALEX Correct. Latency is the biological delay between a causal exposure and the outcome becoming clinically detectable. Lag is the analytic choice you make to accommodate that delay, usually by shifting the start of the risk window forward by some fixed amount, or by excluding early follow-up time from the analysis entirely.

JORDAN And once-exposed-always-at-risk.

ALEX That's a design convention, not a biological claim. It says once a patient has had qualifying exposure to a drug, they remain in that drug's risk set for the rest of follow-up, even if they stop taking it or switch to something else. It's a conservative choice for long-latency outcomes specifically, and it's exactly what today's CorEvitas item tests.

JORDAN So let's get into what her team actually did.

ALEX This is an ISPE oral, session 6F-05, presented by Austin, with Moore-Clingenpeel among the co-authors, alongside Foster, Harrold, and Litman. ALEX They built eight simulation scenarios, and the switching patterns inside those scenarios were derived from real treatment patterns observed in the CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry, so it's not an arbitrary simulation, it's calibrated to how often psoriasis patients in that registry actually cycle between biologics. JORDAN Now the paper underneath. Why does any of this connect to immortal time.

ALEX Because the reason exposure-episode definition is a live design question at all is that getting it wrong is one of the classic routes into immortal time bias, and Suissa is the paper that formalized why. Immortal time is person-time during follow-up when, by construction of how you defined exposure, the outcome could not possibly have occurred yet. Not because the patient was healthy, but because the exposure-defining event hadn't happened, so that stretch of calendar time is definitionally "immortal" with respect to the analysis.

JORDAN Give me the two ways it gets into a study.

ALEX First, misclassification. You have a patient who eventually starts the drug, and you go back and label them "exposed" from cohort entry, even though the actual first dose came weeks or months later. Every day before that first dose gets counted as exposed time, and by definition the patient survived to actually take the drug, so that span is artificially outcome-free. Second, exclusion. Some designs handle the ambiguity by throwing that early span out of the analysis altogether, which sounds safer but still biases things, because you've selectively removed person-time only from the exposed group, not proportionally from both arms.

JORDAN And the direction of the bias.

ALEX It inflates apparent benefit and deflates apparent harm, because that immortal stretch is outcome-free time credited to the exposed group specifically. The abstract states that the resulting bias in the rate ratio scales with how much immortal time you've accumulated, and that it's worse under a hazard that's decreasing over time, the Weibull case in Suissa's illustration, than under a constant hazard, the exponential case, because with a decreasing hazard the early follow-up you're misclassifying is exactly the period where events would otherwise have been most likely.

JORDAN The abstract also traces this back to the 1970s, to heart transplantation. Why bring that up here.

ALEX Because it's the cleanest version of the mistake, and it explains why the bias is so persistent. The classic error was counting waiting-list time before a transplant as if it were transplanted time, which makes transplantation look protective partly because you can only be on the waiting list if you haven't already died. Suissa's contribution is showing that same structural mistake recurs in pharmacoepidemiology under time-based, event-based, and exposure-based cohort definitions, quantifying how the bias scales, and illustrating it with a cohort of lung cancer patients.

JORDAN Tie that back to time-zero alignment, the thing we hammered on in episode three.

ALEX Immortal time is essentially what you get whenever eligibility, exposure assignment, and the start of the risk-time clock aren't pinned to the same instant. If time zero drifts away from the moment exposure status actually became true, you've opened a gap, and that gap is immortal time waiting to happen, whether you fill it by misclassifying it as exposed or by quietly cutting it out.

JORDAN So where does her team's OEAR-DOI finding sit relative to that.

ALEX It's a related but distinct problem. Their three definitions aren't primarily about time-zero placement, they're about what happens to a patient's risk-time after a switch. But the logic rhymes. ITT keeps attributing follow-up to the first drug even after a patient has moved on, which risks assigning a late outcome to a drug the patient may not have been on for years, understating that drug's true late risk and potentially overstating the new drug's safety by omission. Overlapping avoids that by letting a patient be at risk under two drugs during the bridging period, which is more honest about ambiguity but can double-count person-time. OEAR-DOI keeps a switcher in the original drug's risk set permanently while also letting them start accruing risk-time for the new drug, so for a long-latency outcome specifically, it's the only one of the three that doesn't quietly discard the person-time furthest from switch, which is exactly the person-time a multi-year latency hypothesis depends on.
