Show notes — TCF7L2 / CFRD (Track C, CF modifier)

Episode date: 2026-06-21 · Slot: track_c_modifier

Blurb

Blackman et al. (Diabetologia 2009) is the foundational demonstration that a general-population type-2-diabetes risk gene, TCF7L2 (lead SNP rs7903146, T = risk), is also a modifier of CF-related diabetes (CFRD). Across 998 pancreatic-insufficient CF patients (CF Twin & Sibling family study, 533 trios) plus an 802-patient independent case-control replication (UNC/CWRU), the T allele was over-transmitted to CFRD cases (family p≈0.004, replication p≈0.02, combined p=2×10⁻⁴; HR 1.75/allele, 95% CI 1.3–2.4) and shifted diabetes onset ~7 years earlier, with a population-attributable risk of ~32%. The methodological highlight is a gene × environment interaction: TCF7L2's effect was negligible in patients on systemic glucocorticoids (HR 1.4, ns) but roughly doubled in steroid-free patients (HR 2.9/allele, 95% CI 1.7–4.9) — pooling over steroid status hides the gene. Where it lands: summary-stats colocalization / PheWAS pleiotropy. Plain-English glossary

  • Beta cell / islet — endocrine pancreas cells that secrete insulin; their failure drives both CFRD and the secretory side of type 2 diabetes.
  • CFRD (CF-related diabetes) — diabetes complicating CF; distinct from type 1 and type 2, driven by beta-cell insufficiency on a damaged pancreas.
  • Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (PI) — inability to make enough digestive enzymes; largely CFTR-genotype-determined; a prerequisite for CFRD (~90% of CF patients).
  • FBAT / PBAT — family-based association test software measuring transmission distortion of an allele to affected offspring.
  • Gene × environment (GxE) interaction — a genetic effect whose size depends on an environmental exposure (here, systemic steroids modulate TCF7L2's effect).
  • Population-attributable risk — fraction of cases in the population attributable to a factor (here ~32% for TCF7L2).
  • Population stratification — confounding from ancestry differences in both allele frequency and disease rate; family-based tests are immune to it.
  • rs7903146 — the lead TCF7L2 SNP; T allele is the type-2-diabetes (and here CFRD) risk allele.
  • TCF7L2 (transcription factor 7-like 2) — Wnt-pathway transcription factor; strongest common type-2-diabetes locus; linked to beta-cell proliferation and insulin secretion/incretin response.
  • Transmission distortion — risk allele passed to affected offspring more often than the expected 50%, signaling association.

Methods / tools to lift

  • Family-based association (FBAT/PBAT) — stratification-proof primary test; transmission of risk allele to affected offspring.
  • Two-cohort design — stratification-immune family study + independent unrelated case-control replication; combine weighted by √N.
  • Censored age-of-onset analysis — treating diabetes diagnosis as a time-to-event (cumulative incidence / hazard ratio) rather than a binary, capturing the onset-shift effect.
  • Pre-specified GxE stratification — analyze with and without steroid exposure; the environmental trigger masks the genetic effect when pooled.
  • PI stratification — restrict to pancreatic-insufficient patients (the only group that develops CFRD).

ALEX Today's Track C modifier paper is "A susceptibility gene for type 2 diabetes confers substantial risk for diabetes complicating cystic fibrosis," first author Scott Blackman, senior author Garry Cutting, published in Diabetologia in 2009. It's an older paper, but a foundational one for the modifier catalog, because it's the first clean demonstration that a garden-variety type-2-diabetes risk gene from the general population also tunes the risk of diabetes inside cystic fibrosis. And it does something we care about methodologically — it catches a gene-by-environment interaction in the process.

JORDAN Let's set up the biology, because there are two different diabetes stories tangled together here. Start with the CF side.

ALEX So cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in CFTR, and we usually talk about the lungs, but CF is multisystem. One of its major complications is CF-related diabetes — CFRD — which is its own thing, not quite type 1 and not quite type 2. It affects something like 25 percent of adolescents and 40 to 50 percent of adults with CF, and as patients live longer it's become the most common systemic complication after lung disease. The mechanism centers on the pancreas. Here you need one anatomy term: the pancreas has two jobs. The exocrine part makes digestive enzymes; the endocrine part — the islet cells — makes insulin. In CF, thick secretions damage the pancreas, and about 90 percent of patients have exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, meaning they can't make enough digestive enzymes and need replacement to absorb food.

JORDAN And that exocrine failure is tied to the diabetes risk?

ALEX It's a prerequisite. Pancreatic insufficiency is largely determined by which CFTR mutations you carry, and you essentially only get CFRD if you're pancreatic-insufficient — the endocrine damage rides along with the exocrine damage. But here's the puzzle the paper opens with: CFTR genotype tells you whether you're pancreatic-insufficient, but among the insufficient patients, it does not explain who actually develops diabetes. Prior work from this same group had shown that most of the diabetes risk in CF comes from other genes — modifier genes. So the question becomes: which ones?

JORDAN And their hypothesis was to borrow from ordinary type 2 diabetes.

ALEX Right, and the reasoning is biological, not just convenient. CFRD and type 2 diabetes share features: both rise monotonically with age, both involve a progressive failure of insulin secretion, both show amyloid deposits in the islets. They don't look like type 1, which peaks in childhood and is autoimmune. So if the diseases share mechanism, maybe they share genes. They went to the validated type-2-diabetes genes and picked the single strongest one: TCF7L2 — transcription factor 7-like 2. In the general population, the common variant of TCF7L2 confers the largest per-allele risk of any common type-2 variant, and the lead SNP is rs7903146, where the T allele is the risk allele. Biologically TCF7L2 is implicated in beta-cell function — insulin secretion and islet proliferation — which is exactly the axis that fails in CFRD.

JORDAN So how did they test it, and what's the cohort?

ALEX Two cohorts, which is a strength. The primary one is the CF Twin and Sibling family-based study — they analyzed 998 pancreatic-insufficient CF patients, yielding 533 parent-parent-child trios. The replication is an independent case-control set of 802 unrelated CF patients from the UNC and Case Western Genetic Modifiers Study. They did two things. First, a plain family-history test: does having a relative with type 2 diabetes raise your CFRD risk? Second, the genetics: do you inherit the TCF7L2 risk allele more often if you have CFRD?

JORDAN Why the family-based design specifically? What does the trio structure buy you?

ALEX This is the methodological reason the paper is worth your time. In a family-based association test — they used the FBAT and PBAT software — you ask whether, among heterozygous parents, the risk allele is transmitted to affected children more often than the 50 percent you'd expect by chance. That's transmission distortion. The beauty is that it's immune to population stratification — the confounding where allele frequencies and disease rates both vary by ancestry and create spurious associations. Because you're comparing transmitted versus untransmitted alleles within a family, ancestry is automatically matched. The trade is statistical power, since only heterozygous parents are informative. So they get a stratification-proof primary analysis, then replicate in the unrelated case-control set where stratification could in principle creep in but the signal still has to show up independently.

JORDAN And did the family history and the gene both pan out?

ALEX Both. Family history of type 2 diabetes raised CFRD risk with an odds ratio around 3 — specifically 2.8 in a first-degree relative, and an overall family-history odds ratio of 3.1 with a p-value of 0.0009. And importantly, that wasn't just because diabetic CF patients were older — it held when they matched diabetic to euglycemic patients on age, sex, and pancreatic status. Then the gene: the TCF7L2 T allele was over-transmitted to CFRD patients in the family study with a p around 0.004, replicated in the case-control study at p around 0.02, combined p of 0.0002. In the family cohort the hazard ratio was 1.75 per risk allele, 95 percent confidence interval 1.3 to 2.4, p 0.0006. And the allele didn't just raise risk — it moved the age of onset earlier by about seven years.

JORDAN Before we get to the steroid twist — help me with the TCF7L2 biology itself. Why would a transcription factor be the strongest common diabetes gene?

ALEX TCF7L2 is a transcription factor in the Wnt signaling pathway — meaning its protein product switches other genes on and off rather than doing metabolic work directly. In the pancreatic islet it's tied to beta-cell proliferation and to the machinery of insulin secretion, including the incretin response that amplifies insulin release after a meal. The risk allele is thought to blunt insulin secretion rather than cause insulin resistance — which is the secretory-defect side of diabetes. That's why it ports so cleanly into CF: CFRD is fundamentally a beta-cell-insufficiency disease, an insulin-secretion problem layered on a damaged pancreas, so a variant that further weakens secretion lands on exactly the vulnerable axis. It's not a coincidence that of all the T2D genes, the secretion one is the one that transfers.

JORDAN So the shared mechanism is specifically the failing beta cell, not diabetes-in-general.

ALEX Right, and that mechanistic specificity is part of why the result is credible rather than a fishing-expedition hit.

JORDAN Seven years earlier per the high-risk genotype — that's clinically real, not just statistically real.

ALEX That's the point they drive home with a vivid comparison: a 14-year-old CF patient carrying two high-risk TCF7L2 alleles has roughly the same predicted diabetes risk as a 60-year-old in the general population carrying none — a 46-year shift attributable to CFTR dysfunction and TCF7L2 together. They estimate the population-attributable risk of TCF7L2 in this CF cohort at 32 percent, which is a large slice for a single common variant. Now the part I find most interesting, and most transferable — the gene-by-environment interaction. Systemic glucocorticoids — steroids — are a known non-genetic diabetes trigger. They found TCF7L2's effect essentially vanished in patients on systemic steroids — hazard ratio 1.4, not significant — but in patients not on steroids the effect was much larger, hazard ratio 2.9 per allele, confidence interval 1.7 to 4.9, p around 0.0001.

JORDAN So the environment was masking the gene.

ALEX Exactly, and that's the methodological lesson. Steroids are a strong enough exogenous push toward diabetes that they swamp the genetic signal — when you stratify them out, the remaining variation is more genetically driven, and the TCF7L2 effect roughly doubles. If you'd analyzed everyone pooled, you'd have measured a diluted average effect and underestimated the gene. The interaction isn't a nuisance to adjust away; it's telling you where the genetic signal lives.

JORDAN Let me push on the limits. What's the load-bearing assumption?

ALEX A few, all phrased as things we'd want to check. First, the whole finding is restricted to pancreatic-insufficient CF — the roughly 90 percent who can develop CFRD at all — so it says nothing about the pancreatic-sufficient minority, and any catalog use has to carry that stratification. Second, the phenotyping is uneven: the family cohort had deep longitudinal glucose data, but the replication case-control set used a coarser "physician diagnosis plus insulin" definition, which likely misclassified some cases and would dilute the replication effect — so the two effect sizes aren't directly comparable. Third, this is 2009, single-SNP TaqMan genotyping of one locus chosen a priori — it's a confirmation of a known gene, not a discovery scan, so it tells us TCF7L2 matters but not how it ranks against the rest of the genome. And fourth, the steroid interaction was only testable in the family cohort; it wasn't replicated, so we'd treat it as strong and biologically sensible but not yet independently confirmed.

ALEX That's the natural extension: TCF7L2 has enormous public type-2-diabetes summary statistics, so this locus is a prime candidate to ask whether the CF-diabetes association and the general-population T2D association share a single causal variant — colocalization — versus merely sitting near each other.
