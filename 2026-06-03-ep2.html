Blurb

Using whole-exome sequencing in up to 234,675 UK Biobank participants, this study ran exome-wide rare-variant (MAF < 0.1%) gene-burden tests — collapsing protein-truncating and damaging-missense variants (CADD ≥ 25) — against birth weight, analyzed twice: people's own birth weight (fetal effects) and the birth weight of women's firstborns (maternal effects). It identified nine genes: eight fetal-acting (ACVR1C, IGF1R, INHBE, NOS3, NRK, NYNRIN, PAPPA2, PPARG), one maternal-only (ADAMTS8), with IGF1R and NOS3 acting both ways. The genes resolve into three biological themes — insulin-like growth factor signalling (IGF1R, PAPPA2), adipose-tissue regulation (PPARG, INHBE, ACVR1C), and placental function (NOS3, NRK, ADAMTS8, NYNRIN). Hits were confirmed with a second pipeline (REGENIE), supported by convergent common-variant GWAS/eQTL evidence at four genes, and validated by recovering the known opposing fetal/maternal effects of GCK as a positive control. For Runyu, the transferable idea is the weighted-linear-model trick for separating two correlated genetic actors (fetus vs mother) — and the sobering reminder that the whole result rides on hundreds of thousands of participants, with some signals resting on a handful of carriers.

Plain-English glossary

  • ADAMTS8 / NRK / NYNRIN / NOS3 — genes implicated here in placental development/function (NOS3 also in blood-pressure biology).
  • ACVR1C / INHBE / PPARG — adipose-tissue-regulation genes; rare LoF variants associated with both birth weight and adult favourable fat distribution.
  • BOLT-LMM — a linear mixed-model association tool that accounts for relatedness and population structure; used for the discovery burden tests.
  • CADD — a tool scoring how damaging a variant is predicted to be; missense variants with CADD ≥ 25 were treated as damaging.
  • eQTL (expression quantitative trait locus) — a variant that changes how much a gene is expressed; used here to corroborate causal genes.
  • ExWAS (exome-wide association study) — a genome-wide scan but restricted to exome genes' rare-variant burden.
  • Fetal-acting vs maternal-acting effect — whether a birth-weight effect comes from the baby's own genotype or the mother's genotype acting through the womb.
  • GCK — glucose-sensing gene; its mutations shift birth weight in opposite directions depending on whether mother or fetus carries them; used as a positive control.
  • IGF / IGF1R / PAPPA2 — insulin-like growth factor signalling: IGF1R is the growth-factor receptor; PAPPA2 regulates growth-factor availability.
  • LMM (linear mixed model) — a regression that includes a random term for genetic relatedness to prevent confounding.
  • Mask — a defined set of variants collapsed together for a burden test (e.g. PTV-only; PTV + damaging missense).
  • MAF (minor allele frequency) — fraction of the population carrying the rarer allele; "rare" here is MAF < 0.1%.
  • PTV (protein-truncating variant) — a variant that cuts a protein short (stop-gain, frameshift, essential splice); almost always destructive.
  • REGENIE — a fast rare/common-variant pipeline (also featured in ep1); used here as the independent confirmation tool.
  • WES (whole-exome sequencing) — sequencing only the protein-coding ~2% of the genome.
  • Weighted linear model (WLM) — a method to approximately condition the fetal effect on maternal genotype and vice versa, without mother-child pairs.

Methods to lift

  • Dual-phenotype design — analyze own birth weight (fetal genotype) and offspring birth weight (maternal genotype) separately to capture both genetic actors.
  • Two-mask collapsing — a PTV-only mask and a PTV + CADD≥25-missense mask, run under both BOLT-LMM and REGENIE for cross-tool robustness.
  • Weighted linear model (WLM) — approximately separate fetal vs maternal effects from summary results without needing mother-child pairs.
  • GCK positive control — annotate known-pathogenic variants in a benchmark gene and confirm the method recovers their established (and opposing) effects before trusting novel hits.
  • Convergence check — look ±300 kb of each hit for common-variant GWAS lead SNPs and eQTL concordance to bolster causal-gene claims.
  • Small targeted PheWAS — test each hit against ~10 pre-chosen related traits (adult adiposity, height, blood pressure, T2D) to read off pleiotropy.

Proposed direction (conditional)

Conditional on inspection of CFF WGS variant calls and registry phenotype linkage: for a CF complication potentially shaped by more than one correlated genetic source, apply a conditional rare-variant gene-burden framework (analogous to the WLM separation of fetal vs maternal effects) to test whether the complication is driven by residual variation at/near CFTR versus an independent modifier gene's rare-variant burden.

What to verify first

  • Carrier counts: whether a few-thousand-patient CF cohort yields enough rare-variant carriers per gene for burden testing to be meaningful (some signals here rested on <15 carriers despite n≈200k).
  • Whether the fetal-vs-maternal conditional trick has an honest biological analogue in CF (i.e. genuinely two correlated genetic sources for the chosen phenotype).
  • Whether the conditioning phenotype is cleanly and consistently measured in the registry.
  • Sensitivity to the damaging-variant definition (CADD ≥ 25 is one choice among many) — re-run under an alternate predictor/threshold.
  • That replication/diversity is a known limitation here; any borrowed design needs its own replication plan.

The title is "Rare variant associations with birth weight identify genes involved in adipose tissue regulation, placental function and insulin-like growth factor signalling." We're reading it because yesterday we surveyed the rare-variant toolbox in the abstract — today we watch a team pick up those exact tools and find real genes for a real trait, birth weight, in a half-million-person biobank.

JORDAN Good, because yesterday was all "here's what a burden test is" with no payoff. But before we dive in, give me the vocabulary for this one specifically.

ALEX Let's do it. First, the data type. Yesterday I mentioned whole-genome sequencing — reading all three billion letters. This paper uses something narrower and cheaper: whole exome sequencing, abbreviated WES. The exome is just the roughly two percent of the genome that actually codes for proteins. Sequencing only the exome is much cheaper, and since protein-breaking variants are the easiest to interpret, it's a popular shortcut. Second, the core method: a gene burden test, which we met yesterday — you bundle all the rare, damaging variants in a gene into one per-person tally and ask whether carrying any of them shifts the trait. They call the genome-wide version of this an ExWAS, an exome-wide association study — same idea as a GWAS, but scanning genes' rare-variant burden instead of common single variants.

JORDAN And "rare" and "damaging" — how do they actually decide which variants count?

ALEX Two filters. For rare, they require minor allele frequency below 0.1 percent — minor allele frequency, MAF, being the fraction of people carrying the rarer version. For damaging, they use two categories. One is protein-truncating variants, PTVs — variants that cut the protein short, like a premature stop signal; those are almost always destructive. The other is missense variants — single-letter changes that swap one amino acid for another — but only the ones a prediction tool flags as severe. The tool they use is called CADD, which scores how damaging a variant is likely to be, and they keep missense variants scoring 25 or higher. They build two "masks" — that's the jargon for a defined set of variants you collapse together: one mask of just PTVs, and a broader mask of PTVs plus damaging missense.

JORDAN Okay. And there's something unusual here — birth weight depends on two people, the baby and the mother. Does that complicate things?

ALEX That's the whole intellectual heart of the paper, so hold onto it. Birth weight is shaped by the fetus's own genes and by the mother's genes acting through the womb environment. Classic example: the GCK gene controls glucose sensing. If a mother carries a GCK mutation, her blood sugar runs higher, more glucose crosses the placenta, and the baby makes more insulin and grows bigger — that's a maternal-genotype effect, even if the baby doesn't carry the mutation. But if the baby carries it, the baby senses glucose poorly, makes less insulin, and grows smaller — a fetal-genotype effect. Same gene, opposite directions, depending on who carries it. So this study runs two parallel analyses: one on people's own birth weight, capturing fetal effects, and one on the birth weight of women's firstborn children, capturing maternal effects.

JORDAN That's clever. So what was actually unknown going in?

ALEX Common-variant GWAS had already found more than two hundred regions of the genome associated with birth weight. But "a region" is not "a gene" — these signals usually sit in stretches of DNA near several genes, and you rarely know which gene is the causal one. Rare protein-breaking variants are far more interpretable: if knocking out gene X changes birth weight, gene X is doing something real. So the unknown was: can rare-variant exome analysis name actual causal genes for birth weight, and separate the fetal-acting ones from the maternal-acting ones? That's the question they set out to answer.

JORDAN And why does birth weight even matter beyond the delivery room? It feels like a narrow thing to spend a half-million-person dataset on.

ALEX That's a fair question, and the answer is the reason this trait gets studied so hard. Birth weight is a window into two things at once: how a fetus grew, and what that growth predicts for later life. There's a long-observed epidemiological link — lower birth weight is associated with higher risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease decades later. Type 2 diabetes, T2D, is the common adult form where the body stops responding properly to insulin. One leading explanation is the fetal insulin hypothesis: the same genes that set how much insulin a fetus makes also set adult metabolic risk, so birth weight and adult disease are partly two readouts of one underlying genetic story. If that's true, finding birth-weight genes is really finding levers on lifelong metabolic health — which is exactly what some of their hits turned out to be.

JORDAN So walk me through how they did it — the methods.

ALEX They worked in UK Biobank — the British half-million-person resource — on the cloud platform where that data lives. They derived two phenotypes: a person's own birth weight, available for about 234,000 people with exome data, and the birth weight of a woman's first child, for about 182,000 women. They cleaned hard — excluded multiple births, implausible values under one or over seven kilograms, and averaged repeated reports. Then they ran the gene burden test with a tool called BOLT-LMM — the LMM stands for linear mixed model, a statistical method that tests association while accounting for relatedness and population structure so cousins in the data don't fake a signal. They set a stringent exome-wide significance cutoff, around one in a million, to survive testing every gene. One design choice I'd push on: defining "damaging" leans heavily on a single predictor, CADD, at a hard cutoff of 25 — prediction tools are imperfect, and a different tool or threshold could move borderline genes in or out. To their credit, they confirmed every hit with a second tool, REGENIE — the fast pipeline from yesterday's paper — and a separately-derived phenotype, so the headline genes aren't an artifact of one software choice.

JORDAN And did it work? What did they find?

ALEX Nine genes, and the structure is the satisfying part. Eight genes showed fetal-acting effects — rare damaging variants in the baby's own copy shifting birth weight: ACVR1C, IGF1R, INHBE, NOS3, NRK, NYNRIN, PAPPA2, and PPARG. Three genes showed maternal-acting effects — IGF1R and NOS3, which act both ways, plus ADAMTS8, which was maternal-only. They used a technique called a weighted linear model to approximately separate fetal from maternal effects without needing actual mother-child pairs, and that's how they sorted the nine. Then they sanity-checked against the common-variant GWAS: four of their genes sat right under known GWAS signals, and at two of them the GWAS lead variant was also an eQTL — a variant that changes how much the gene is expressed — pointing the same direction. That convergence of rare variants and common variants on the same genes is strong evidence these are genuinely causal.

JORDAN Do the genes make biological sense, or are they just statistics?

ALEX They cluster into three clean stories, which is rare and reassuring. First, insulin-like growth factor signaling: IGF1R is the receptor that growth factors dock onto to drive fetal growth, and PAPPA2 controls how available those growth factors are — both long-suspected, now nailed by rare variants. Second, fat-tissue regulation: PPARG is the master switch for making fat cells, and INHBE and ACVR1C tune fat storage; intriguingly, the same variants that lowered birth weight also tracked with a healthier adult body-fat distribution, linking how you grew in the womb to your metabolism decades later. Third, the placenta: NOS3, NRK, ADAMTS8, and NYNRIN are all involved in building and running the placenta. And they did a careful negative-control move — they showed their method could recover the known fetal-and-maternal effects of GCK in the expected opposite directions, which validates the whole machine.

JORDAN One thing nags me — some of these genes had only a handful of carriers. How can eleven people give you a real result?

ALEX That's the honest tension of rare-variant work, and worth dwelling on because it's the crux for our project. The power of a burden test doesn't come from many carriers per gene — it comes from the effect being large and clean when it's there. If a protein-truncating variant reliably knocks a gene out, eleven carriers all shifted in the same direction can clear even a one-in-a-million significance bar, because the alternative — eleven unrelated people all drifting the same way by chance — is wildly unlikely. But "can" is doing a lot of work. With eleven carriers you get a real signal only if the effect is strong and the variant annotation is right; weaker effects or noisier genes vanish into nothing. That's why the authors leaned so hard on replication and convergent GWAS evidence — small carrier counts are believable only when several independent lines agree. For us, the lesson is double-edged: rare variants can deliver with few carriers, but only for the biggest effects, and a small cohort sees fewer carriers of everything.

JORDAN So this is a model run, almost. Where does it point for the CF project?

ALEX Here's one concrete direction it suggests. The trick that makes this paper sing is separating two genetic actors whose genotypes are correlated — fetus and mother — using a weighted linear model instead of needing both people sequenced. In plain English for our world: when an outcome is shaped by more than one correlated source of genetics, you can sometimes tease the sources apart statistically. For cystic fibrosis, an analogous move would be to take a CF complication that might be shaped both by variants near CFTR itself and by variants in a modifier gene, and ask which genetic source is really driving it. Formally: in a CF cohort, apply a conditional rare-variant burden framework to disentangle whether a complication phenotype is driven by residual variation at the CFTR locus versus an independent modifier gene's burden.

JORDAN And before trusting that, what does this paper close and what stays open?

ALEX It closes a methodological worry beautifully — it shows that rare-variant burden testing, cross-checked with a second tool and convergent common-variant evidence, can name causal genes you'd believe. It also models the discipline of negative controls and replication. What it leaves open is, again, power. Every number here rides on a cohort of 180,000 to 234,000 people, and even so, some genes had only a handful of carriers — PPARG's strongest signal came from eleven people. The authors themselves flag that their replication sample was smaller and call for confirmation in larger and more ancestrally diverse datasets.
