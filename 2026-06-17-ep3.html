Mekus 2000 — Categories of F508del-homozygous CF twin and sibling pairs with distinct phenotypic characteristics

Blurb. A foundational European CF Twin and Sibling Study paper (Tümmler group, Hannover) that holds CFTR genotype fixed — F508del homozygotes — and uses the monozygous-vs-dizygous twin contrast to show CF disease severity is shaped by an inherited component beyond CFTR itself (identical twins more concordant in overall severity than fraternal pairs, DELTA P=0.05). It defines DELTA, a composite severity index that linearly combines weight-for-height % (nutrition) and FEV1 % predicted (lungs), and an algorithm that sorts genotype-matched pairs into discordant (DIS), concordant-mild (CON+), and concordant-severe (CON−) categories — the modifier-enriched families later genetics would mine. Lands on the Liz / CFFPR phenotype & data-quality priority (method only): build a standardized composite CF-severity index and a genotype-matched discordant-extreme selection routine. (Non-OA 2000 paper; built from the structured abstract, flagged abstract-only on-air; clinical-unit effect sizes are in the paywalled full text and not asserted.)

Plain-English glossary.

  • Concordance / discordance (pair): how alike (concordant) or unalike (discordant) the two members of a twin/sib pair are on a trait.
  • DELTA: the paper's composite severity index — a linear combination of wfh% and FEVPerc capturing overall disease severity and pair discordance in one number.
  • deltaF508 / F508del / p.Phe508del: the most common CF-causing CFTR variant; a 3-bp deletion removing phenylalanine at residue 508, causing a class-II folding defect so the protein is degraded before reaching the cell surface.
  • Dizygous (DZ) twins: fraternal twins — ~50% shared genome (like ordinary sibs) but shared womb/household environment.
  • FEV1 % predicted (FEVPerc): forced expiratory volume in 1 s relative to a healthy same-size person — standard CF pulmonary severity measure.
  • Modifier gene: a gene other than the disease gene (CFTR) that influences how severe the disease is.
  • Monozygous (MZ) twins: identical twins — ~100% shared genome.
  • Pancreatic insufficiency / malabsorption: CF damage to the exocrine pancreas impairing nutrient absorption — why nutritional status (wfh%) is a core severity axis.
  • Weight-for-height % (wfh%): nutritional-status severity measure.

Full citation. Mekus F, Ballmann M, Bronsveld I, Bijman J, Veeze H, Tümmler B. Categories of deltaF508 homozygous cystic fibrosis twin and sibling pairs with distinct phenotypic characteristics. Twin Res. 2000;3(4):277-293. PMID 11463149 · DOI 10.1375/136905200320565256. (No PMCID; not open access.) Cohort: European CF Twin and Sibling Study (NCT00676169).

ALEX Track C today, and we're going back to a foundational one: F. Mekus and colleagues, out of Burkhard Tümmler's group in Hannover, "Categories of deltaF508 homozygous cystic fibrosis twin and sibling pairs with distinct phenotypic characteristics," published in Twin Research in 2000. Heads up that this is a closed-access paper from twenty-five years ago, so I'm working entirely from the structured abstract — I'll flag where a number I'd love to have is in the paywalled full text and not in front of me. We're reading it because this is one of the studies that established, with human data, that something other than CFTR shapes how severe cystic fibrosis turns out to be — which is the entire premise of the modifier catalog.

JORDAN Let me load up on the biology first. Start with deltaF508 — that keeps coming up and I want it precise.

ALEX DeltaF508 is the single most common cystic-fibrosis-causing change in the CFTR gene — in modern nomenclature it's written p.Phe508del, or F508del. It's a three-base-pair deletion that removes a single amino acid, a phenylalanine, at position 508 of the CFTR protein. The consequence is a folding defect: the protein gets made but misfolds, so the cell's quality control degrades it before it ever reaches the cell surface where it's supposed to sit as a chloride channel. It's the classic "class II" defect. The key fact for today is that it's the most frequent CF allele, so you can assemble a sizable group of patients who carry two copies of the exact same broken gene.

JORDAN And that's deliberate — they want everyone in the study to have the same CFTR genotype. Why does that matter so much?

ALEX Because it's how you isolate the modifiers. If two people both carry two copies of F508del, then their CFTR gene is, for practical purposes, identical. So if one of them has gentle disease and the other is severely affected, that difference cannot be coming from CFTR — it has to come from somewhere else: other genes, the environment, or chance. Holding CFTR fixed turns the rest of the genome and the environment into the visible variables. That's the logic of the whole modifier field, and this study built it into the cohort design.

JORDAN Now the twins. What's the actual inference engine in a twin-and-sib study?

ALEX This is the elegant core. Monozygous twins — identical twins — share essentially 100% of their genome. Dizygous twins — fraternal — and ordinary siblings share on average about 50%, just like any two sibs, but critically they're typically raised at the same time in the same household, so they share a lot of environment too. Now here's the move: take pairs where both members have CF and the same F508del-homozygous genotype, and measure how alike or different the two members are. If identical twins are markedly more alike than fraternal twins and sibs, that extra similarity can't be CFTR — it's already held fixed — and it can't be shared environment, because the fraternal pairs share that too. What's left is the rest of the inherited genome. Greater concordance in identical pairs is the fingerprint of a heritable modifier component.

JORDAN Before the numbers — what was actually contested here? Didn't everyone already assume CF severity varied?

ALEX Everyone could see it varied — two patients with the same F508del-homozygous genotype could have wildly different courses. The contested part was why. One camp leaned on environment and chance: access to care, nutrition, infections, the luck of which bug colonizes your lungs and when. The other camp suspected other genes — modifiers — but suspicion isn't evidence, and you couldn't separate the two explanations just by looking at a clinic population, because sicker and healthier patients also differ in environment. What was genuinely unknown was whether any of the variability was inherited beyond CFTR at all. The twin-and-sib design is precisely the instrument that can answer that, because the monozygous-versus-dizygous contrast subtracts shared environment out. So the paper isn't claiming a new gene — it's trying to establish that the heritable-modifier question is even worth asking, which in 2000 was not yet settled.

JORDAN What were they measuring as "severity"? You can't twin-compare a vibe.

ALEX Two clinical axes, the two that matter most for CF prognosis. One is weight-for-height percent — they abbreviate it wfh% — which is a nutritional-status measure; CF causes pancreatic insufficiency and malabsorption, so how well a patient maintains body weight is a core severity signal. The other is FEV percent — FEV1 percent predicted — the fraction of air you can forcibly blow out in one second relative to a healthy person your size, which is the standard pulmonary-function severity measure. Lungs and nutrition: the two organs that drive CF outcomes. Then they did something I like — they built a composite. They define a parameter called DELTA as a linear combination of wfh% and FEVPerc, designed to capture overall disease severity in a single number, and to capture how discordant a pair is on overall severity rather than on just one axis.

JORDAN So what was the cohort, and what did the twin comparison actually show?

ALEX The European CF Twin and Sibling Study assembled, per the abstract, 277 sibling pairs, 12 pairs of dizygous twins, and 29 pairs of monozygous twins — 318 pairs total — and of those, 114 were reported homozygous for F508del. The headline result: monozygous twins had a significantly lower DELTA than dizygous twins, with a P-value of 0.05. Lower DELTA means more concordant — the identical twins were more alike in overall severity than the fraternal pairs. And the authors read that exactly the way the design demands: CF disease severity is modulated by an inherited component in addition to the CFTR gene itself. That's the load-bearing sentence. With CFTR held fixed and environment broadly shared, the extra similarity of identical twins points at modifier genes.

JORDAN A P of 0.05 on 29 versus 12 pairs is... not exactly thunderous. Is that the whole paper?

ALEX You're right to flag it, and no, it isn't — and that's the second contribution, which I think is the more durable one methodologically. They argue that extreme phenotypes are more informative for analyzing any quantitative trait, and they build an algorithm to sort pairs into three categories: DIS, the discordant pairs — two people, same genotype, very different outcomes; CON-plus, concordant and mildly affected; and CON-minus, concordant and severely affected. The abstract says the algorithm reliably discriminated between these categories, and that the selected pairs showed non-overlapping properties for wfh%, FEVPerc, and the intra-pair differences. So the deliverable isn't really the borderline P-value — it's a principled procedure for finding the most informative families: the discordant identical-genotype pairs that are gold for hunting modifiers, and the concordant extremes that anchor the ends of the severity spectrum.

JORDAN Why are the discordant pairs the gold ones — spell that out?

ALEX Because a discordant pair is a controlled experiment that nature already ran. Two people, same CFTR genotype, often shared household, and yet one has mild disease and one severe. Every shared cause is cancelled out by design, so whatever differs between them — a modifier allele one inherited and the other didn't, or a specific exposure — is concentrated and visible. Later modifier studies, including ones using this very same European twin-and-sib cohort, leaned on exactly that enrichment to chase candidate genes. So this paper is partly infrastructure: it defines the phenotype and hand-picks the families that downstream genetics would mine.

JORDAN Bring it to the current work. Where does this land?

ALEX This lands on the Liz, CFFPR phenotype and data-quality priority — and I'll stay strictly on transferable method here, nothing about any particular registry's internals. Two methods transfer. First, the composite severity index: rather than analyzing a nutritional measure and a lung measure separately, build a single standardized DELTA-style index that combines them into one overall-severity number, which gives you more statistical traction and a cleaner phenotype to associate against. Second, the genotype-matched extreme-pair design as a way to define analysis-ready phenotypes. So the buildable artifact: a severity-phenotyping notebook. The MVP — take a longitudinal cohort, compute a standardized composite severity index from a nutritional axis and a pulmonary axis per person, then implement the discordant-extreme selection: within a genotype-matched stratum, rank individuals or related pairs and flag the most discordant and the concordant extremes. Input is longitudinal nutritional and lung-function measures plus genotype labels; output is a per-subject severity index and an extremes/discordance flag. Difficulty small-to-medium — it's feature engineering and ranking, not modeling. It gives you a defensible severity phenotype and a modifier-enriched subgroup in one pass.

JORDAN What would we need to check before trusting any of that?

ALEX Several things, all as "verify first." One: a composite index hides its components — if you collapse nutrition and lung function into one DELTA number, you need to check that the two axes are weighted sensibly and that the composite isn't being driven entirely by one of them, because a patient can be severe on lungs and fine on nutrition. Two: era and treatment confounding — this is a year-2000 cohort, and CF care has changed enormously since, most dramatically with CFTR modulator therapies that shift both weight and FEV1, so any severity index computed across a long time span has to account for changing standard of care or you'll mistake a treatment-era effect for a modifier effect. Three: age and cross-sectional snapshots — FEV1 percent and weight-for-height both move with age and disease progression, so when you measure matters, and a single time point can mislabel someone who's early in a steep decline. Four: power — the twin signal here rested on a few dozen pairs at P equals 0.05, so the heritability claim is suggestive, not settled, and any modern reanalysis would want far more pairs before leaning on it. And to be clear, none of this is about deciding a research design — it's about what you'd pressure-test before building the phenotype.
