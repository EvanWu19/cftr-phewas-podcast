Blurb

Eastman et al. (J Cyst Fibros 2021) re-examine a much-cited claim that the SLC26A9 intron-5 SNP rs7512462 modifies lung-disease severity and ivacaftor response in cystic fibrosis patients carrying G551D-CFTR. Pooling three cohorts (Hopkins Twin & Sibling, UNC modifier study, and the prospective GOAL ivacaftor study — 272 for severity, 141 for drug response), they find a clean null across additive, recessive, and 16-SNP haplotype models (p ≈ 0.25–0.93), with >97% power to replicate the originally reported (large) effects. They argue the SLC26A9 signal is tissue-specific — real for pancreatic/intestinal phenotypes (diabetes, meconium ileus) via an eQTL in pancreas, but not lung — and conclude rs7512462 should not be used to individualize ivacaftor therapy. Where it lands: a tissue-specific colocalization notebook at SLC26A9 (summary-stats colocalization priority).

Plain-English glossary

  • Additive (dose) model — tests a linear trend across 0/1/2 copies of an allele.
  • eQTL — expression quantitative trait locus; a variant statistically linked to the expression level of a gene, often tissue-specific.
  • FEV1 % predicted — forced expiratory volume in 1 second vs. the value expected for a healthy person of the same age/sex/height; the standard lung-function readout.
  • G551D (p.Gly551Asp) — a CFTR gating mutation: channel reaches the surface but won't open properly.
  • Gating mutation — class of CFTR defect where the channel fails to open; the target class for ivacaftor.
  • GOAL study — prospective G551D Observational study, designed to measure ivacaftor response.
  • Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) — expected genotype proportions at a well-behaved locus; a genotyping-quality check.
  • Haplotype — a set of nearby variants inherited together on the same chromosome.
  • Intron — non-coding stretch spliced out of mRNA; an intronic variant can't change protein sequence directly.
  • Ivacaftor — CFTR potentiator drug that holds gating-defective channels open.
  • Linkage disequilibrium (LD) — non-random co-inheritance of nearby variants; high LD = variants travel together.
  • Meconium ileus (MI) — newborn intestinal obstruction, a CF complication and SLC26A9-associated phenotype.
  • Minor allele frequency (MAF) — frequency of the less common allele (here C allele ≈ 0.46).
  • SaKnorm — Survival-adjusted Kulich normalized lung phenotype; an age-comparable CF lung-severity score accounting for mortality attrition.
  • SLC26A9 — a chloride transporter and candidate CF modifier gene.
  • SNP (rs7512462) — single-nucleotide polymorphism in SLC26A9 intron 5.

Methods / tools to lift

  • Replication-power estimation — compute power to replicate a prior reported effect size (here >97%), so a null is a refutation, not an underpowered shrug (R pwr).
  • Baseline-adjusted drug-response modeling — adjust for baseline FEV1 (sicker lungs have more headroom) and restrict baseline windows to de-confound change scores.
  • Mixed-effects pooling — combine related individuals/cohorts with random effects (nlme); also analyze an "unrelated" one-per-family subset.
  • Haplotype testing — phase a panel of locus SNPs (Eagle on the Michigan Imputation Server; VCFtools --IMPUTE) to test haplotypes, not just the index SNP.
  • HWE / MAF QC — HardyWeinberg R package as a genotyping-quality gate.

ALEX Welcome to the modifier-gene track. Today: "SLC26A9 SNP rs7512462 is not associated with lung disease severity or lung function response to ivacaftor in cystic fibrosis patients with G551D-CFTR," Eastman and colleagues from the Cutting and Knowles groups, in the Journal of Cystic Fibrosis, 2021. We have the full author manuscript today, so this is not abstract-only. It's a negative-result paper — a careful failure to replicate a much-cited modifier finding — and those are exactly the ones the catalog needs, because a modifier you cross off is as valuable as one you add.

JORDAN Before the result, prime me. A pile of genetics vocabulary is about to land. Start with the players.

ALEX SLC26A9 is a chloride transporter — it moves chloride ions across the cell membrane, which matters in CF because CF is fundamentally a chloride-and-fluid-transport disease. It's a long-standing candidate modifier: prior genetics tied it to cystic-fibrosis-related diabetes, to meconium ileus — that's a newborn intestinal blockage seen in CF — and to newborn pancreatic enzyme levels. The variant in question, rs7512462, is a SNP — a single-nucleotide polymorphism, one common single-letter difference in the population — sitting in intron 5 of the gene. An intron is a non-coding stretch that's spliced out of the messenger RNA, so this variant doesn't change the protein directly; if it does anything, it's by tweaking how much gene gets made.

JORDAN And the disease side — G551D, gating, ivacaftor?

ALEX G551D is a specific CFTR mutation, written p.Gly551Asp — glycine to aspartate at residue 551. It's a gating mutation: the CFTR channel reaches the cell surface but won't open properly to pass chloride. Ivacaftor is the drug built for exactly that — a potentiator that pries the channel open. It works, but response varies a lot between people even with the same mutation, and that unexplained variation is what makes everyone hunt for genetic modifiers.

JORDAN Two more I keep hearing: SaKnorm, and the modeling words — additive, MAF, Hardy-Weinberg.

ALEX SaKnorm is the Survival-adjusted Kulich normalized lung phenotype — a quantitative CF lung-disease severity score, built so you can compare a 10-year-old and a 40-year-old fairly while accounting for the fact that the sickest patients don't survive to the older ages. The genetics words you mostly know, so I'll be fast: minor allele frequency, MAF, is how common the rarer allele is; an additive or dose model just counts zero, one, or two copies of an allele and tests a linear trend; Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is the expected genotype proportions if a locus is behaving normally — checking it is a standard genotyping-quality gate.

JORDAN So what was the prior claim they're attacking?

ALEX Not attacking — stress-testing. Earlier work proposed that the C allele of rs7512462 marked better baseline lung function and a bigger FEV1 jump on ivacaftor, in people carrying G551D or other gating mutations. FEV1 percent-predicted is the standard lung-function number — forced expiratory volume in one second, expressed against what a healthy person of your age, sex, and height would blow. The catch: that original lung-response association rested on just 24 individuals. And a second study did find an association — but in the opposite direction. So you have a much-cited modifier claim built on tiny samples with contradictory direction, and it was starting to influence talk of individualizing therapy. That's a dangerous combination, and worth a definitive look.

JORDAN Why do tiny-sample modifier claims like the original keep happening — and keep getting cited?

ALEX Two forces. First, the candidate-gene era trained the field to chase plausible biology in small cohorts, and SLC26A9 is biologically plausible — it's a chloride transporter that physically interacts with CFTR, so a lung effect is an easy story to believe. Believable plus a low p-value in 24 people gets published and cited because the mechanism reads clean. Second, in a small genotype-stratified sample, a couple of unusual individuals can drag a mean and produce a significant-looking trend that's really noise. The fix the field landed on is exactly what this paper does: demand replication in independent, adequately powered cohorts before a modifier informs anything clinical. A pretty mechanism is a hypothesis, not evidence.

JORDAN How did they build the definitive look?

ALEX They pooled three well-characterized cohorts — the Twin and Sibling study at Hopkins, the gene-modifier study at UNC, and the prospective GOAL study, which was purpose-built to measure ivacaftor response. For lung-disease severity they had 272 G551D carriers with SaKnorm; for drug response they had 141 GOAL subjects with FEV1 tracked at one, three, and six months on ivacaftor. They used an additive dose model on rs7512462, mixed-effects to handle related individuals and pool cohorts, and adjusted for sex, cohort, and — importantly — CF-related diabetes, since that SNP cluster is tied to diabetes and diabetes tracks with worse lung disease. They also genotyped 16 additional SLC26A9 SNPs to test whole haplotypes, not just the one variant.

JORDAN Push back on something.

ALEX My pushback is on the response phenotype, and they partly pre-empt it. They found baseline FEV1 is inversely related to how much you improve — sicker lungs have more room to gain — so "change in FEV1" is confounded by where you started. They handled it by restricting to baseline FEV1 between 40 and 90 percent-predicted and also testing the full range, which is the right move. But the deeper issue is that a six-month average change is a coarse readout of a drug effect, and if the true modifier effect is small, this phenotype may simply be too blunt to see it. They'd argue — correctly — that the prior claims implied a large effect, and a large effect would not hide. Which sets up the result.

JORDAN Give me the numbers.

ALEX Clean null across the board. Lung-disease severity, 272 carriers: no association, p equal to 0.25 without diabetes as a covariate, 0.28 with it. The 243 unrelated subjects: same story. Drug response in the 71 GOAL subjects in that 40-to-90 baseline window: p of 0.90, and 0.93 with diabetes adjustment — about as null as a p-value gets. Widen to 115 or all 141 subjects: still nothing, p from 0.56 to 0.69. Recessive models, haplotype models across all 16 SNPs, the diabetes-risk versus low-risk haplotype contrast — all null. And the key number that makes this a real refutation rather than an underpowered shrug: they estimate over 97% power to replicate the previously reported associations, because those prior effects were implied to be large. The genotypes also sat in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, MAF around 0.46, so it's not a genotyping artifact.

JORDAN That over-97%-power number — make sure I read it right. Power to detect what, exactly?

ALEX Power to replicate the specific effect the earlier studies reported, not power to detect any effect at all. That's the crucial distinction. Because the original associations came from such small samples, the effect sizes they implied were large — small studies can only reach significance on large apparent effects. So Eastman and colleagues asked: if that large reported effect were real, what's our chance of seeing it in 141 well-characterized subjects? Over 97%. Seeing nothing, at that power, means the originally reported effect almost certainly isn't real at that magnitude. It leaves the door open only for a much smaller effect the original studies couldn't have detected either — which is a very different, and much weaker, claim than the one in circulation. That's how you turn a null into a refutation instead of a shrug.

JORDAN They even reinterpret why the original might have been wrong.

ALEX They do, and it's a nice forensic touch. From the reported allele frequency and the tiny sample, the original ivacaftor-response study should have included roughly three people with the informative CC genotype but appears to show only one — so the original signal may have rested on a near-absent genotype class. That's a cautionary tale about small-n genotype-stratified claims, which you'll appreciate.

JORDAN Is there a general lesson there for how we read the modifier literature?

ALEX Yes — treat any genotype-stratified claim by inspecting the cell counts, not just the p-value. If a reported effect hinges on a genotype class that should barely exist at the stated allele frequency and sample size, the association is fragile by construction, however clean the headline looks. It's a thirty-second sanity check we should run on every candidate before it earns a line in the catalog: does the genotype table have enough people in the informative cell to support the claim at all?

ALEX As open questions, not conclusions.
