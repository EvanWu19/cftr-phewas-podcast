Blurb

PheCAP (Zhang et al., Nature Protocols 2019) is a semi-supervised, code-shipping pipeline (PheCAP R package) for turning noisy EMR data into a trustworthy yes/no phenotype label while spending the minimum amount of expensive gold-standard chart review. It combines structured features (code/med/lab counts) with NLP features auto-curated from public knowledge sources (Wikipedia/Medscape/Merck/Mayo/MedlinePlus → NER → UMLS CUIs → majority voting), then prunes the candidate feature space with SAFE — surrogate-assisted feature extraction — before training a penalized-logistic-regression classifier against ~200 gold labels. Key engineering ideas: silver-standard surrogates do feature selection so gold labels are reserved for the final fit; orthogonalization against surrogates + healthcare-utilization defends against the documentation-volume confounder; output is a per-patient probability you can threshold or carry into association studies. Deployed across 20+ phenotypes, 4 EMR systems, and the VA (~170 sites). Where it lands: Liz / CFFPR phenotype & data quality (method only) and the AI-tooling career lane. Buildable artifact: a portable SAFE phenotyping harness validated on a public labeled EMR set.

Plain-English glossary

  • CUI (Concept Unique Identifier) — a stable ID in UMLS for a clinical concept (e.g., C0003873 = rheumatoid arthritis); lets text mentions map to a standardized vocabulary.
  • Data mart — a static, project-scoped extract of EMR data for the filter-positive patients, for reproducibility and access control.
  • Gold-standard label — chart-reviewed ground truth (a clinician read the record); accurate but expensive and rate-limiting.
  • Healthcare utilization (H) — total notes/codes/visits; a confounder because sicker, more-documented patients score high on everything. Orthogonalized out.
  • NER (named-entity recognition) — identifying clinical terms in free text so they can be mapped to UMLS concepts.
  • Negation handling — excluding negated mentions ("no evidence of X") and family-history mentions so they don't count as patient hits.
  • Phenotyping — classifying whether a patient truly has condition X from EMR data (not the dictionary sense of "phenotype").
  • SAFE (surrogate-assisted feature extraction) — pruning the candidate feature list using silver-standard surrogates before any gold labels are used.
  • Silver-standard / surrogate — a cheap, noisy proxy label (e.g., main diagnosis-code count) available for all patients; extreme values indicate true cases.
  • UMLS (Unified Medical Language System) — a master terminology that unifies ICD, CPT, RxNorm, etc., and assigns each concept a CUI.

Methods / tools to lift

  • Orthogonalization step — residualize features against surrogates + healthcare utilization so only beyond-surrogate signal survives.
  • Auto-built NLP dictionary — NER over public encyclopedia articles → UMLS CUIs → majority voting (≥50% of articles) → note-parsing dictionary.

ALEX Today on Track B we're reading "High-throughput phenotyping with electronic medical record data using a common semi-supervised approach," the PheCAP protocol, lead author Yichi Zhang, senior authors Tianxi Cai and Katherine Liao, published in Nature Protocols in 2019. We picked it because it ships real code — there's a PheCAP R package — and because it solves a problem that sits right at the center of your world: turning messy EMR data into a trustworthy phenotype label, with the smallest possible amount of manual chart review. That label-efficiency idea is the whole reason it's worth your morning.

JORDAN Before the method — you keep saying "phenotyping" like it's obvious. Define it the way it's used here, because I suspect it's narrower than the dictionary.

ALEX It is narrower. In EMR research, a phenotype is just a yes/no answer to "does this patient actually have condition X." Sounds trivial, but it's the hard part. The record doesn't have a clean flag for "rheumatoid arthritis." It has billing codes, which are entered for reimbursement and are noisy — a code for a condition often means "we tested for it," not "they have it." It has medications, labs, and free-text clinical notes. So phenotyping is the task of combining all that into a reliable classification you can build a research cohort on. The terms you'll want from this paper are mostly informatics rather than biology: a gold-standard label is a chart-reviewed truth, a clinician actually read the record and decided. A silver-standard or surrogate is a cheap noisy proxy — like the raw count of a diagnosis code — that you can get for free on millions of patients but can't fully trust.

JORDAN And the expensive thing is the gold standard.

ALEX Brutally expensive. A domain expert reading charts is the rate-limiting step of the entire pipeline — the protocol budgets a couple weeks for it and recommends starting it on day one. Everything clever in PheCAP is in service of needing fewer of those gold labels. That's the design north star.

JORDAN So what was wrong with how people did this before?

ALEX Two failure modes. The dominant approach was rule-based: a human expert hand-writes logic — "at least two RA codes plus a positive anti-CCP lab plus a DMARD prescription equals a case." Simple, but it doesn't scale, because every phenotype needs a new bespoke rule, and code accuracy varies across conditions and institutions, so a rule tuned at one hospital degrades at the next. The other camp went fully unsupervised — methods like PheNorm or the anchor-and-learn approach use only silver-standard labels, no chart review at all. Those scale, but they can't give you a validated classification rule or honest performance numbers, because without any gold labels you literally cannot measure your own positive predictive value. For a cohort that's going to anchor a genetic study, not knowing your misclassification rate is disqualifying.

JORDAN So PheCAP is the middle path — semi-supervised.

ALEX Exactly, and it's worth walking the pipeline because the architecture is the transferable part. It starts with a filter: an expert specifies a loose inclusion rule, usually "at least one ICD code for the condition," which throws out the overwhelming majority of patients who obviously don't have it and leaves a "filter-positive" data mart with a workable prevalence. Then two things happen in parallel. One branch builds features from structured data — counts of codes, meds, labs — and crucially also from the narrative notes using NLP. The other branch sends a random ~200 patients to chart review for gold labels.

JORDAN Tell me about the NLP branch, because "extract features from notes" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

ALEX It is, and this is the piece that I think is genuinely clever. They don't hand-pick note features. They build the candidate feature dictionary automatically from public knowledge sources — Wikipedia, Medscape, the Merck Manual, Mayo Clinic, MedlinePlus. They run named-entity recognition over those articles to pull out clinical terms, map each to a UMLS concept — UMLS is the Unified Medical Language System, a giant terminology that gives every clinical concept a stable Concept Unique Identifier, a CUI. Then a "majority voting" step keeps only the CUIs that appear in at least half the source articles, which strips out incidental mentions. That curated CUI list becomes the dictionary they use to parse the actual patient notes — counting non-negated mentions, so "no evidence of coronary disease" doesn't count as a hit.

JORDAN So the encyclopedia tells them which concepts are worth counting, and then they count those concepts in the real notes.

ALEX Precisely. Now the methodological heart — the step I'd want you to steal — is what they call SAFE, surrogate-assisted feature extraction. You've got a huge candidate feature list and only a couple hundred gold labels, which is a terrible ratio for feature selection. So before touching the gold labels, they prune features using the silver standard. They regress every candidate feature against a surrogate — the main diagnosis-code count, or the main NLP concept count, the things that are extreme only when the patient truly has the disease — across a thousand patients sampled at the surrogate's tails, repeat that two hundred times, and keep features selected in more than half the replications. Only the survivors go into the final supervised model, which is a penalized logistic regression with an adaptive elastic-net penalty trained against the gold labels.

JORDAN I'll push on one thing there. Using a diagnosis-code count as the surrogate to pick features that then predict the disease — isn't that circular?

ALEX It's the right worry, and they have a partial answer. There's an orthogonalization step: each surviving feature is regressed against the surrogates and against healthcare utilization — total note count, total code count — and only the residual is kept. So a feature only earns its place if it carries information about the disease beyond what the surrogate and the patient's overall sickness-slash-documentation volume already explain. Healthcare utilization is the silent confounder in all EMR work — sicker, more-documented patients light up on everything — and orthogonalizing against it is how they keep the model from just learning "this person visits the hospital a lot." It's not magic, but it's a principled defense.

JORDAN Did it actually work, and how do they show it?

ALEX The validation is honestly the modest part on paper, because it's a protocol with a worked example rather than a benchmark sweep. In their coronary-artery-disease example, at a chosen threshold the algorithm hit a positive predictive value of 97 percent at about 64 percent sensitivity and 94 percent specificity — and the point is you can dial that threshold: a genetic study wants high specificity for a clean phenotype, a pharmacovigilance study wants higher sensitivity. The broader claim is deployment scale: PheCAP has been run across more than 20 phenotypes and four EMR systems, plus the VA system spanning around 170 centers. So the evidence for "it works" is less one killer number and more that the same pipeline ports across institutions — which is exactly what a rule-based approach can't do. One honest limitation they state: it's built for phenotypes with a clear clinical definition and prevalence above roughly one percent; rare or fuzzy phenotypes are not where it shines.

JORDAN One more on the NLP side — they lean on cTAKES and NILE and MetaMap. Do those names matter to us or are they interchangeable plumbing?

ALEX Mostly interchangeable plumbing, but worth knowing what they do. cTAKES is Apache's clinical text pipeline — sentence splitting, tokenizing, dictionary lookup against UMLS, and critically a negation module so it knows "denies chest pain" is a negative. NILE and MetaMap are alternative concept-extractors that do the same core job: find a clinical term in free text and map it to a UMLS CUI. The protocol's point is that the choice of extractor is swappable as long as it handles negation and family-history exclusion — you don't want "mother had diabetes" counting as the patient's diabetes. The architecture is engine-agnostic, which is exactly what you'd want in a harness you're going to maintain.

JORDAN So the durable design decision is "map text to standardized concepts and exclude negated and family mentions," not "use this specific library."

ALEX Right, and that abstraction is what makes it portable across institutions that run different NLP stacks.

JORDAN And a key output is a probability, not just a label.

ALEX Right, and that's underrated. Every patient gets a predicted probability of having the phenotype, and you can either threshold it into yes/no or carry the probability itself into a downstream association study, which can improve power. The label isn't a wall, it's a dial.

JORDAN So where does this land for us?

ALEX This lands on the Liz-sponsored phenotype-and-data-quality priority — and I'll be careful to talk only about method, since the registry's internals are yours and your leadership's, not mine to describe. The transferable insight is that you can extract a complex, well-defined phenotype with high accuracy while spending your scarce gold-label budget wisely, by letting cheap surrogates do the feature pruning first. The buildable artifact, and this is the Track B portfolio piece: a portable SAFE phenotyping harness. Implement the surrogate-assisted feature-selection plus orthogonalization plus penalized-logistic-regression chain as a clean, reusable module, and validate it end-to-end on a public labeled EMR dataset — a MIMIC-derived phenotype where chart-style labels already exist — showing it recovers the phenotype with on the order of 100 to 200 gold labels and reporting the full PPV/sensitivity/specificity curve. Input is a codified-plus-NLP feature matrix and a small gold-label set; output is a fitted algorithm plus per-patient probabilities. Difficulty medium — the PheCAP R package gives you a reference implementation to check yourself against, so most of the work is the wrapper, the surrogate definition, and the validation harness.

JORDAN Why build it on a public set rather than going straight to the real thing?

ALEX Because a portfolio tool you can show and a method you can trust both require a known answer to validate against, and a public dataset gives you that without touching anything sensitive. Prove the harness recovers a phenotype where you already know the truth, characterize where it breaks, and only then is it a credible instrument.

JORDAN What would we need to verify before relying on it?

ALEX Several open checks. First, surrogate quality is everything — SAFE only works if the surrogate is genuinely extreme-when-true; for a phenotype where the obvious code is a poor proxy, feature pruning could discard real signal, so we'd test sensitivity to the surrogate choice. Second, the orthogonalization defends against utilization confounding only as well as your utilization measure captures it; we'd want to check that residualizing didn't also strip true signal. Third, transportability is a claim, not a guarantee — a model trained on one note style may not carry to another, so any cross-source use needs its own small gold-label validation rather than assumed reuse. Fourth, the whole thing assumes the phenotype has a definition two reviewers would agree on; the protocol is explicit that the algorithm's ceiling is inter-rater agreement, so for any fuzzy phenotype we'd measure that agreement first. None of those touch registry internals — they're properties of the method we'd establish on neutral ground.

JORDAN That's a good discipline: validate the instrument before you point it at the data that matters.

ALEX Exactly. Tomorrow on Track B we'll likely stay in the reproducible-tooling lane, possibly on pipeline or representation-model code. One-line takeaway: PheCAP's real contribution isn't the classifier — it's spending almost no gold labels by letting cheap surrogates prune the feature space first, and that label-efficiency trick is the part worth packaging into a tool you own.
