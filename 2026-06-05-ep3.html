Blurb

GWAS points at regions, not genes. Dang and colleagues used gene-expression imputation (TWAS/PrediXcan + FUSION) to translate the International CF Gene Modifier Consortium's lung-disease GWAS (n=6,365 CF patients, US/Canada/France, KNoRMA lung-function phenotype) into specific, prioritized, direction-annotated candidate genes. Building prediction models from 48 GTEx tissues plus two CF-relevant tissues (lymphoblastoid cell lines and nasal epithelial biopsies) and whole blood — 51 tissues total — they imputed each patient's gene expression from DNA and tested it against severity. Result: 379 candidate modifier genes, narrowed to 52 cross-method consensus genes, 28 of which sit within ~1 Mb of the 5 known GWAS peaks. Most consistent: HLA Class II (HLA-DRB1) on chr6 and CEP72/EXOC3/TPPP at the chr5 peak. New leads include MET (~700 kb upstream of CFTR on chr7), a chr16 CHP2/PRKCB signal the GWAS had missed, plus TAPT1 and HEATR2/DNAAF5. Proposed direction (conditional): adopt the 52-gene consensus list as a prior set for a CF PheWAS, mapping modifier pleiotropy beyond lung function — with a cohort-overlap and tissue caveat attached.

  • TWAS via PrediXcan + FUSION — impute genetically-regulated expression from DNA, then associate with phenotype; run both engines to cross-check.

ALEX Our third paper today comes from inside the cystic fibrosis world itself. It's a 2020 study in the journal PLoS One by Hong Dang and colleagues, titled "Mining GWAS and eQTL data for CF lung disease modifiers by gene expression imputation." After two methods papers about gene interactions, this is a change of gear: a real gene-hunting study on the actual CF cohort whose data sits at the center of your project. We're reading it because it hands you something concrete — a prioritized list of candidate genes that tune CF lung disease severity, built from the same consortium data you'd be working with.

JORDAN Let's do the vocabulary first, because there are two acronyms in the title alone. What's a GWAS, and what on earth is an eQTL?

ALEX Let's build both. A GWAS — genome-wide association study — scans hundreds of thousands of spots where people's DNA differs, each spot called a SNP, a single-nucleotide polymorphism, and asks which spots line up with a trait. In CF, the trait here is lung disease severity. The problem GWAS runs into: it usually points at a *region* of the genome, not a specific gene, and it tells you "something here matters" without telling you *which* gene or *why*. Now the second term. An eQTL — expression quantitative trait locus — is a DNA spot where your genetic variant influences how much a nearby gene gets switched on. "Expression" means how actively a gene is being read out into its product. So an eQTL is a variant that turns a gene's volume knob up or down. The big idea of this paper is to combine the two: use eQTL knowledge to translate a vague GWAS region into a specific statement like "this variant matters *because* it changes the expression of this particular gene."

JORDAN So instead of "there's a signal somewhere around here," you get "the signal is this gene, dialed up or down." How do they actually do that translation?

ALEX Through a technique called TWAS — transcriptome-wide association study. The transcriptome is the full set of genes being expressed in a tissue. TWAS works in two steps. Step one: in a reference dataset where you have both people's DNA and their measured gene expression, you build a predictive model — a formula that says "given someone's variants near gene X, here's how much gene X is probably expressed." Step two: you take your CF patients, who have DNA but maybe not direct expression measurements, and you *impute* — statistically predict — each person's gene expression from their DNA using that formula. Then you ask: does the predicted expression of gene X track with lung disease severity across patients? If yes, gene X becomes a candidate modifier. The clever part is you've converted a question about anonymous DNA variants into a question about specific, named genes.

JORDAN Where do they get the reference data to build those prediction formulas?

ALEX Several sources, and this is worth naming. The big public one is GTEx — the Genotype-Tissue Expression project — which measured gene expression across dozens of human tissues in hundreds of donors. They used GTEx models for forty-eight tissues. But they also built CF-specific models from two tissue types that actually matter for this disease: EBV-transformed lymphoblastoid cell lines — those are immune cells from patients, grown in the lab, abbreviated LCLs — and nasal epithelial biopsies, the lining cells of the nose, which are a proxy for the airway cells where CF does its damage. Plus a whole-blood dataset. Altogether fifty-one tissues' worth of prediction models. The tooling they used is called PrediXcan for one arm and FUSION for a second arm — both are standard TWAS engines, just two different implementations, and running both lets them cross-check.

JORDAN Before the results — tell me about the cohort, since this is supposedly the data I care about.

ALEX This is the heart of why this paper is on your list. The cohort is the International Cystic Fibrosis Gene Modifier Consortium: five cohorts, about six thousand three hundred sixty-five CF patients, more than ninety percent European ancestry, drawn from the US, Canada, and France. The severity phenotype is a standardized lung-function score with an unwieldy name — KNoRMA, which stands for Kulich Normal Residual Mortality Adjusted average. Plain English: it's a carefully age- and survival-adjusted version of "how good is this patient's lung function compared to other CF patients," built on FEV1, the standard breathing-test number. One design choice I'd push on: they relied on imputed expression models built largely from GTEx tissues that are *not* airway — and even their CF-specific tissues, LCLs and nasal cells, are imperfect stand-ins for the deep lung. So every hit carries a question of whether the right tissue was modeled. They're aware of this and use many tissues partly to hedge it, but it's a real limitation.

JORDAN Fair. So what did they find?

ALEX They ran the imputation across all fifty-one tissues, combined the results with meta-analysis, and landed on three hundred seventy-nine candidate modifier genes overall. Then they tightened the screws: genes that agreed across multiple independent analysis methods gave a high-confidence set of fifty-two consensus modifier genes. And here's a telling number — twenty-eight of those fifty-two sit within about a million DNA bases of the five known CF lung-disease GWAS peaks. So the method largely rediscovers the established signals, which is reassuring, and then adds new ones. At the known peaks, the most consistent genes were the HLA Class II immune genes on chromosome 6 — particularly HLA-DRB1 — and, at the chromosome 5 peak, three genes: CEP72, EXOC3, and TPPP. EXOC3 is part of the exocyst, machinery that helps deliver proteins to the cell surface; CEP72 is a centrosome protein; TPPP is involved in microtubules. At the chromosome 11 peak, the usual suspects EHF and APIP showed up, along with PDHX.

JORDAN And the genuinely new ones — the genes *not* sitting on top of old GWAS peaks?

ALEX That's the exciting part, and it's where TWAS earns its keep, because it can find a gene whose individual variants never crossed the GWAS finish line but whose *combined* expression signal does. A few stood out. MET — a gene encoding a receptor tyrosine kinase, a cell-surface signaling receptor — popped up sitting about seven hundred thousand DNA bases upstream of CFTR itself on chromosome 7. That's provocative: a modifier candidate practically next door to the disease gene. They also flagged TAPT1 on chromosome 4 and a gene they call HEATR2, also known as DNAAF5, involved in cilia — the little hairs that sweep mucus out of airways, which is exactly the broken process in CF. And strikingly, they surfaced a brand-new signal on chromosome 16, at genes CHP2 and PRKCB, that the original GWAS had not reported because it didn't survive the GWAS multiple-testing penalty — the expression-imputation approach brought it back into view. When they checked the overall agreement between the GWAS signal and the imputed-expression signal across more than twenty-five thousand genes, the two were significantly correlated, which tells you the imputation isn't drifting off into noise.

JORDAN Did they answer the question they set out to answer?

ALEX Partially, and they're honest about which part. The question was: can we move from vague GWAS regions to specific, prioritized, mechanistically-interpretable modifier genes? On prioritization, yes — they deliver a ranked, cross-method-validated list and even predict the *direction* of each gene's effect, whether higher expression means milder or worse disease. On the deeper question — proving any of these genes is genuinely causal rather than just correlated — no, and they don't claim to. They also raise a careful caveat of their own: imputed expression can be confounded by allele bias, a technical artifact where the prediction reflects which DNA allele you carry rather than true biological expression. So they frame the output as hypothesis-generating: a prioritized hunting list, not a verified mechanism.

Your project is a phenome-wide study — a PheWAS, scanning CFTR-related variation against many phenotypes at once. One plain-English version of the direction: take this paper's fifty-two consensus modifier genes as a pre-specified target list, and instead of asking only "do they affect lung function," ask "across the full registry phenome, what *else* do these genes associate with?" The formal one-sentence version: using the consensus modifier genes from this study as a prior gene set, test their genetically-imputed expression against the breadth of registry phenotypes in the CF cohort to map modifier pleiotropy beyond lung function. Frame this as one possible direction this episode suggests — its appeal is that it inherits a validated, direction-annotated gene list rather than starting a scan from scratch.
