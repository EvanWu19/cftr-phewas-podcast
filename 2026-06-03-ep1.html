Blurb

This systematic review (PRISMA-registered) surveys the software landscape for rare-variant analysis, narrowing 1,238 candidate papers down to 17 open-source pipelines and scoring each on functionality and usability — trait types, statistical tests, ways of defining testing units, variant selection and weighting, confounder adjustment, file formats, documentation quality, and maintenance activity. The verdict: no single pipeline does everything. The authors instead offer a matched-tool guide — STAARpipeline for newcomers and standard work (built-in testing-unit definitions), REGENIE for biobank-scale speed and active maintenance, Ravages and OrdinalGWAS for categorical/ordinal traits — and warn that seven of the 17 tools are stale (>3 years without update) and two are formally abandoned. For Runyu, the proposed direction is to pick one actively-maintained, recessive-model-capable pipeline and run a genome-wide rare-variant gene-burden scan against a CF severity phenotype. The open question the paper cannot answer: whether such a scan has any power at the few-thousand-patient scale of a CF cohort, since these tools were built for cohorts ten to a hundred times larger.

Plain-English glossary

  • Burden test — a rare-variant test that collapses all qualifying variants in a unit into one per-person score, then tests that score against the trait; powerful when variants act in the same direction, weak when they oppose.
  • Combination test (e.g. SKAT-O) — blends a burden test and a variance-component test to stay robust whether variants act in the same or opposing directions.
  • Common variant — a DNA variant carried by a sizeable fraction of people (typically MAF > 1%).
  • GWAS (Genome-Wide Association Study) — a scan of (usually common) variants across the genome for association with a trait.
  • Inheritance model (additive / dominant / recessive) — the assumed relationship between genotype and effect; CF biology is recessive, and only some pipelines support a recessive model.
  • MAF (minor allele frequency) — the fraction of chromosomes in a population carrying the rarer version of a variant; "rare" usually means MAF < 1%, often < 0.1%.
  • Pipeline — the end-to-end software that takes genetic + phenotype data through to a results table.
  • pLoF (putative loss-of-function) — a variant predicted to knock out a gene's function (e.g. stop-gain, frameshift, essential splice).
  • PRISMA — a reporting standard/checklist that makes a systematic review's search and exclusion steps reproducible.
  • Promoter / enhancer — regulatory DNA: a promoter is a gene's local on-switch; an enhancer is a more distant booster.
  • Rare variant — a DNA variant carried by very few people; individually hard to test, so aggregated into testing units.
  • RVAAT (rare-variant aggregate association test) — any test that groups multiple rare variants and tests them jointly.
  • SKAT (sequence kernel association test) — a variance-component RVAAT that tests the spread of variant effects, tolerant of opposing directions.
  • Sliding window — a testing-unit definition that marches a fixed- or variable-size window along the genome instead of using genes.
  • Testing unit — the group of rare variants tested together (a gene, a regulatory element, or a window).
  • UK Biobank — a British research resource with deep genetic + health data on ~500,000 volunteers.
  • Variance-component test — see SKAT.
  • WGS (whole-genome sequencing) — reading a person's entire DNA sequence, not just selected sites.

Your DNA is a three-billion-letter sequence, and at any given position people can differ by a single letter — those one-letter differences are called variants, or sometimes SNPs, which stands for single nucleotide polymorphisms. A variant is "common" if a lot of people carry it — say more than one percent of the population — and "rare" if very few do. Minor allele frequency, abbreviated MAF, is just the fraction of people carrying the rarer version; a MAF below one percent, often below a tenth of a percent, is what people mean by rare.

JORDAN And why do we care about the rare ones specifically? Aren't common variants easier to study?

ALEX They are easier, and that's the catch. For twenty years the workhorse has been the GWAS — Genome-Wide Association Study — which scans common variants across the genome looking for ones that show up more often in people with a disease. But common variants individually tend to have tiny effects. Rare variants are the opposite: each is carried by few people, but when one does something, it can do something big — a strong push toward disease or a large change in a measurable trait. The problem is statistical. If only ten people in your whole cohort carry a given rare variant, you can't say anything reliable about it one at a time. So the field invented a trick.

JORDAN Which is?

ALEX You stop testing variants one at a time and you group them. You take, say, all the rare variants that fall inside a single gene, bundle them into one "testing unit," and ask: do people who carry any rare variant in this gene differ from people who don't? That bundling is the heart of rare variant analysis, and the family of statistical tests that do it are called rare variant aggregate association tests. The paper abbreviates them RVAATs. Now, a pipeline — that's just the software that walks you from your raw genetic data all the way to a table of results, handling every messy step in between. This review is a tour of seventeen such pipelines.

JORDAN And those RVAATs — are they all the same kind of test under the hood?

ALEX No, and this is worth thirty seconds because the names recur everywhere in the field. There are three broad families. Burden tests collapse all the rare variants in a unit into a single score — essentially "how many damaging variants does this person carry" — and test that score against the trait. They're powerful when most variants push the same direction, but they cancel out if some raise risk and some lower it. The second family, variance-component tests, the famous one being SKAT — that stands for sequence kernel association test — instead test whether the spread of effects in the unit is bigger than chance, so they cope fine with variants pushing in opposite directions. The third family, combination tests like SKAT-O, literally blend a burden test and a variance-component test to hedge. The review found that thirteen of the tools offer at least one burden test, almost all offer a variance-component test, and one tool, RVTESTS, offers seventeen different test statistics by itself.

JORDAN Seventeen tests in one tool. Is more always better?

ALEX That's the trap, and the paper is sharp about it. RVTESTS gives you a huge menu of tests but very limited built-in ways to define the testing units beyond genes — so it's deep in one dimension, shallow in another. STAARpipeline is the mirror image: fewer tests, but rich built-in options for defining units and selecting variants. "More features" only matters if they're the features your question needs.

JORDAN So before we even get to results — what was unknown here? Why does anyone need a review of software?

ALEX Because the landscape is a swamp, and no map existed. Whole-genome sequencing has exploded — early studies had a thousand people, and now a single biobank like UK Biobank has whole-genome data on over five hundred thousand. UK Biobank, by the way, is a large British research resource with deep genetic and health data on half a million volunteers; it's one of the reference datasets this whole podcast keeps circling. With that much data and that many rare variants, dozens of analysis tools have appeared, each making different choices. And the choices multiply fast. How do you define the testing unit — by gene, or by a sliding window of nearby variants? Which variants do you keep — only the ones predicted to break a protein? How do you weight them — do rarer ones count more? Which statistical test do you run? Every one of those is a fork in the road, and every tool supports a different subset of the forks. The authors point out that earlier reviews focused on the statistics — the math of the tests — but nobody had systematically compared the actual software on whether it's usable, maintained, and able to scale. That's the gap they set out to fill.

JORDAN So how did they do it? You can't exactly "experiment" on a review.

ALEX Right, it's a systematic review, which is a formal, rules-based literature survey — they followed a published standard called PRISMA, which is basically a checklist that forces you to document exactly how you searched and what you excluded so the whole thing is reproducible. They searched two databases, MEDLINE and Google Scholar, up to late 2023, for open-source tools that take genetic data, group rare variants into testing units, and do association testing. Out of more than twelve hundred candidate papers they narrowed to seventeen pipelines. Then — and this is the careful part — they scored each one against a long checklist of features: what kinds of traits it handles, which statistical tests, which file formats, whether it adjusts for confounders, how good the documentation is, how actively it's maintained. They even emailed the tool authors to verify their reading. One design choice I'd push on, though: a single person did the initial screening and feature extraction, with a second person reviewing. For a field this fiddly, that's a thin margin — one reader can easily miss a buried feature, and they admit some functionality might exist in the code but not the documentation they read.

JORDAN Before the findings — you keep saying "define the testing unit." Is the gene not just the obvious unit?

ALEX It's the obvious one, but not the only one, and the choices matter. You can group by gene, yes. You can group by other functional pieces of the genome — promoters, which are the on-switches in front of genes, or enhancers, which are more distant boosters. Or you can abandon biology entirely and use a sliding window — just march a fixed-size window of, say, a few thousand letters along the chromosome and test each window. Sliding windows shine in the parts of the genome that don't code for proteins and aren't well annotated, which is most of it. The review found eleven tools can group by gene, fewer handle sliding windows, and only one — STAARpipeline again — supports a variable-size sliding window. Then there's variant selection within the unit: do you keep every rare variant, or only the ones predicted to truly damage the protein — what the field calls putative loss-of-function, abbreviated pLoF, meaning the variant likely knocks the gene out? And weighting: most tools let you give rarer variants more weight, on the theory that rarer means bigger effect. Each of these knobs is a place tools differ.

ALEX The honest headline is: no single pipeline does everything. Not one of the seventeen could handle all five trait types, offer a rich menu of tests, and give you flexibility in defining testing units, all at once. So instead of a winner they give you a matched-tool guide. For a newcomer running standard analyses, they recommend STAARpipeline, because it has built-in definitions for testing units — you basically hand it your genotype and phenotype files and go. For raw speed at biobank scale, they point to REGENIE, written in C++, heavily parallelized, actively maintained, well documented — it came out on top on nearly every "is this software alive and cared for" metric they tracked. For unusual trait types they name specialists: Ravages for unordered categorical traits, OrdinalGWAS for ordered ones. And they're blunt about the graveyard — seven of the seventeen tools, including some well-known names, hadn't been updated in more than three years, and two were confirmed by their own authors to be abandoned. A tool that doesn't run on today's data formats is worse than no tool, because you'll sink days finding that out.
