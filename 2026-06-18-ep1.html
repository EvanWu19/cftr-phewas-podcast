Show notes — AlphaMissense: proteome-wide missense effect prediction

Track A — CF/CFTR variant interpretation & harmonization. Flagged abstract-only: full text is paywalled at Science; this episode is built from the published abstract plus publicly released facts about the model.

Blurb

AlphaMissense (Cheng et al., Science 2023) adapts the AlphaFold structure model into a pathogenicity predictor for every possible missense variant in the human proteome — about 71 million single-amino-acid substitutions. The trick that makes it matter: it does not train on clinical pathogenicity labels (which would risk circularity with the very databases used to evaluate it). Instead it fine-tunes AlphaFold's structural representation on human and primate population frequency data, letting natural selection serve as a weak label for "what's tolerated." It reports state-of-the-art accuracy across clinical and deep-mutational-scanning benchmarks, classifies 89% of possible human missense variants as likely benign or likely pathogenic, and even predicts gene cell-essentiality from average per-gene score. Where it lands for us: a buildable CFTR VUS discordance table for the Karen Raraigh variant-interpretation collaboration.

Plain-English glossary

  • Cell essentiality — whether a gene is required for a cell to survive/proliferate; here, inferred from the average pathogenicity score across all of a gene's possible variants.
  • Deep mutational scanning (DMS) — an experimental assay that measures the functional effect of thousands of mutations in a protein at once; used as a non-clinical benchmark for variant predictors.
  • Evolutionary conservation — positions in a protein kept constant across species over evolutionary time are inferred to be functionally important, so changes there are more likely damaging.
  • gnomAD — large reference database of human population variant frequencies.
  • HGVS nomenclature — the standard syntax for naming a variant at DNA/protein level (e.g., p.Gly551Asp); needed to line a score up with the right change.
  • Missense variant — a single-nucleotide change that swaps one amino acid for another; the protein is still made but with one residue altered.
  • Pathogenicity score — continuous 0–1 output; thresholds bin it into likely benign / ambiguous / likely pathogenic.
  • Proteome-wide — covering every protein and every possible substitution, not a single gene.
  • Purifying (negative) selection — selection that removes harmful variants from a population, keeping them rare; the basis for the population-frequency proxy.
  • Structural context — where in the folded 3D protein a residue sits (buried core, binding pocket, surface); supplied by the AlphaFold backbone.
  • Variant of uncertain significance (VUS) — a variant with insufficient evidence to classify as benign or pathogenic.

Methods / tools to lift

  • Weak-label training via population frequency — use human + primate allele frequency as a proxy target for "what selection tolerates," avoiding circular dependence on clinical labels.
  • Structure-conditioned variant scoring — reuse a pretrained AlphaFold-style structural representation so the model "sees" the residue's 3D environment.
  • Per-gene aggregation — average per-variant scores to a gene-level signal (here, cell-essentiality), recovering power that variant-count statistics lack for short genes.
  • Precomputed lookup table — ship all ~71M predictions as a static resource so downstream users just join, not run a model.

Where it lands + buildable artifact

Serves: Karen Raraigh / CFTR variant interpretation.

MVP (paste-ready): CFTR variant evidence table — notebook that joins AlphaMissense precomputed CFTR missense scores (by genomic coordinate + protein HGVS) against CFTR2 classifications, ClinVar assertions, and gnomAD frequency, then outputs a ranked list of discordances (AlphaMissense-pathogenic but CFTR2-VUS, and vice versa). Input: AlphaMissense CFTR slice + CFTR2 export. Difficulty: S.

What to verify first

  • CFTR-specific calibration — confirm scores cleanly separate already-adjudicated CFTR pathogenic vs benign variants before trusting them on VUS; the shipped global threshold may not be CF-optimal.
  • Recessive / mild-effect blind spot — population-frequency proxy is weakest for recessive, late-onset, and residual-function variants — precisely the clinically interesting CFTR middle.
  • Missense only — no splice-variant coverage; CFTR has major splice alleles, so AlphaMissense is one column, never the full call.
  • Ancestry bias — frequency databases are Europe-heavy; check behavior on non-European variants.
  • Build / HGVS mapping — pin genome build and protein-change mapping so every score aligns with the correct variant.

For a lot of genes, the majority of observed missense variants are VUS. The abstract's headline is that AlphaMissense classifies 89% of all possible human missense variants as likely benign or likely pathogenic — it's trying to drain the VUS swamp.

JORDAN Two more. "Structural context" and "evolutionary conservation."

ALEX Evolutionary conservation is the old, powerful idea: if a position in a protein has stayed the same across millions of years of evolution — humans, mice, fish, all carrying the same amino acid there — then that position probably matters, and changing it is probably bad. Structural context is what AlphaFold brought: the model has a learned sense of the protein's three-dimensional shape, so it can ask whether a swap is buried in the protein core, sitting in a binding pocket, or harmlessly out on the surface. AlphaMissense fuses both signals.

JORDAN Okay. So what was actually unsolved before this? We've had conservation-based predictors for twenty years.

ALEX We have. SIFT, PolyPhen, then the ensemble methods like REVEL, then deep models like the EVE family and the CADD framework. The problem is twofold. First, accuracy — they disagree with each other and with functional data more than you'd want, especially on the hard, in-between variants that actually end up as VUS. Second, and this is subtle, calibration and circularity. Many predictors were trained on known pathogenic and benign variants from databases like ClinVar. But those databases are themselves partly built from predictor output and from the same conservation logic. So when you test the predictor on ClinVar, you're partly grading it on its own homework. The field has been openly worried that reported accuracies are inflated by this leakage.

JORDAN Before the fix — how bad is the circularity in practice? Is this a theoretical worry or a measured one?

ALEX It's measured, and it's the reason the field got religion about benchmark hygiene. If your predictor was trained on ClinVar pathogenic and benign labels, and then you evaluate on a held-out slice of ClinVar, you'll report a gorgeous accuracy that partly reflects the predictor having seen variants from the same genes, sometimes the same functional logic, in training. People started insisting on benchmarks the model could not have memorized — independent clinical cohorts, and especially deep mutational scanning data, which is generated in a wet lab with no reference to any predictor. A model that only looks good on its cousins of the training set, and worse on the wet-lab data, is overfit to the label-generation process. So "did you train on clinical labels at all" became a first-order question, not a footnote.

JORDAN So how do you escape your own training labels?

ALEX That's the clever move here, and it's the part of the abstract I'd underline. AlphaMissense does not train on clinical pathogenicity labels at all. It starts from AlphaFold's structural representation and fine-tunes on human and primate population frequency data — essentially, which variants actually show up in living populations and at what frequency.

JORDAN Why does population frequency stand in for pathogenicity?

ALEX Because natural selection already ran the experiment. A variant that's truly damaging to a gene that matters tends to get purged — carriers have fewer descendants, so the variant stays rare or absent. A variant that's harmless drifts up to common frequencies. Primates extend the same logic across species: an amino acid that's tolerated across primates is probably fine in humans. So the model learns "what does selection tolerate" without ever being told "this specific variant is the pathogenic one." It's a weak-label trick — population frequency as a proxy for the thing you actually care about, deployed at proteome scale.

JORDAN Walk me through what they built on top of AlphaFold.

ALEX At a high level it's two ingredients. One, the structural backbone — they take AlphaFold's learned protein representation, the thing that already encodes 3D geometry and residue environments, so the model "sees" where a mutation lands in the folded protein. Two, a protein-language-model-style objective over sequences, so it also captures the evolutionary, multiple-sequence-alignment signal of what's conserved. They fine-tune that combined system on the population-frequency objective, then read out a single continuous pathogenicity score from zero to one for every substitution, with thresholds that bin into likely benign, ambiguous, and likely pathogenic.

JORDAN You said you'd push on a design choice. Push.

ALEX My pushback is on the weak label itself. Population frequency is a fantastic proxy for strong purifying selection, but it's blind to two things that matter clinically. It's blind to late-onset and recessive effects — a variant that only hurts you after reproductive age, or only when homozygous, isn't strongly selected against, so it can sit at deceptively common frequency while still being clinically real. And it inherits the ancestry bias of the frequency databases, which are still heavily European. So I'd expect the model to be strongest on early-onset, dominant, strongly-selected disease and relatively weaker on exactly the milder, modifier-like effects.
