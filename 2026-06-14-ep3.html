Blurb

Nagao and colleagues (BMJ Open Respiratory Research, 2026) screened 458 Japanese patients with pulmonary non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) disease and/or non-CF bronchiectasis for deleterious CFTR variants, using exome + Sanger sequencing plus MLPA (to catch copy-number deletions). Deleterious CFTR variants appeared in 16 patients (3.5%) — all single-allele carriers, and none of them the variants on European newborn-screening panels — a striking contrast to a prior US study that found ~50%, attributable to how rare CF-causing variants are in Japan (CF incidence ~1:590,000 vs ~1:3,500 in the US). Clinically, treated carriers fared worse: 12-month culture conversion 20% vs ~70% (p=0.04) and macrolide resistance 60% vs ~12% (p=0.02), with a family cluster where the p.Leu1156Phe variant tracked with disease. The proposed direction: make ancestry a front-and-center variable in any CFTR cohort analysis, and treat treatment-response (does the infection clear; does resistance emerge) as a high-value endpoint. The catch: this is a carrier (single-allele) effect on small numbers — it doesn't transfer in magnitude to a two-copy CF cohort, and whether the registry even captures granular microbiology outcomes is unverified.

Plain-English glossary

  • Allele: one of your two copies of a gene (one from each parent).
  • Bronchiectasis: permanent widening and scarring of the airways.
  • CADD / AlphaMissense / REVEL / SpliceAI: in-silico (computer-prediction) tools estimating whether a variant is damaging.
  • Carrier: a person with one broken copy of a recessive-disease gene — usually considered healthy.
  • CFTR (Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane conductance Regulator): the gene building the cell-surface chloride channel; broken in cystic fibrosis.
  • CFTR2: a curated database of which CFTR variants are known to cause disease.
  • Culture conversion: when treatment clears the bacteria from sputum — the sign the drugs are working.
  • Deleterious variant: a harmful variant (here, predicted or database-confirmed).
  • Exome sequencing: reading the protein-coding ~2% of the genome.
  • Macrolide: the backbone antibiotic for NTM disease; resistance means the main drug fails.
  • MAC (Mycobacterium avium complex): the most common NTM culprit.
  • MLPA (multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification): a method specialized for detecting copy-number changes (e.g., a deleted exon).
  • NTM (non-tuberculous mycobacteria): environmental mycobacteria (soil/water) causing slow chronic lung infection — not TB.
  • Recessive: needing both gene copies broken to have the disease.
  • Sanger sequencing: an older, highly accurate method for confirming a specific DNA spot.

Methods to lift

  • Three-method variant ascertainment — exome (discovery) + Sanger (confirmation) + MLPA (copy-number/exon-deletion): single-method screens miss structural variants.
  • CFTR2-first pathogenicity filter, in-silico tools second — database-curated calls preferred; CADD/AlphaMissense/REVEL/SpliceAI for the uncertain remainder.
  • 1:8 age- and sex-matched case-control design — matched comparison of variant carriers vs non-carriers.
  • Treatment-response endpoints — 12-month culture conversion and emergent macrolide resistance (Fisher's exact test) as outcomes, not just diagnosis.

Proposed direction (conditional)

What to verify first

  • This is a carrier (single-allele) effect — magnitude does not transfer to a two-copy CF cohort; the dose being varied is smaller and subtler.
  • Small numbers (16 carriers, 14 treated) — effects are suggestive, not definitive.
  • Does the registry capture longitudinal microbiology (culture conversion, macrolide resistance), or only a diagnosis code?
  • Are there functional anchors (sweat chloride, assays) in the cohort to validate "deleterious" calls beyond prediction software?
  • Cohort ancestry fields: genetic vs self-reported, and enough non-European patients to test transferability.
