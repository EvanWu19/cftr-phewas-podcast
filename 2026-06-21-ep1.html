Episode date: 2026-06-21 · Slot: track_a_variant

Blurb

CADD-Splice (Rentzsch et al., Genome Medicine 2021) is the CADD release that closed the tool's long-standing splicing blind spot by folding two deep-learning splice predictors — SpliceAI (10 kb residual net) and MMSplice (75 bp CNN) — in as features of the genome-wide logistic-regression score. On the experimental MFASS splice benchmark, CADD-Splice nearly tripled splice auPRC over the prior release (0.063 → 0.108 → 0.185) while staying 0.995-correlated with old CADD genome-wide, so gains are concentrated at splice positions. On ClinVar-pathogenic-vs-gnomAD-common, it reached auROC 0.957 (intronic) / 0.978 (splice site), beating both old CADD and the standalone splice nets — the generalist wins on real variant interpretation because most "splice-region" variants aren't actually splice-driven. Where it lands: the Karen / CFTR variant-interpretation collaboration, because CFTR's signature deep-intronic and synonymous splice variants (3849+10kb C>T, 2789+5G>A, 3272-26A>G) are invisible to protein-only scores. Buildable artifact: a CFTR splice-variant annotation/discordance notebook.

Plain-English glossary

  • Acceptor site — the 3′ end of an intron where the spliceosome makes the downstream cut; usually marked by an invariant AG dinucleotide.
  • Alternative splicing — one gene producing multiple transcripts by including different combinations of exons; >90% of multi-exon human genes do it.
  • Canonical dinucleotides — the near-invariant GT (donor) / AG (acceptor) bases at intron boundaries; the "easy" splice variants hit these.
  • CADD (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion) — genome-wide variant effect predictor; logistic regression trained on ~15M human-derived (proxy-benign) vs matched simulated (proxy-deleterious) variants over 100+ annotations.
  • Deep intronic variant — a variant far from the exon-intron boundary that can still create a cryptic splice site or disrupt regulation (e.g., CFTR 3849+10kb C>T).
  • Donor site — the 5′ start of an intron where the upstream cut is made; usually an invariant GT.
  • Exon / intron — exons are retained in mature mRNA; introns are spliced out of pre-mRNA.
  • Masking (SpliceAI) — zeroing biologically nonsensical predictions (splice gain at an existing site, loss outside any site) before using the score, to raise signal-to-noise.
  • MFASS — Multiplexed Functional Assay of Splicing using Sort-seq; minigene reporter assay measuring splice disruption for ~27k variants; the paper's experimental benchmark.
  • Percent-spliced-in (psi) — fraction of transcripts in which a given exon segment is included; here also a leakage trap (HAL used reference psi while labels were defined by Δpsi).
  • Splicing / spliceosome — removal of introns and joining of exons in pre-mRNA to make translatable mRNA.
  • Synonymous (silent) variant — a coding change that leaves the amino acid unchanged but can still disrupt a splicing enhancer.

ALEX Welcome back. Today's Track A paper is "CADD-Splice — improving genome-wide variant effect prediction using deep learning-derived splice scores," by Philipp Rentzsch, Max Schubach, Jay Shendure, and Martin Kircher, published in Genome Medicine in 2021. We're reading it because CADD is one of the most-used variant scores on the planet, and this is the version where its authors finally fixed CADD's biggest blind spot: splicing. For a gene like CFTR, where some of the most important disease variants are deep in introns and invisible to a protein-centric score, that fix matters a lot.

JORDAN Let me anchor the vocabulary before we go further, because "splicing" is the whole paper. Walk me through it like I haven't thought about it since a textbook.

ALEX Happy to. Your genes are written in DNA as alternating chunks — exons, which are the parts that end up in the final messenger RNA and get translated into protein, and introns, the stretches between them that get cut out. When a gene is transcribed, the cell makes a pre-mRNA that still contains the introns, and then the spliceosome snips out each intron and stitches the exons together. The cut points are defined by short sequence signals: the donor site at the start of the intron, the acceptor site at its end. Those are usually marked by an almost-invariant GT at the donor and AG at the acceptor — the canonical dinucleotides.

JORDAN So a variant that hits that GT or AG breaks the cut.

ALEX Right, and those are the easy ones — everybody's predictor catches a canonical splice-site mutation. The hard part is everything else. A variant fifteen bases into the intron, or a "silent" change inside an exon that doesn't alter the amino acid but does disturb a splicing enhancer, can still cause an exon to be skipped or an intron to be retained. Two more terms: alternative splicing, where one gene produces several transcripts by including different exon combinations — over ninety percent of multi-exon human genes do this — and percent-spliced-in, or psi, which is just the fraction of transcripts in which a given exon segment is included. Psi will come back later as a sneaky source of bias.

JORDAN Got it. So the background problem is: general-purpose variant scores were bad at the non-obvious splice variants.

ALEX Exactly, and it's worth being precise about what CADD even is, since it's the spine of the paper. CADD — Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion — is a genome-wide variant effect predictor. The clever trick is its training set. Instead of using a curated list of "known pathogenic" versus "known benign" variants, which carries every ascertainment bias of decades of human study, CADD trains on roughly fifteen million variants that became fixed or nearly fixed in the human lineage since the split with other great apes — those are the proxy-benign class — against a matched set of simulated de novo variants, the proxy-deleterious class. The logic: anything that survived evolution is probably tolerable; a random simulated mutation is more likely to be harmful. It's a logistic regression over a hundred-plus annotations.

JORDAN And because it's trained on evolution rather than clinical labels, it doesn't overfit to the handful of genes people have studied to death.

ALEX That's the selling point. But the same breadth is why it underperformed on splicing. Conservation scores — how constrained a base is across species — are a major CADD feature, and they're nearly useless inside exons for splice prediction because most exonic bases are already maximally conserved for protein reasons. So the splice signal drowns. Up through CADD version 1.3, the only splice information was a crude distance-to-nearest-canonical-site within twenty base pairs. Version 1.4 added dbscSNV, an ensemble score, but it only covers a narrow window right around the canonical sites. Everything distal was still a blind spot.

JORDAN So what did they actually build?

ALEX They took two modern deep-learning splice predictors and folded them in as features. The first is SpliceAI — a deep residual neural network that reads ten thousand bases of raw sequence around a position and predicts, base by base, the probability that it's a splice donor or acceptor. It was trained on GTEx RNA expression across tissues. The second is MMSplice — a convolutional neural network that looks at a tighter seventy-five-base window on each side of a known junction, trained on a massively parallel reporter library of random sequences. Both take genomic sequence as input rather than precomputed annotations, which is a design choice they're emphatic about.

JORDAN Why is "sequence in, not annotations in" such a big deal to them?

ALEX Because a sequence-based model can be re-run when the genome build or the gene annotation updates, without retraining. An annotation-derived feature goes stale. They benchmarked the candidate scores on MFASS — the Multiplexed Functional Assay of Splicing using Sort-seq — which experimentally tested about twenty-seven thousand human population variants in a minigene reporter and measured whether each one shifted splicing. They labeled a variant "splice-disrupting" if it changed the exon's psi by more than 0.5. One design choice I'll push on: MFASS is a minigene assay, not the native genomic context, and it doesn't contain gain-of-function variants or deep intronic ones beyond about forty bases. So it's the best benchmark available, but it's narrow, and the authors do acknowledge that you can't generalize confidently to the deep-intronic variants that — for CFTR specifically — are exactly the interesting cases.

JORDAN That caveat is going to matter for us. But first — did the integration work?

ALEX On the splice-only benchmark, the specialized nets win, as you'd expect. SpliceAI hit an area-under-the-precision-recall-curve of 0.328, MMSplice 0.361. Interestingly their predictions only correlate around 0.6 with each other — different architectures, different windows — so the authors combined them, equally weighted, into a score they call MMAI, which reached 0.416. That's the "two models see different things" result. Now the integration: plain CADD on the MFASS set went from auPRC 0.063 at version 1.3, to 0.108 at 1.4, to 0.185 for CADD-Splice. So they nearly tripled the splice performance relative to the last released version.

JORDAN And the worry with any change like that is you improve one thing and quietly wreck everything else.

ALEX Which is the result I find most important. Across a hundred thousand random genome-wide variants, CADD-Splice correlates with the previous CADD at a Spearman of 0.995. The overall behavior is essentially unchanged — the gains are surgically concentrated at splice-relevant positions. They then validated on real disease variants: ClinVar pathogenic variants against common gnomAD variants as controls. CADD-Splice reached an area-under-the-ROC of 0.957 on intronic variants and 0.978 on splice-site variants, beating not just old CADD — 0.879 and 0.938 — but also the specialized MMSplice and SpliceAI run alone. That's the headline: the general score, with splice nets folded in, beat the splice nets themselves on actual pathogenic-variant classification.

JORDAN Wait — how does the generalist beat the specialists on their own turf?

ALEX Because even an "intronic" or "splice-region" test set contains variants whose real effect isn't splicing — and CADD carries conservation, consequence category, and a hundred other annotations that catch those. The specialists only model splicing, so when a splice-region variant is actually pathogenic for some other reason, they miss it. The lesson the authors draw — and I think it's the transferable one — is that a process-specific score is superior only when you already know the sole possible effect is that process. In real variant interpretation you don't know that, so you want the integrated generalist. There's also a careful trick called masking: SpliceAI predicts both splice gain and splice loss, and they zero out nonsensical predictions — predicted gain at an already-existing site, predicted loss outside any site — before feeding the score in. That denoising improved the signal.

JORDAN One thing flagged for me — the psi business you said was sneaky.

ALEX Good catch. An earlier model, HAL, looked great on exons, but it used the reference allele's psi as a feature — and the benchmark labels were defined by the change in psi. So psi was partly predicting itself. Circularity. They showed that without that feature HAL drops to roughly MMSplice's level. It's a clean reminder to check whether a feature is leaking the label, which is the kind of thing you'd want to verify before trusting any of these on a new dataset.

JORDAN So where does this land for us?

ALEX This lands squarely on the Karen collaboration — CFTR variant interpretation. Here's why it's not generic: CFTR has marquee splice variants that a protein-only score completely misses. 3849+10kb C>T sits deep in an intron and creates a cryptic splice site. 2789+5G>A and 3272-26A>G are intronic splice-modulating variants with real, modulator-relevant clinical consequences. A missense-focused predictor returns nothing useful for these because there's no amino acid change to score. CADD-Splice is precisely the tool that does fire on them. The buildable artifact: a CFTR splice-variant annotation notebook. Take every intronic and synonymous CFTR variant from CFTR2 and gnomAD, pull CADD-Splice and raw SpliceAI scores for each, join on genomic coordinate to the CFTR2 functional classification and ClinVar assertion, and output a discordance table — variants CADD-Splice calls splice-damaging that CFTR2 currently lists as uncertain or unclassified, and clinically pathogenic splice variants that the scores miss. Input is a CFTR variant list with coordinates; difficulty is small-to-medium because the scores are precomputed and downloadable, so it's mostly a careful coordinate-and-HGVS join.

JORDAN What would we need to check before trusting that notebook?

ALEX Several things, all phrased as open. First, MFASS — the benchmark that anchors these scores — tops out around forty intronic bases from the junction, so for CFTR's genuinely deep-intronic variants we'd be extrapolating beyond where any of these tools were validated; we'd want to check each deep variant against actual RNA or minigene evidence rather than trusting the score. Second, coordinate and build hygiene: CADD ships separate GRCh37 and GRCh38 models, and a silent liftover error would scramble a splice call, so we'd verify the build and the HGVS mapping end to end. Third, masking assumes the annotated splice-site set is correct; for a gene with tissue-specific and alternative CFTR isoforms, we'd want to confirm which transcript annotation the scores used. And fourth, these are predictions of a splicing effect, not of clinical severity — a variant can disrupt splicing and still be modulated, so the discordance table is a triage queue, not a verdict.

JORDAN That's a clean line — the score flags a mechanism, the clinic decides the meaning.

ALEX Exactly. Tomorrow on Track A we'll stay in variant-effect land but likely shift toward protein-structure-based prediction. The one-line takeaway for today: fold a sequence-based splice net into a generalist score and you get the best of both — but only inside the window where the splice net was actually validated, which for CFTR's deep introns is the part we'd still have to check by hand.
