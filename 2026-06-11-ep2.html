How did this episode land for you?

Blurb

Winters and colleagues asked whether a loss-of-function variant carries the same risk across ancestries, using the heart gene PKP2 (the main gene in ARVC) as their test case across four biobanks totaling over 660,000 people — MyCode, All of Us, Penn Medicine Biobank (PMBB), and UK Biobank. Two findings pull in opposite directions: PKP2 predicted-pathogenic variants were 2–3× more common in African- than European-ancestry individuals (strongly so in MyCode, All of Us, and UK Biobank), yet the link between carrying a variant and actually having an ARVC-related trait showed up only in European-ancestry carriers. The honest conclusion is that carrier frequency and disease risk are different numbers, and ancestry can split them — driven either by genuinely lower penetrance or by under-diagnosis in under-studied groups, which this data can't distinguish. The CFTR parallel is direct: CFTR variants were catalogued mostly in European patients, so any penetrance estimate should be ancestry-stratified from the start, treating an apparent "mild" signal in an under-studied group as a possible ascertainment artifact, not just biology. The binding question is whether CF cohorts even hold enough non-European patients to estimate ancestry-specific penetrance.

Plain-English glossary

  • Ancestry (genetic) — the population history written into a person's DNA; related to, but not identical with, self-reported race/ethnicity.
  • ARVC — arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy; an inherited heart-muscle disease raising arrhythmia and sudden-death risk.
  • Ascertainment — how subjects came to be in a study; recruitment method silently shapes which associations are detectable.
  • Biobank — a large research collection linking people's DNA to their medical records.
  • Fisher's exact test — a statistical test for whether two categories are associated more than chance.
  • gnomAD — a large public reference database of population allele frequencies.
  • Logistic regression — a model testing whether a factor (here, carrier status) is associated with a yes/no outcome, adjustable for age and sex.
  • Loss-of-function (LOF) — a variant that breaks the gene/protein rather than subtly altering it.
  • Odds ratio (OR) — how much a factor multiplies the odds of an outcome.
  • Penetrance — the fraction of variant carriers who actually develop the associated disease.
  • PKP2 (plakophilin-2) — the most commonly mutated gene in ARVC; helps heart cells adhere and conduct.
  • Predicted pathogenic variant — here, the union of ClinVar pathogenic/likely-pathogenic calls and an in-house high-impact filter (VEP HIGH, not last exon, MAF < 0.1%).
  • Self-reported ethnicity — a checkbox category (used by UK Biobank) treated as a proxy for genetic ancestry.

Methods to lift

  • Multi-cohort replication across ascertainment types — test the same question in population (UK Biobank), diverse-national (All of Us), health-system (MyCode), and referral (PMBB) biobanks; divergence is informative.
  • Two-source pathogenic variant definition — combine ClinVar P/LP calls with a transparent in-house filter (VEP impact HIGH, not last exon, MAF < 0.1%).
  • Separate the two questions — test carrier-frequency-by-ancestry (Fisher's exact) and carrier-to-phenotype association (logistic regression, age/sex-adjusted) independently; never assume they move together.
  • Ancestry assignment — genetic classifiers (random forest / kernel density on reference panels) vs self-report; note the seam when pooling cohorts.
  • Underpowering caveat — explicitly flag that a null in a smaller ancestry subgroup may be absence of power, not absence of effect.

Proposed direction (conditional)

Conditional on inspection of CFF WGS variant calls and registry phenotype linkage: test whether the penetrance of specific CFTR variants for particular phenotypes differs across ancestry groups, modeling ancestry explicitly rather than adjusting it away, and treating any apparent low-penetrance signal in an under-studied group as a diagnosis-ascertainment question, not only a biological one.

What to verify first

  • Whether the cohort holds enough African-ancestry, Latino, and other non-European CF patients to estimate ancestry-specific penetrance at all.
  • How ancestry would be assigned in the available data, and whether that method is consistent across any combined cohorts.
  • Whether under-diagnosis of CF in non-European groups could masquerade as low penetrance (the ascertainment trap).
  • Whether the CFTR variants of interest are common enough within each ancestry group to test, given F508del's European skew.

They find that the very same variant looks common and threatening in one population and quieter in another — and that the reason might be less about biology than about who got studied.

JORDAN Before we dig in, give me the words. What's PKP2, what's loss-of-function, and what does "by ancestry" actually mean?

ALEX Let's take them one at a time, assuming nothing. PKP2 is a gene — it stands for plakophilin-2 — and it helps heart muscle cells stick together and conduct their electrical signal. It's the most commonly mutated gene in a heart condition called ARVC, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, which is a disease where heart muscle is gradually replaced by fat and scar, raising the risk of dangerous rhythms and sudden death. Loss-of-function, often shortened to LOF, means a variant that breaks the gene — it knocks out the protein rather than just tweaking it, so these are the changes you'd most expect to cause disease. Now, "ancestry." Your genetic ancestry is the population history written into your DNA — which ancestral groups your genome descends from. It is not the same as the race someone checks on a form, though the two are tangled together, and we'll see this paper wrestle with exactly that tangle. The last term you need is penetrance: that's the fraction of people who carry a disease variant who actually go on to show the disease. A variant can be fully penetrant — everyone who has it gets sick — or low-penetrance, where many carriers stay perfectly healthy. Penetrance is the hinge this whole paper turns on.

JORDAN So what was the open problem? Why did anyone need to do this?

ALEX Because almost everything we know about which variants cause disease was learned in people of European ancestry. The big historical studies, the reference databases, the lists of "known pathogenic variants" — overwhelmingly European. That creates a quiet bias: if a variant is common in a non-European group but was first catalogued as dangerous in Europeans, you might be over-calling it dangerous in everyone, or you might be missing that it behaves differently in different groups. For ARVC specifically, a few earlier studies hinted that disease-associated variants were more common in people of African ancestry, but the studies were small and couldn't tell whether those carriers actually got sick. The unknown the authors are chasing: does a PKP2 loss-of-function variant carry the same risk in people of African ancestry as in people of European ancestry — or have we been generalizing from one population to all of them?

JORDAN How do you even test that? Walk me through it.

ALEX They went big and they went multi-cohort, which is the smart move. They pulled genetic and health-record data from over 660,000 people across four separate biobanks — a biobank being a large research collection linking people's DNA to their medical information. The four were MyCode, which is the Geisinger health system's biobank with 175,500 people; All of Us, the diverse national US program, 245,394; the Penn Medicine Biobank, abbreviated PMBB, 43,925, recruited through referrals to one academic center; and the UK Biobank, 200,580, a national population study in Britain. Those four differ enormously in who's in them — African-ancestry fraction ranges from 25.7 percent in PMBB and 23.2 percent in All of Us down to 3.0 percent in MyCode and just 1.6 percent in the UK Biobank — and that variety is the point. To define a "predicted pathogenic variant," they used two sources combined: variants already flagged pathogenic in ClinVar, plus variants their own filter predicted would severely break the protein — high-impact, rare, under one-tenth of one percent frequency, not in spots where a break wouldn't matter. Then a Fisher's exact test — a standard test for whether two categories are associated more than chance — asked whether carrying a PKP2 variant lined up with African versus European ancestry. And logistic regression, adjusted for age and sex, asked the real question: do carriers actually have ARVC-related traits?

ALEX Here's where I'd push on a design choice. The four cohorts define ancestry in genuinely different ways — MyCode and All of Us use genetic algorithms trained on reference populations, while the UK Biobank leans on self-reported ethnicity from a checkbox at recruitment. The authors argue self-report is a reasonable proxy, and maybe, but you're now comparing "genetically inferred African ancestry" in one cohort against "ticked the Black box" in another, and quietly treating them as the same variable. I'd want that seam acknowledged louder than it is.

JORDAN So what did the numbers say — same risk or not?

ALEX Two findings, and they pull in opposite directions, which is what makes the paper interesting. First, the carrier finding: people with a PKP2 predicted-pathogenic variant were two to three times more likely to be of African than European ancestry — and this held across three of the four biobanks, with strong statistics. Fisher's p-value of 3 times ten-to-the-minus-four in MyCode, 1 times ten-to-the-minus-nine in All of Us, 5 times ten-to-the-minus-three in the UK Biobank. The odd one out was PMBB, the single referral center, where the enrichment vanished — p of 0.7, essentially no difference. So far that reads as "this broken-gene variant is genuinely more common in African-ancestry genomes." But then the second finding flips the mood. When they asked who actually had ARVC-related traits, the association showed up only in European-ancestry carriers. In MyCode, European carriers of a PKP2 variant had about 1.78 times the odds of an ARVC trait; in All of Us, about 2.01 times, with a p of 0.01. African-ancestry carriers? No significant association in any cohort. PMBB and the UK Biobank showed no significant carrier-to-trait link at all.

JORDAN Why did PMBB keep being the odd one out — no enrichment, no association? Is that a real biological difference or something about the cohort?

ALEX Almost certainly something about the cohort, and it's a clean illustration of a concept called ascertainment — how people got into the study in the first place. PMBB is a single referral center: people land in it because they were sent to an academic hospital, often already sick, often for heart problems specifically. So its sample isn't a slice of the general population the way the UK Biobank or All of Us tries to be — it's enriched for cardiac patients to begin with. When your whole cohort is pre-selected for the disease you're studying, the contrast between carriers and non-carriers gets washed out, because even your "controls" are unusual. That's why PMBB also had the highest baseline rate of ARVC-related traits, 23 percent, versus 4.1 percent in the UK Biobank. The takeaway for you: a cohort's recruitment method silently shapes what associations you can even detect. The same CFTR variant studied in a CF clinic population versus a general biobank could give you different-looking answers for reasons that have nothing to do with the gene.

ALEX They answered it, but the answer is uncomfortable rather than clean, and they're honest about that. The two findings together don't say "this variant is harmless in African-ancestry people." They flag, repeatedly, that the African-ancestry subgroups were smaller and underpowered, so a real association could be hiding behind too few people.
