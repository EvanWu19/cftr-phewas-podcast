Paper: Li Z, et al. "A reference panel for linkage disequilibrium and genotype imputation using whole-genome sequencing data from 2,680 participants across India." HGG Advances (2026).

Blurb (~150 words)

A pure infrastructure paper, and a clear lesson in how the reference data underneath a study quietly sets the ceiling on what it can resolve. The authors built LASI-DAD — the largest, most nationally representative Indian genetic reference panel to date — from 30× whole-genome sequencing of 2,680 participants drawn across 18 Indian states. As an LD lookup panel it holds 69.5M variants (170% more than 1000 Genomes South Asian; 213% more than TOP-LD), with nearly all the gain in rare variants. As an imputation panel it beat TOPMed by a mean 38% and Genome Asia Pilot by 27% in accuracy; meta-imputation across panels added up to 107% for the rarest variants. Yet a European-derived height PRS still explained only ~10–17% of variance in Indian subgroups vs. ~45% in Europeans — a better answer key can't fix a test written for another population. Proposed direction: an ancestry-stratified audit of imputation/LD-reference quality across the CFF cohort before interpreting any modifier locus as resolved.

Plain-English glossary

  • Bi-allelic / multi-allelic — a position with two versions (ref + one alt) vs. three or more; this panel kept only bi-allelic sites.
  • Founder event — a population bottleneck through few ancestors, which pushes some rare variants to higher frequency (and boosts mapping power).
  • Genotype imputation — statistically filling in unmeasured genotypes using a reference library of full genomes.
  • Imputation accuracy — here, the Pearson correlation between imputed and true genotypes.
  • LASI-DAD — Longitudinal Aging Study in India, Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia; the WGS cohort the panel was built from.
  • LD (linkage disequilibrium) — the non-random tendency of nearby alleles to be inherited together.
  • LD block / LDetect / Big-LD — segments of correlated variants, and the tools that delineate them at broad vs. fine scale.
  • MAF (minor allele frequency) — fraction of chromosomes carrying the minor allele; "rare" ≈ ≤1%.
  • Meta-imputation — combining imputation results from multiple reference panels for better accuracy, especially for rare variants.
  • PRS (polygenic risk score) — a single number summing many small variant effects to estimate genetic predisposition to a trait.
  • Reference panel — a library of complete genomes used as the answer key for LD and imputation.
  • 1000G / TOPMed / GAsP / TOP-LD — existing reference panels (1000 Genomes, Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine, Genome Asia Pilot, TOP-LD); all under-represent India.
  • 30× coverage — each genome letter read ~30 times for accuracy.
  • VQSR — Variant Quality Score Recalibration, a variant-QC filter; the panel used a stringent 99.8% tranche.
  • WGS — whole-genome sequencing.

Methods to lift

  • Population-matched reference panel construction from 30× WGS — the upstream resource that determines imputation quality and fine-mapping resolution for any locus.
  • LD characterization at two scales: LDetect (broad, ~1,262 blocks) + Big-LD (fine, ~106k blocks); varLD scores to quantify regional LD differences between populations.
  • Meta-imputation (combining panels) — a concrete way to boost rare-variant imputation accuracy when no single panel dominates.
  • Ancestry-stratified PRS transfer evaluation — measuring how prediction accuracy decays with genetic distance from the discovery population (the equity/power lesson).
  • QC discipline: stringent VQSR (99.8%) filter, bi-allelic restriction, call-rate and depth filters — and the reminder that "variant count" depends heavily on filter stringency, so cross-panel counts aren't clean comparisons.

Proposed direction (conditional)

Conditional on inspection of CFF WGS variant calls and registry phenotype linkage: Conduct an ancestry-stratified assessment of imputation quality and LD-reference concordance across the CFF whole-genome cohort — quantifying whether association and fine-mapping power at CFTR-modifier loci degrades for under-represented-ancestry patients — before interpreting any locus as resolved.

What to verify first

  • How the CFF registry WGS was generated (directly sequenced vs. imputed): if all patients are fully sequenced at 30×, the imputation half is less relevant and the LD half carries over.
  • The registry's actual ancestry composition (not yet inspected).
  • Whether the CF modifier loci of interest sit in regions where ancestry-driven LD differences are large vs. small (Li shows this varies a lot region to region).
  • Note: gains here concentrated in rare variants; many classic CF modifier signals are common variants, where panel-choice effects were smaller — so effect size for this specific question is unknown.

ALEX Welcome back. Our second paper today is from 2026, in the journal HGG Advances, first author Zhi Li and colleagues, titled "A reference panel for linkage disequilibrium and genotype imputation using whole-genome sequencing data from 2,680 participants across India." Yesterday's paper was about judging a single variant. This one is about the infrastructure that decides which variants you can see at all — and it's a clean window into why where a study's reference data comes from quietly shapes everything downstream.

JORDAN I'm going to need definitions before the title stops being intimidating. Start with "reference panel."

ALEX Sure. Picture a reference panel as a community library of complete, carefully-read genomes from a particular population. Once you have that library, you can do two powerful things, and the title names both. The first is "linkage disequilibrium," LD. LD is just the fact that nearby genetic letters tend to travel together — inherited as a block, not shuffled independently. If I know your letter at one spot, LD lets me guess your letter at a neighbor with some confidence. The second thing is "genotype imputation." Imputation means filling in the blanks: most studies only directly measure a sparse scatter of positions using a cheap "microarray" chip, and imputation uses the library of full genomes to statistically infer the millions of positions you didn't measure.

JORDAN So the panel is the answer key, and imputation is using the answer key to fill in a half-finished test.

ALEX That's a perfect way to hold it. And the quality of the answer key matters enormously, because if your library doesn't contain people genetically similar to your patient, the guesses get worse. Two more terms you'll need. "Whole-genome sequencing," WGS, is reading all three billion letters directly — that's how this library was built, at "30x" coverage, meaning each letter was read about thirty times over for accuracy. And "polygenic risk score," PRS, which we'll hit in the results: a single number that adds up the small effects of many variants to estimate someone's genetic predisposition to a trait like height or body-mass index.

JORDAN Why India specifically? What was unknown or contested here?

ALEX The motivating problem is representation. India has over 1.4 billion people and more than 4,500 anthropologically distinct groups, yet Indians make up less than two percent of participants in the global catalog of genome-wide association studies. So the answer keys we've built — the famous ones are the 1000 Genomes Project, TOPMed which stands for Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine, and the Genome Asia Pilot — barely contain Indian genomes. The authors note 1000 Genomes had only about 200 Indians, mostly expatriates living in Houston and the UK, and TOPMed's only South Asian samples came from 644 people in a Pakistani heart-disease study.

JORDAN And does that actually matter genetically, or is it just a fairness point?

ALEX It's both, and the genetics is the interesting half. The Indian population descends largely from a mix of two ancestral groups the authors call Ancestral North Indians and Ancestral South Indians, and that South Indian component isn't found anywhere outside the subcontinent. On top of that, long traditions of marriage within communities created "founder events" — bottlenecks where the population passed through a small number of ancestors — more extreme, the authors say, than in Ashkenazi Jewish or Finnish populations. Founder effects push certain rare variants up to higher frequency, which actually increases your statistical power to map them to disease. So India is both underserved and unusually informative — but only if you have a proper reference library.

JORDAN So they built one. From what?

ALEX From a study called LASI-DAD — the Longitudinal Aging Study in India, Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia. It's an aging-and-cognition study that whole-genome-sequenced its participants. After quality control they kept 2,680 individuals, and crucially these were sampled to be nationally representative — drawn from 18 states and territories, not one region. Let me name the quality-control idea once because it recurs across all these papers: "QC" means throwing out unreliable data — samples with low coverage, contamination, sex mismatches — and throwing out unreliable variant positions. They applied a fairly strict filter and ended up with about 71 million high-quality variant positions to work with.

JORDAN Push back on one design choice for me — anything they did that you'd question?

ALEX One honest tension: they restricted the panel to "bi-allelic" variants — positions with only two versions, a reference and one alternative — and excluded "multi-allelic" sites where three or more versions exist. That keeps the analysis clean, and they're upfront that it's a limitation they'd fix in future work. The cost is that some real, possibly interesting complex positions are simply absent from the library. It's a defensible simplification, but it's a choice, and it slightly undercuts the "most comprehensive" framing.

JORDAN Alright, the results. Did the new library actually beat the old ones?

ALEX Substantially, and let me give you the concrete numbers because they're the point. As an LD lookup panel, this new resource — they call it LASI-DAD after the study — contained 69.5 million variants. That's a 170 percent increase over the 1000 Genomes South Asian panel and a 213 percent increase over another panel called TOP-LD. Almost all of that gain is in rare variants, MAF below one percent, exactly the ones a smaller library can't see.

JORDAN And for the imputation side — the fill-in-the-blanks side?

ALEX Better accuracy, measured as the correlation between the imputed guesses and the true genotypes. Against TOPMed, the new panel improved imputation accuracy by 3 to 101 percent, with a mean of 38 percent. Against Genome Asia Pilot, 3 to 73 percent, mean 27 percent. And for rare variants specifically — MAF at or below one percent — the new panel could impute about 62 million such variants, a 182 percent increase over TOPMed. So both more variants visible and more accurate guesses.

JORDAN Is there a trick to squeeze even more out of these libraries, or is one panel always the best you can do?

ALEX There's a nice trick they demonstrate called meta-imputation — combining the guesses from several reference panels instead of betting on one. For the rarest variants, MAF at or below 0.1 percent, meta-imputation across all three panels boosted accuracy by 14 to 107 percent, mean 64 percent, over the single best panel. For more common variants it barely helped — gains under one percent — because the single good panel already nailed those. So the payoff from blending answer keys lands exactly where the rare variants live. And there's a quieter point buried in the quality-control choices: the new panel used a stricter filter than the older ones. That's why it actually reports fewer common variants in some comparisons — not because it's worse, but because the larger sample size let it re-classify some borderline positions and drop weakly-supported sites the looser panels kept. Stringency and completeness pull against each other, and which one you want depends on whether you're hunting common signals or rare ones.

JORDAN So even the definition of "how many variants" depends on how picky your filter is. Good to know it's not a clean count.

ALEX Never a clean count. Hold that thought, because it'll matter when you're comparing the registry's variant calls against any external reference.

JORDAN Did better imputation translate into anything people actually care about, like risk prediction?

ALEX It did, modestly, and the honesty about the modesty is what I want you to notice. Using the new panel as the LD reference improved polygenic-risk-score performance by 2.1 to 35.1 percent across traits. But here's the sobering part. Even with the better Indian panel, a height polygenic score derived mostly from European-ancestry studies explained only about 17 percent of height variation in the most European-like Indian subgroup, dropping to 10 percent in the most genetically distant subgroup — versus nearly 45 percent in Europeans. The tool got better, but the gap with Europe stayed large, because the underlying discovery studies were European. A better answer key can't fix a test that was written for someone else.
