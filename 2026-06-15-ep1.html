Paper: Johansson I, et al. "Proof of principle concept for the analysis and functional prediction of rare genetic variants in the CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 genes." Human Genomics (2025).

Blurb (~150 words)

This is a small, rigorous benchmark of a question every variant-level genomics project eventually faces: when you find a rare missense variant, can a computer tell you whether it breaks the protein? Johansson et al. took 11 rare variants in two drug-metabolism enzymes (CYP2C19, CYP2D6) from the Estonian Biobank, ran each through a five-algorithm in-silico prediction framework, and then measured the actual enzyme activity in cells. The computer correctly anticipated 6 of 11 (54%) — but the misses were systematic: predictions were excellent at the "completely dead" extreme (93% accurate in the 0–10% activity bucket) and unreliable in the intermediate-activity zone, where it produced both false alarms (M339T predicted damaging, ~70% active) and false reassurances (L314M predicted near-dead, ~60% active). The proposed direction for the CFTR PheWAS: tier rare-variant annotations by prediction confidence rather than trusting a single score, and quarantine the gray-middle variants. Open question: the intermediate zone — where CFTR "residual function" variants live — is exactly where prediction is weakest.

Plain-English glossary

  • ADME — Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion; the four processes governing a drug's fate in the body. The framework used here was "ADME-optimized."
  • CADD — Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion; a widely used in-silico score where higher = more likely damaging. One of the five predictors combined here.
  • Catalytic activity — how fast an enzyme performs its chemical reaction; measured here as the rate a variant enzyme metabolized a test drug.
  • CYP2C19 / CYP2D6 — two cytochrome P450 enzymes that metabolize many antidepressants/antipsychotics; the FDA's two highest-priority pharmacogenes.
  • Deep mutational scanning — a lab technique that measures the functional effect of thousands of variants at once; the authors flag it as the data needed to train better predictors.
  • HEK293 — a human cell line (kidney-derived) used as a dish to express and test mutant proteins.
  • In silico — "in silicon," i.e., a computer prediction.
  • In vitro — "in glass," i.e., a real experiment in cells/test tubes (the gold standard here).
  • Loss-of-function (LoF) / pLOF — a variant that destroys protein function; "p" = predicted.
  • MAF (minor allele frequency) — the fraction of population chromosomes carrying the variant; ≤1% = rare.
  • Missense variant — a single-letter change that swaps one amino acid for another (vs. truncating/frameshift, which cut the protein short).
  • Pharmacogene — a gene whose protein affects drug response.
  • WGS / WES — whole-genome / whole-exome sequencing (all letters vs. the ~2% protein-coding letters).

Methods to lift

  • Five-predictor consensus framework (LRT, Mutation Assessor, PROVEAN, VEST3, CADD), trained on 337 functionally characterized variants across 43 ADME genes — a model for combining orthogonal predictors rather than trusting one.
  • In-silico → in-vitro validation loop: predict, then express the mutant in HEK293 cells and measure catalytic rate against a substrate (omeprazole for CYP2C19, bufuralol for CYP2D6) — the discipline of checking predictions against real biology.
  • Confidence-stratified interpretation: the paper's finding that accuracy is bucket-dependent (93% in the 0–10%-activity bucket, poor in the 20–80% middle) is itself a reusable annotation strategy.
  • Structural modeling (PDB structures + AlphaFold rotamers + docking in AutoDock Vina/UCSF Chimera) to explain why a swap impairs function — and the cautionary counterexample (R133C, damaging but far from the active site) showing structure alone isn't enough.

Proposed direction (conditional)

Conditional on inspection of CFF WGS variant calls and registry phenotype linkage: Build a CFTR-and-modifier rare-variant annotation pipeline that stratifies each variant by predicted-effect confidence — reserving a high-confidence "loss of function" tier for variants where multiple orthogonal predictors agree at the damaging extreme, and explicitly quarantining intermediate-score variants pending functional or clinical evidence before allowing them to drive any association.

What to verify first

  • Whether CFTR has a curated functional dataset (for the rare variant tail specifically) comparable to the 337-variant training set used here.
  • In the actual registry sequencing: how many rare CFTR/modifier variants fall into the untrustworthy intermediate-prediction band (footnote vs. central design problem).
  • Whether Estonian/European-derived prediction calibration transfers to the registry's ancestry mix.

JORDAN Okay, but before we go anywhere near CYP2C19 — that's a mouthful — can we slow down? What's a "rare genetic variant" in the first place?

ALEX Let's build it from the ground up. Your genome is a three-billion-letter instruction book. A "variant" is just a spot where your letters differ from the standard reference book — a typo, basically. A "common variant" is a typo lots of people share. A "rare variant" is one almost nobody else has. The way scientists measure that is MAF — minor allele frequency — which is simply the fraction of copies of that letter in the population. A MAF of one percent or below is the usual line for "rare." Most of the rare ones are "missense" variants, meaning the typo changes one amino acid — one bead — in the protein chain. It doesn't delete the protein; it swaps one part.

JORDAN And how do they even find these rare typos?

ALEX Two technologies, and you'll hear both constantly. WGS — whole-genome sequencing — reads all three billion letters. WES — whole-exome sequencing — reads only the roughly two percent of the book that actually codes for proteins, which is cheaper. This paper drew its variants from the Estonian Biobank, a national research collection of DNA from Estonian volunteers, using both whole-genome and whole-exome data.

JORDAN Got it. So they have a pile of rare typos. What's the actual problem?

ALEX The problem is interpretation. CYP2C19 and CYP2D6 are "pharmacogenes" — genes whose proteins metabolize drugs, meaning they chemically chew up medications so your body can clear them. These two are enzymes from the cytochrome P450 family, and the FDA considers them the two most important genes for predicting how people respond to many antidepressants and antipsychotics — drugs like escitalopram, sertraline, risperidone. If your enzyme works slowly, the drug builds up; if it works fast, the drug vanishes before it helps. Common variants in these genes are already used in clinics. But the authors point out that rare variants are estimated to explain about four to six percent of the inherited differences in how these two enzymes work — and we mostly have no idea what any individual rare variant does.

JORDAN So four to six percent sounds small, but across millions of people that's a lot of unexplained drug response.

ALEX Exactly. And here's the crux that matters for you. When a rare variant shows up, the fast, cheap move is to run it through a computer prediction tool — "in silico," meaning "done in silicon," in a computer. These tools guess whether a variant is damaging. The slow, expensive, gold-standard move is "in vitro" — "in glass," meaning in actual cells in a dish — where you build the mutant protein and measure whether it still works. The whole field leans on the cheap computer predictions. This paper asks the uncomfortable question: when we check the computer's homework against the real biology, how often is it right?

JORDAN That is a great question. How did they check?

ALEX They picked eleven rare missense variants — seven in CYP2C19, four in CYP2D6 — from the Estonian Biobank. For each one, they did both things. On the computer side they used a published framework that combines five different prediction algorithms with names like LRT, Mutation Assessor, PROVEAN, VEST3, and CADD. CADD is the one you'll meet most often — it's a score where higher means "more likely to be damaging." This framework was specially tuned on 337 already-characterized variants across 43 drug-metabolism genes, and it spits out a predicted activity level — what percent of normal enzyme function the variant should have.

JORDAN And the real-biology side?

ALEX They took human kidney-derived cells in a dish — a workhorse line called HEK293 — and forced them to produce each mutant enzyme. Then they fed the cells a test drug and measured how fast the enzyme chewed it up. For CYP2C19 the test drug was omeprazole, the heartburn medication; for CYP2D6 it was bufuralol, a research compound. The rate of chewing tells you the real catalytic activity. They also built 3D structural models of each mutant protein to see, physically, why a swap might jam the machinery.

JORDAN I want to push on one thing. They tested each enzyme with only one drug. Is that a problem?

ALEX It's a real limitation, and to their credit they flag it themselves. These enzymes are "promiscuous" — they act on many different drugs, and a variant might cripple the enzyme for one drug but not another. So "this variant has thirty percent activity" really means "thirty percent for omeprazole specifically." That caveat will echo when we talk about your project, because a CFTR modifier might look broken for one body system and fine for another.

JORDAN Okay — the headline. Did the computer predictions match the lab?

ALEX Partially, and the pattern is the interesting part. The single cleanest number: the prediction tools correctly anticipated the functional impact of six out of the eleven variants — fifty-four percent. Just over a coin flip. But it wasn't random where they succeeded and failed. The tools were excellent at the extremes. Take the CYP2C19 variant called A297V — the computer scored it as maximally damaging, and in the dish it had essentially zero percent activity. Perfect agreement. Same story for H251Q, the other severely broken one.

JORDAN So when a variant is totally dead, the computer nails it.

ALEX Right. The authors note the algorithm had a ninety-three percent accuracy rate for variants in that zero-to-ten-percent-function bucket. The trouble was the middle and the surprises. There was a CYP2C19 variant, M339T, that the computer confidently called damaging — and in the dish it had about seventy percent of normal activity, basically fine. A false alarm. And going the other way, a CYP2D6 variant called L314M was predicted to have zero-to-ten percent activity, nearly dead — but the real enzyme retained almost sixty percent function. And one more that's mechanistically telling: D301N. The computer said it should keep twenty to thirty percent activity, but in the dish it was almost completely dead, because position 301 turns out to be a critical catalytic residue that the prediction tool simply doesn't know is special.

JORDAN So the computer doesn't know which positions are load-bearing.

ALEX That's the lesson in one sentence. The structural modeling showed why some swaps broke things — the A297V change caused twelve physical "clashes," atoms jammed against each other, distorting the pocket where the drug binds. But the authors also found a variant, R133C, that was the second-most-damaging in the lab yet sits nowhere near the known active site or the channels into it. The structure-based reasoning couldn't explain it. Their honest conclusion: in silico tools are genuinely useful at the damaging extreme, unreliable in the intermediate zone, and we still need large-scale real-biology data — they specifically mention "deep mutational scanning," which is a technique that measures thousands of variants at once — to train better models.
