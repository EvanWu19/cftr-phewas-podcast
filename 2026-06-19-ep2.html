Plain-English glossary

  • Confounder: a common cause of both treatment and outcome.
  • Consistency (causal): the treatment is well-enough defined that the observed outcome under a treatment equals the potential outcome under that treatment.
  • Contrast of potential outcomes: a comparison (difference or ratio) of the outcomes a person would have under different treatments.
  • G-estimation / structural nested model: models the treatment effect directly per time point; solves for the effect using the condition that, net of effect, treatment is independent of the counterfactual given history.
  • G-formula / g-computation: model outcome on treatment + confounder history, then simulate everyone under a set treatment regime and average — sequential standardization over the confounder distribution.
  • IPW / marginal structural model: weight each person by the inverse probability of the treatment they received given history, creating a pseudo-population where treatment is unconfounded; fit the outcome model there.
  • Positivity: every covariate stratum contains both treated and untreated individuals (required because IPW divides by the treatment probability).
  • Potential (counterfactual) outcome: the outcome a person would have under a specified treatment.
  • Time-varying confounding affected by prior treatment: an intermediate variable that is both a confounder of later treatment and a consequence of earlier treatment — simultaneously on and off the causal path.

Methods / tools to lift

  • G-formula (g-computation) — fit time-sequential outcome/confounder models, simulate under fixed regimes, average predicted outcomes.
  • IPW + marginal structural model — propensity model → inverse-probability (stabilized) weights → weighted outcome regression in the pseudo-population.
  • G-estimation of a structural nested model — direct per-time effect parameter; specialist tool for continuous treatments / effect modification.
  • Triangulation — run ≥2 g methods and check agreement; add a doubly-robust estimator as a cross-check.

JORDAN Then teach me the problem. What's a "g method" trying to fix that ordinary regression gets wrong?

ALEX One specific, nasty structure: time-varying confounding affected by prior treatment. Let me unpack that with the words, since the data-science part you already know. A confounder is a common cause of both the treatment and the outcome. In a longitudinal registry, you have treatments measured repeatedly over time — say a therapy started, stopped, adjusted visit to visit — and you have clinical variables, also measured repeatedly, that influence whether the next treatment happens and also affect the outcome. Lung function is the canonical CF example of such a variable. Here's the trap: that intermediate clinical variable is simultaneously a confounder for the next treatment decision and itself a consequence of the previous treatment. It sits on the causal pathway and off to the side at the same time.

JORDAN And why does that break a normal regression?

ALEX Because you're stuck between two errors. If you don't adjust for that time-varying variable, it confounds the treatment-outcome relationship and your estimate is biased. But if you do adjust for it the ordinary way — throw it in the regression as a covariate — you block part of the treatment's own effect, because it's a mediator of earlier treatment, and you can open a spurious path by conditioning on it. Standard regression has no setting that's correct here. You either underadjust or overadjust. That's the exact situation g methods were built for, and it's endemic to any registry where treatment and disease state co-evolve over years.

JORDAN Make the trap concrete for me. Walk a patient through two visits.

ALEX Take a registry-style longitudinal series — purely hypothetical, just to show the structure. Visit one, a patient's lung function dips, so the clinician escalates therapy. Visit two, the lung function partly recovers, which influences whether therapy is escalated again, and also feeds the long-run outcome. Now ask: what's the total effect of "escalate aggressively throughout" versus "don't"? If you adjust for visit-two lung function to remove its confounding of the visit-two decision, you've just conditioned on something that the visit-one treatment caused — you've thrown away part of the very effect you're trying to measure, because improved lung function at visit two is partly how the earlier treatment worked. Leave it out and it confounds. There is no covariate-adjustment setting that gives the right answer, and the more visits you stack, the worse the tangle. That's why a method that handles the whole treatment-and-confounder history jointly isn't a luxury here.

JORDAN So what are the methods, concretely? Give me the family.

ALEX There are three classic g methods, and this is standard, established content. First, the g-formula, also called g-computation. You model how the outcome depends on treatment and the confounder history, then you simulate: you set everyone's treatment to a value of interest, push them through the fitted models step by step through time, and average the predicted outcomes. Do it for "always treated" and "never treated" and the contrast is your effect. It's a standardization over the confounder distribution, done sequentially through time.

JORDAN That's the "simulate the counterfactual world" one. Second?

ALEX Inverse-probability weighting, which fits a marginal structural model. You model the probability of the treatment each person actually received given their history — the propensity — and weight each person by the inverse of that probability. Intuitively, you up-weight people who got an unlikely treatment given their covariates, building a pseudo-population in which treatment is no longer associated with the confounders. Then a simple weighted regression in that pseudo-population recovers the causal effect. The marginal structural model is the model for the outcome in that reweighted world.

JORDAN And the third?

ALEX G-estimation of a structural nested model. This one models the treatment effect directly as the difference it makes at each time point, and solves for the effect parameter by exploiting the condition that, after you remove the effect, treatment should be independent of the counterfactual outcome given history. It's the least used of the three and the most awkward to explain, but it has real advantages with continuous treatments and certain kinds of effect modification. The honest summary: g-formula and IPW are the two you'll reach for first; g-estimation is the specialist tool.

JORDAN Why does this connect to data quality, not just effect estimation?

ALEX Because these estimators are unforgiving about exactly the things a data-quality pass surfaces. Positivity violations, irregular visit spacing, treatment definitions that drift over time, missingness that depends on disease state — each of those quietly breaks a g method, and each is a data-quality property you can measure before you ever fit a model. So in practice the method and the quality audit are the same project from two directions: the assumptions g methods need are a checklist of data properties worth profiling. That's the honest reason this is a registry-tooling episode and not just a stats lecture — the estimator tells you which data-quality diagnostics actually matter.
