Plain-English glossary

Active adverse-event verification / centralized clinical verification — A quality-control process where reported adverse events are checked against source records by a central team rather than accepted as submitted.（中文：由中央团队核实不良事件报告，而非直接采信上报内容。）

GVP Module VIII — The EU's Good Pharmacovigilance Practices guideline governing how post-authorisation safety studies are designed, registered, and reported.（中文：欧盟药物警戒质量管理规范第八章,规范上市后安全性研究的设计、登记与报告。）

HMA-EMA Catalogue of Real-World Data Studies — The public registry of post-authorisation study protocols and results, formerly called the EU PAS Register.（中文：上市后研究方案与结果的公开登记平台,前身为EU PAS注册库。）

Imposed PASS — A post-authorisation safety study a regulator requires as a legal condition of marketing authorisation or as a specific obligation; contrasted with a non-imposed (voluntary) PASS included in a risk management plan without being a binding condition.（中文：监管机构强制要求的上市后安全性研究,与非强制、自愿纳入风险管理计划的研究相对。）

Marketing Authorisation Holder (MAH) — The company holding the legal approval to market a drug, and therefore holding the regulatory obligations attached to that approval.（中文：持有药品上市许可的公司,承担相应的监管义务。）

PMR / PMC — FDA's postmarketing requirement (legally enforceable) and postmarketing commitment (agreed but not enforced the same way) — the US analogue to the EU's imposed/non-imposed PASS distinction.（中文：美国FDA的上市后要求（具强制力）与上市后承诺（非强制）,对应欧盟强制/非强制PASS的区分。）

Post-authorisation safety study (PASS) — Any study conducted after drug approval to identify, characterize, or quantify a safety hazard, confirm the safety profile, or measure the effectiveness of risk-minimization measures.（中文：药品获批后开展的研究,用于识别、描述或量化安全性风险,或评估风险最小化措施的效果。）

Registry vs. registry study — A registry is an open-ended data collection system; a registry study is a defined investigation with its own protocol, analysis plan, and enrollment criteria that draws its population from the registry.（中文：登记系统是开放式的数据收集体系,登记研究则是有独立方案和分析计划的具体研究,其研究人群来自登记系统。）

Safety concern categories (EU RMP) — The three-way classification of a risk in a risk management plan: important identified risk, important potential risk, and missing information.（中文：欧盟风险管理计划中风险的三类划分:重要已识别风险、重要潜在风险、缺失信息。）

ALEX A post-authorisation safety study, a PASS, is any study conducted after a drug is approved with the primary aim of identifying, characterizing, or quantifying a safety hazard, confirming the safety profile, or measuring the effectiveness of risk-minimization measures. In the EU that's defined under GVP Module VIII, the pharmacovigilance guideline that governs how these studies are designed, registered, and reported. The critical distinction is imposed versus non-imposed. An imposed PASS is one the regulator requires as a legal condition, either as a condition of the marketing authorisation itself or as a specific obligation attached to a conditional approval. A non-imposed PASS is one the marketing authorisation holder includes in its risk management plan voluntarily, sometimes because PRAC, the EU's safety committee, requested it without making it a legal condition. That's the category 3 language you'll hear people use informally, a PASS that lives in the RMP without being a binding condition of staying on the market.

JORDAN And the American equivalent.

ALEX PMR and PMC, postmarketing requirement and postmarketing commitment. A PMR is FDA-imposed and legally enforceable, a PMC is something the sponsor agrees to do without the same enforcement teeth. Different agencies, same basic split between "you must" and "you said you would."

JORDAN And separately from whether a study is imposed, there's the question of what it's studying. What's a safety concern, in this vocabulary.

ALEX In an EU risk management plan, safety concerns get sorted into three buckets, important identified risks, important potential risks, and missing information. An important identified risk is one with enough evidence behind it that the causal link is treated as established for RMP purposes. An important potential risk is a plausible signal that hasn't cleared that bar yet, something biologically credible or seen in a related product class. Missing information is exactly what it sounds like, a population or an exposure duration or a subgroup the pre-approval data simply didn't cover, long-term use in pregnancy is a classic example. A PASS gets attached to a specific safety concern in one of those three buckets, which is worth knowing because it means the study's design question is usually not "is this drug safe," it's "what do we now know about this one named concern that we didn't know at approval."

JORDAN What's the "why," concretely.

ALEX Three buckets, and they map almost exactly onto what the CorEvitas poster is trying to solve. First, the nature of the data and quality assurance, meaning whether registries collect a common core data set with shared definitions, common coding, and whether they can show consistency, accuracy, and completeness on demand. Second, governance, informed consent, and data sharing, meaning whether a registry has a functioning process for a regulator or a marketing authorisation holder to actually request and receive data within GDPR and national law. Third, stakeholder communication, meaning whether the registry, the sponsor, and the regulator talk to each other early enough in the drug's lifecycle that the registry's data model was ever built to answer the question that ends up being asked. The paper also draws a distinction I think is underrated, between a registry and a registry study. A registry is an open-ended data collection system. A registry study is a defined investigation with its own protocol, its own analysis plan, its own enrollment criteria, that happens to draw its source population from the registry. Conflating the two is exactly the kind of category error that makes registry data look flakier to a regulator than it needs to.
