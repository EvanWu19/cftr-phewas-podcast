Track A — CF/CFTR variant interpretation & harmonization

Blurb

PrimateAI reframed missense pathogenicity prediction around a free, bias-free label source: common variants from six non-human primate species. Because humans and chimpanzees share 99.4% amino-acid identity and a variant common in a primate population has already survived purifying selection, identical-by-state human variants are very likely benign — no human curator required. Sundaram et al. trained a 36-layer deep residual network (~400k parameters) on 385k benign (common-human + primate) variants framed as benign-versus-unlabeled, with sequence-predicted secondary structure and solvent accessibility baked in as sub-networks. It beat 20 prior classifiers on a withheld primate test set (91% vs 80%) and on developmental-disorder de novo case/control discrimination (~88% within-gene accuracy), and crucially showed that ClinVar-trained tools carry curation biases the selection-derived datasets don't. Where it lands: the Karen / variant interpretation priority — not the tool itself, but its label-provenance philosophy applied to CFTR's heavily curated (and therefore circularity-prone) variant set. Buildable artifact: a CFTR variant-label provenance auditor.

Plain-English glossary

  • Allele frequency — how often a given variant appears in a population; high frequency is classic evidence of benign consequence (caveat: weaker for recessive disease).
  • De novo mutation — a new variant present in a child but neither parent; central to developmental-disorder genetics.
  • Identical-by-state — the exact same amino-acid change occurring independently in two species (e.g., human and chimp).
  • Missense variant — a single-base change that swaps one amino acid for another; protein is full-length but altered.
  • Missense:synonymous ratio — a population-genetics readout of selection; flat across the frequency spectrum means selection isn't purging the variants (benign).
  • Position frequency matrix (PFM) — per-position summary of which amino acids appear across aligned species; how the model encodes conservation.
  • Purifying (negative) selection — removal of fitness-reducing variants from a population, keeping them rare.
  • Secondary structure — local protein fold class: alpha helix, beta sheet, or coil.
  • Semi-supervised / benign-vs-unlabeled — training with only a positive (benign) label set against an unlabeled pool, no explicit pathogenic labels.
  • Solvent accessibility — whether a residue is buried inside the protein or exposed on its surface.
  • Variant of uncertain significance (VUS) — a variant seen in a patient whose disease relevance is unknown.

Methods / tools to lift

  • Benign-vs-unlabeled framing — use a vetted-benign positive set against a generated unlabeled missense pool; sidesteps pathogenic-label scarcity and circularity.
  • Cross-species labels — common primate variants as a selection-derived benign truth set (6 species, ~35 My divergence covered).
  • Sequence-predicted structure sub-networks — predict secondary structure / solvent accessibility from sequence and embed them, rather than relying on sparse crystal structures (the piece AlphaMissense/PrimateAI-3D later replaced with AlphaFold folds).
  • Matched-threshold benchmarking — set each classifier's threshold via trinucleotide-matched unlabeled variants for fair head-to-head comparison.
  • Within-gene evaluation — test benign-vs-pathogenic discrimination inside the same gene set to avoid rewarding gene-level priors.

ALEX Welcome back. Today we're reading "Predicting the clinical impact of human mutation with deep neural networks," Laksshman Sundaram and colleagues, Nature Genetics, 2018 — the paper that introduced PrimateAI. This is Track A, variant interpretation, and it's a foundational one. The whole pitch is almost absurdly clean: you can label benign human variants for free, without a single curator, by borrowing the immune system of evolution from monkeys. We're reading it because the idea underneath it — using cross-species population data as a label source — is something you could lift straight onto CFTR.

JORDAN Okay, "borrowing labels from monkeys" needs unpacking. Before we get into the network, give me the genetics vocabulary. What's a missense variant, and what does "variant of uncertain significance" actually mean here?

ALEX Good place to start. A missense variant is a single DNA change that swaps one amino acid for another in the protein — the gene still makes a full-length product, but one building block is different. Some swaps are harmless, some break the protein, and the whole game is telling those apart. A variant of uncertain significance — VUS — is one where we genuinely don't know. We've seen it in a patient, but we can't say if it causes disease or is just noise. The reason VUS are everywhere is brutal arithmetic: there are over 70 million possible missense changes in the human genome, and only about one in a thousand is common enough in the population for us to have confidently called it benign. Everything else is dark.

JORDAN And the trick is that the rare, scary ones get removed by natural selection?

ALEX Exactly — that's "purifying selection." If a variant hurts your fitness, it tends not to spread; you don't pass it to many descendants, so it stays rare. Flip that around: if a variant is common in a healthy population, selection has already vetted it. It's probably benign. That's the logic diagnostic labs already use when they filter out anything above roughly 0.1 percent allele frequency. Allele frequency is just how often a given variant shows up in a population. The problem is humans went through population bottlenecks — we're descended from a small ancestral pool — so we simply don't have enough common variation to cover the genome. Most positions have never been "tested" by a common human variant.

JORDAN So they go find a bigger pool of common variants. In chimps.

ALEX Right, and this is the conceptual heart of the paper. Humans and chimpanzees share 99.4 percent amino acid identity. A variant that's common in chimpanzees has already survived purifying selection in chimps — and because the proteins are nearly identical, the same change is very likely benign in humans too. The technical phrase they use is "identical-by-state": the exact same amino acid change occurring independently in both species. They show that human variants which are identical-by-state with common chimp variants have a flat missense-to-synonymous ratio across the whole human frequency spectrum — which is a population-genetics way of saying selection isn't acting against them. They're benign. Then they extend it to six non-human primates — chimp, bonobo, gorilla, orangutan, rhesus, marmoset — going back about 35 million years of divergence to New World monkeys, and the signal holds.

JORDAN Why does that matter so much? Couldn't you just use a known pathogenicity database to train a model?

ALEX That's the background problem, and it's the part I want you to really sit with, because it transfers directly to CF work. Everyone before this trained supervised classifiers on human-curated databases — ClinVar and the like. But those databases have two diseases of their own. First, coverage: about half of ClinVar variants come from just 200 genes, roughly one percent of the genome. So your "truth set" is wildly unrepresentative. Second, circularity. Many curated calls were themselves influenced by older prediction tools, and the new tools then trained on those calls, which trained the next tools. The field had been quietly eating its own tail. Sundaram's argument is that primate variation is an objective, bias-free label source — selection did the labeling, not a committee.

JORDAN That's a clean critique. So what did they actually build?

ALEX A deep residual network they call PrimateAI. And here's the design choice I think is elegant and also worth pushing on. For each variant, the input is the 51-amino-acid window centered on the variant — the reference sequence and the mutated sequence side by side — plus position frequency matrices from multiple sequence alignments across 99 vertebrates. A position frequency matrix is just a column-by-column summary of which amino acids show up at each spot across related species; it's how the network "sees" conservation. Then, instead of feeding in a crystal structure, they trained two separate sub-networks to predict secondary structure — alpha helix, beta sheet, coil — and solvent accessibility — whether a residue is buried or exposed on the surface — from sequence alone, and bolted those in as sub-networks. Total depth, 36 convolutional layers, about 400,000 parameters. Tiny by today's standards.

JORDAN Why predict structure instead of just using the real structure?

ALEX That's their stated reason, and it's defensible: only about 4,000 human proteins had good crystal structures at the time, so using real structure would bias the model toward well-studied proteins and leave gaps everywhere else. Predicting it from sequence gives uniform coverage. And they checked — predictions versus DSSP-annotated real structure made no substantial difference. But here's my pushback. They're spending model capacity to reconstruct structure as an intermediate, when the structure signal is itself imperfect — their secondary-structure model tops out around 80 percent accuracy. You could argue the network is learning a slightly blurry proxy. This is exactly the gap that PrimateAI-3D and AlphaMissense later closed by using real predicted folds from AlphaFold. So this paper is the "before" picture in that arc — which is why it's a useful one to read right before we read those.

JORDAN And the training setup — they don't have pathogenic labels at all, right? Just benign ones?

ALEX Correct, and this is the part data scientists will appreciate. It's semi-supervised, framed as benign-versus-unlabeled. The positive class is 385,000 labeled benign variants — common human plus primate. The other class is a pile of 68 million possible missense variants they generated computationally, matched for trinucleotide context, coverage, and alignability, with anything seen in ExAC removed. The network learns to separate "vetted benign" from "could be anything," and pathogenicity falls out the other side. They train eight models on eight random unlabeled samples and average them. No pathogenic truth set touches training — which is the whole point, because that's what keeps it bias-free.

JORDAN So does it work? Give me numbers.

ALEX Three evaluations, and they're careful about each. First, a withheld set of 10,000 primate variants the network never saw. PrimateAI calls 91 percent of them benign at a matched threshold, versus 80 percent for the next best classifier. And they decompose the gain: about half comes from the deep network itself, half from adding primate data to the training set. That second half is the real message — the data source matters as much as the architecture. Second, a clinical test: de novo mutations in 4,293 children with developmental disorders from the DDD cohort, versus de novo mutations in 2,517 healthy siblings. "De novo" means brand-new mutations not inherited from either parent. PrimateAI separates cases from controls far better than any prior tool, Wilcoxon p below ten-to-the-minus-28.

JORDAN De novo in cases versus controls feels like it could cheat — couldn't the model just learn which genes are disease genes?

ALEX Sharp, and the authors worried about the same thing. So their third test restricts to 605 genes already implicated by protein-truncating variation, and asks whether the model can tell benign from pathogenic within the same gene. It can — at a cutoff of 0.803, it flags 65 percent of de novo missense in cases as pathogenic versus 14 percent in controls, about 88 percent accuracy. That's the headline number. And applying that threshold sharpened the statistical signal enough to surface 14 new candidate intellectual-disability genes that hadn't reached genome-wide significance before. One more telling result: classifier performance on the primate test set and on the DDD set were correlated, but neither correlated with performance on recent ClinVar variants — which they read as evidence that ClinVar carries human curation biases the other two datasets don't.

JORDAN So where does this land for our actual work?

ALEX This one lands squarely on the Karen / variant interpretation priority. The transferable idea is not "go run PrimateAI on CFTR" — modern tools dominate it now. The transferable idea is the labeling philosophy: a benign-versus-unlabeled, selection-derived truth set, and a hard-nosed awareness that your labels may be circular. CFTR is one of the most heavily curated genes on Earth — CFTR2 has expert calls on hundreds of variants — and that richness is exactly where circularity hides. So here's the buildable artifact. A CFTR variant-label provenance auditor: a notebook that takes a list of CFTR missense variants and, for each, pulls the CFTR2 clinical determination, the ClinVar call, gnomAD popmax allele frequency, and the scores from PrimateAI, AlphaMissense, REVEL, and CADD, then flags two things — variants where a "pathogenic" curated call sits at a suspiciously common allele frequency, and variants where every in-silico tool agrees but the tools may share training lineage. Input: a CFTR variant list with HGVS notation. Output: a provenance table with circularity flags. Difficulty: medium — it's mostly API plumbing and join logic, no model training.

JORDAN What would we need to check before trusting that?

ALEX A few things, all phrased as "what we'd need to verify." First, allele-frequency interpretation in CFTR is special: it's a recessive disease, so a pathogenic variant can sit at meaningful carrier frequency in the population without contradiction — the 0.1 percent benign heuristic that works for dominant disease does not transfer cleanly, and we'd need a CF-appropriate frequency threshold before flagging anything. Second, we'd need to confirm which tool scores in dbNSFP are independent versus derived from each other, because the whole circularity flag depends on knowing the training lineage — and that lineage isn't always documented. Third, PrimateAI's own authors explicitly ask that their scores not be fed into other classifiers, so if we're auditing for circularity we should respect that and treat PrimateAI as a reference axis, not an ensemble input. And we'd want a CF geneticist — this is literally Karen's domain — to sanity-check a handful of the flagged variants before we trust the flag logic at all. Nothing here decides a study design; it's a screening tool to start a conversation.

One-line takeaway for today: PrimateAI's lasting lesson isn't the network, it's that letting evolution label your benign set sidesteps the curation biases that quietly poison everything trained on ClinVar.
