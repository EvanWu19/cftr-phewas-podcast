Blurb (~150w)

Hawkes and colleagues (Nature Communications, 2026) used whole-genome sequencing (WGS) — reading all ~3 billion DNA letters, coding and non-coding — to map rare and low-frequency variant contributions to height, BMI, and waist-hip ratio across 447,461 UK Biobank (discovery) and 225,515 All of Us (replication) participants, 672,976 total. They found 90 replicated rare/low-frequency single-variant associations, 135 rare coding aggregate associations (e.g., UBR3 loss-of-function → +2.7 kg/m² BMI), and 51 non-coding aggregate associations — including a 5′UTR signal in FGF18 (up to 6 cm height) with no coding signal, the clearest argument for WGS over exome data. But 97% of rare associations sat near known GWAS loci, and rare variants explained only ~11% of height heritability (≈0 for BMI/WHR). Proposed CF direction: include non-coding/regulatory rare-variant aggregates (not just coding) when testing CF modifier loci against a quantitative phenotype. Open question: whether ~5,000 CF patients yield enough rare-variant carriers to power any aggregate test.

Plain-English glossary

  • Aggregate / burden test: bundles all rare variants in a gene/region and tests them collectively (more powerful than one-at-a-time for rare variants).
  • Anthropometric traits: body measurements — here height, BMI, waist-hip ratio adjusted for BMI.
  • All of Us (AoU): large, deliberately diverse US biobank; used here for replication.
  • Annotation (VEP, LOFTEE, CADD, GERP, SpliceAI): software predicting each variant's consequence/damage/conservation, used to decide which variants go in a bundle ("mask").
  • BMI: body mass index — weight scaled for height.
  • Common vs rare variant: common = MAF >1%; rare = MAF <0.1% here.
  • Convergence: rare-variant signals landing near already-known common-variant loci.
  • Exome sequencing: reads only the ~2% protein-coding genome.
  • GWAS: genome-wide association study, chip-based, mostly common variants.
  • Heritability: fraction of trait variation attributable to genetics.
  • Loss-of-function (pLOF): a variant that breaks a gene's protein.
  • MAF / MAC: minor allele frequency / minor allele count.
  • Mask: the chosen set of variants bundled into an aggregate test.
  • Missing heritability: the gap between twin-study heritability and what discovered variants explain.
  • REGENIE: scalable association-testing program.
  • RHE-mc: method used to partition heritability across variant-frequency bins.
  • Single-variant test: tests one variant at a time against the trait.
  • 5′UTR: regulatory region of a gene's mRNA upstream of the protein-coding part.
  • WGS: whole-genome sequencing.

Methods to lift

  • WGS single-variant + aggregate two-track design — REGENIE single-variant tests (MAC ≥ 5) plus rare-variant aggregate (burden/SKAT/ACAT) tests for coding and non-coding regions.
  • Non-coding aggregate masks — 5′UTR, upstream, regulatory-region and conserved-window aggregates (GERP > 2, phastCons 99th percentile, JARVIS, SpliceAI) — the part that found FGF18.
  • Stringent significance thresholds — derived from null simulated traits: P < 2.95×10⁻¹⁰ (single), P < 8.71×10⁻⁹ (aggregate).
  • GIANT-locus adjustment — re-test every signal after conditioning on previously reported common-variant loci, so reported effects are independent of known signal.
  • RHE-mc partitioned heritability — split heritability into MAF bins to ask how much rare variants explain (answer: little).
  • Replication in a diverse cohort (AoU) with cross-ancestry consistency checks (45/52 AFR height variants within 35 kb of known loci).

Proposed direction (conditional)

Conditional on inspection of CFF WGS variant calls and registry phenotype linkage: in the CFF WGS cohort, test rare-variant aggregate associations between candidate CFTR-modifier loci and a continuous CF phenotype (e.g., sweat chloride or ppFEV1), building non-coding regulatory masks (5′UTR, upstream, conserved) alongside coding masks, using the VEP/LOFTEE/CADD annotation-and-burden pipeline this paper lays out.

What to verify first

  • How many CF patients actually have analyzable WGS (Hawkes used 447k discovery / 225k replication; cohort is ~5,000).
  • Whether the target phenotype is continuous and cleanly measured registry-wide.
  • Expected number of rare-variant carriers per candidate gene — does it clear a minimum bar for any aggregate test?
  • Whether the cohort is realistically powered only for common-variant / known-modifier work rather than genome-wide rare-variant discovery (Hawkes is the calibration reference for that ceiling).

JORDAN Start with the title. What are "anthropometric traits"?

ALEX Anthropometric just means body-measurement. The three traits here are height, BMI — body mass index, weight scaled for height — and waist-to-hip ratio adjusted for BMI, which is a measure of where you carry fat rather than how much. They're picked not because anyone urgently needs more height genetics, but because they're extremely well-measured in huge numbers of people, which makes them the perfect test track for a new method.

JORDAN And the central contrast in the title — "rare and common" variants. Define those.

ALEX A common variant is a spelling difference in the DNA that lots of people carry — by convention a MAF, minor allele frequency, above one percent, meaning it appears on more than one percent of chromosomes. A rare variant is one carried by very few people — here they go down below 0.1 percent, a tenth of a percent. Now, why does the split matter? Two different technologies historically chased these two pools. GWAS — genome-wide association study — uses cheap genotyping chips that read mostly common variants. Exome sequencing reads the protein-coding two percent of the genome and catches rare coding variants. This paper uses the third, most expensive option: WGS, whole-genome sequencing, which reads all three billion letters, coding and non-coding alike. The promise of WGS is that it can see rare variants in the non-coding 98 percent that the other two methods miss entirely.

JORDAN So what was the open problem that justified spending all that sequencing money?

ALEX It's the puzzle called "missing heritability." Take height: we know from twin studies it's about 80 percent heritable, yet even a GWAS of 5.4 million people, finding over 12,000 common-variant signals, only accounts for around 40 percent of the variance. So half the inherited signal is unaccounted for. One hypothesis is that it hides in rare non-coding variants — the part of the genome no cheap method reads well. Hawkes and colleagues set out to test that directly: with true whole-genome data at scale, how much rare-variant signal is really out there, where does it sit, and does it tell us about new genes or just re-confirm places we already knew?

JORDAN Walk me through how they did it.

ALEX The cohort design is itself a lesson. They used 447,461 UK Biobank participants for discovery — finding signals — and 225,515 people from the All of Us program, AoU, the large and deliberately diverse US biobank, for replication, meaning re-testing the signals in independent people to make sure they're real. That's 672,976 total. Then two flavors of test. A single-variant test asks, one variant at a time, does this spelling correlate with height — they used REGENIE for that, the same scalable workhorse from yesterday's episode. But single-variant tests are weak for rare variants, because if only forty people carry a variant you can't say much. So they also did aggregate tests, also called burden tests: instead of testing one rare variant, you bundle all the rare variants in a gene or region together and ask whether carrying any of them, collectively, shifts the trait. To decide which variants to bundle, they leaned on a stack of annotation tools — VEP to predict each variant's consequence, LOFTEE to flag likely "loss-of-function" variants that break a gene, CADD and GERP and SpliceAI to score how damaging or conserved a spot is. One design choice I'll push on: the aggregate test result depends heavily on which variants you choose to put in the bundle — the "mask" — and there are many defensible masks. That's researcher freedom that can quietly inflate findings if you're not disciplined, and it's worth noting they set very stringent significance thresholds to compensate.

JORDAN How stringent?

ALEX For single variants, a p-value below about 3 times ten to the minus ten; for aggregates, below about 9 times ten to the minus nine — both far harsher than the usual genome-wide line, precisely because they tested so much of the genome. Good discipline.

JORDAN So — did the whole-genome bet pay off? What did they find?

ALEX A qualified, interesting yes. They found 90 rare and low-frequency single-variant associations that replicated. Some are biologically vivid: a rare insertion near a microRNA gene added about 3.8 centimeters to height per copy; two independent rare variants upstream of a gene called IGF2BP2 substantially lowered waist-hip ratio. On the aggregate side, 135 rare coding-variant gene associations — for example, gene-breaking variants in UBR3 associated with a 2.7-unit jump in BMI — and crucially, 51 non-coding aggregate associations, including one in the 5-prime untranslated region — the regulatory stretch just before a gene starts — of FGF18, tied to up to 6 centimeters of height. That FGF18 signal is the money shot for the WGS argument, because FGF18 had no coding signal at all; only by reading the non-coding genome could you see it.

JORDAN You mentioned All of Us is deliberately diverse. Did the signals hold up across ancestries, or only in the European group?

ALEX They checked, and this is reassuring for the general method. They re-tested height associations in roughly 55,000 African-ancestry and 42,000 admixed-American-ancestry All of Us participants. The pattern held: in the African-ancestry analysis, 45 of 52 height-associated variants — about 87 percent — sat within 35 kilobases of a previously reported common-variant signal, and a similar picture in the admixed-American group. So the convergence between rare and common signals isn't a quirk of European data; it shows up across ancestries. That said — and this connects straight to yesterday's Wen episode — replicating in a few tens of thousands of non-European people is still far more than a 5,000-person CF cohort sliced by ancestry could ever muster. The reassurance is about biology, not about whether you personally can run it.

JORDAN That sounds like a strong case for whole-genome sequencing. Where's the gut-punch?

ALEX Two findings temper it. First, the convergence in the title: 97 percent of the rare-variant associations sat near a locus a common-variant GWAS had already flagged. So rare variants mostly pointed back to neighborhoods we already knew, rather than opening entirely new territory. And second — the heritability accounting. When they partitioned height's heritability, the rare and low-frequency variants together explained only about 11 percent of it, and for BMI and waist-hip ratio essentially none. The missing heritability is, by this measure, mostly not hiding in rare variants after all. It concentrates around common signals.

JORDAN Quick definitional check — you said "5-prime untranslated region" and "microRNA." What are those, in one breath each?

ALEX The 5-prime UTR is the stretch of a gene that gets copied into the messenger RNA but sits in front of the part that codes for protein — it's regulatory real estate that tunes how much protein gets made, without being protein itself. A microRNA is a tiny RNA molecule that doesn't make a protein at all; its job is to dial down other genes. Both live in the non-coding genome, and both are exactly the kind of place that cheap chips and exome sequencing skip — which is why Hawkes's whole-genome data could light them up.
