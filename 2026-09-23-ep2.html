Plain-English glossary

Cubic regression spline — A flexible piecewise-cubic function, joined smoothly at chosen points (knots), used here to let the weight function w(u) take whatever shape the data support rather than a shape fixed in advance.（中文：一种灵活的分段三次函数,在选定的节点处平滑连接,此处用于让权重函数 w(u) 呈现数据支持的形状,而非预先固定的形状。）

Cumulative dose (as exposure metric) — Total exposure summed over the observation period, implicitly a flat (equal) weight applied to a dose regardless of when it was taken.（中文：观察期内暴露量的累加总和,隐含地对任何时间点的剂量赋予相同的权重。）

Current-use metric — An exposure definition in which only exposure at the present moment counts toward risk; in weight-function terms, a spike at u=0 and zero elsewhere.（中文：一种暴露定义,只有当前时刻的暴露才计入风险;用权重函数表示,即在 u=0 处为单点峰值,其余为零。）

Fixed lag — An exposure definition that assigns zero weight to exposure before a chosen lag time and a constant weight after it; a step-function special case of w(u).（中文：一种暴露定义,在选定的滞后时间之前赋予零权重,之后赋予恒定权重;是权重函数 w(u) 的阶跃函数特例。）

Knot (spline) — A point along the exposure-time axis where the spline's piecewise segments join; the number and placement are chosen using information criteria.（中文：样条函数分段连接的位置点,其数量与位置通常通过信息准则选定。）

Weight function, w(u) — The function describing how much exposure taken u time units in the past contributes to the hazard at the current time; in WCE this is estimated from the data rather than assumed.（中文：描述过去 u 个时间单位前的暴露对当前时刻风险贡献程度的函数;在加权累积暴露法中,该函数由数据估计得出,而非事先假定。）

Weighted cumulative exposure (WCE) — A method for modeling the cumulative effect of a time-varying exposure on a hazard, where past exposure is weighted by an estimated function of time-since-exposure and summed into a single time-dependent covariate inside a Cox model.（中文：一种建模时变暴露对风险累积影响的方法,过去的暴露按照一个估计出的、随暴露后经过时间而变化的函数加权,汇总为 Cox 模型中的单一时变协变量。）

ALEX A standard time-varying Cox model lets exposure status change over time, but it still forces you to pick, in advance, how that history translates into current risk. Current-use models say only today's exposure matters. Ever-use models say any exposure ever, no matter how long ago, contributes equally. Cumulative dose models add up total exposure and treat a milligram from ten years ago the same as a milligram from last month. A fixed lag model says exposure doesn't count until some chosen number of months have passed, then counts fully. Every one of those is a modeling decision made before you've looked at the data, about how much a given week of past exposure should matter today.

JORDAN And weighted cumulative exposure does what instead.

ALEX It says the hazard at time t depends on a weighted sum of the entire past exposure history, where the weight given to exposure taken u time units ago is described by a function, w of u. Instead of you choosing how quickly relevance decays with time since exposure, you estimate that decay from the data.

JORDAN Walk me through what that actually looks like mechanically.

ALEX You build a time-dependent Cox model as usual, hazard at time t as a function of covariates, but the time-varying exposure term isn't a single number, it's a weighted integral, or in practice a weighted sum, over the exposure history up to t. Each past exposure gets multiplied by w of u, where u is how long ago that exposure occurred, and those weighted contributions are summed into one score that enters the model like any other covariate. The trick is that w of u itself is not chosen, it's represented using cubic regression splines and estimated as part of the model fit, with the number and placement of knots chosen using information criteria, and the weight function is typically constrained to go to zero at the far end of the exposure window, so exposure infinitely far in the past eventually stops contributing.

JORDAN You said standard approaches are all special cases of this. Make that concrete.

ALEX Current-use is a weight function that's a spike at u equals zero and zero everywhere else, all the weight concentrated on today, nothing on history. Ever-use is a flat weight function, constant over the whole window, treating any exposure in the window as equally relevant regardless of recency, which is also what a simple cumulative-dose sum implicitly assumes, a flat weight applied to however much drug was taken. A fixed lag is a step function, zero weight from zero up to the lag point, then a constant weight afterward. Every one of those is a specific, rigid shape for w of u that you're imposing by assumption. WCE doesn't assume the shape, it estimates it, and the standard exposure metrics fall out as what you'd get if you'd forced the spline flat, or forced it to a spike, or forced it to a step.

JORDAN What's the actual payoff of not assuming the shape.

ALEX You get to read the latency structure off the data instead of asserting it. If you assume a fixed lag of, say, sixty months, you've baked in your answer to "when does exposure start to matter" before you've analyzed anything. If you estimate w of u with splines instead, the shape of the fitted curve tells you where the influence of past exposure actually rises and falls, which is a testable, visualizable object rather than a design choice you defended in a protocol. That's precisely the gap we flagged in episode two, that essentially nothing in this literature models time-varying cancer risk explicitly. WCE is the direct methodological answer to that complaint.

JORDAN And practically, once you've fit that spline, what do you do with it.

ALEX You can plot the estimated w of u against u and read off where it rises, where it peaks, where it goes flat or back to zero, and you can integrate the weighted exposure score into the same hazard ratio interpretation you're already comfortable with, since it still enters the Cox model as a single time-varying covariate. The spline is doing the work of discovering the shape, but once fit, the rest of the model behaves like any other time-dependent Cox model you'd already know how to read.
